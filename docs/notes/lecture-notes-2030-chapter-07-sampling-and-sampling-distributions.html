Chapters 5 and 6 described data generating mechanisms: if you know the mechanism, what values will it produce? This chapter asks the question a manager actually faces, which runs the other way. She has one sample, and from it one number: an average order size, a share of satisfied customers. How far might that number be from the truth she cannot see?

The answer is another probability distribution. Draw a different sample and you get a different average. The distribution of all the averages you could have gotten is the sampling distribution, and its standard deviation, the standard error, is the single most important number in the second half of this course. Every confidence interval in Chapter 8 and every hypothesis test after it is built from one.

As before, you will not compute these by hand on an exam. You will be handed output and asked what it means. In this chapter the most important thing it can mean is “this sample is (or is not) the kind of sample that claim would produce.”

Why Sample?

A population is all possible subjects of interest in a study. A sample is a subset of the population. A census records every member of the population.

Nielsen estimates the viewing habits of roughly 129 million US television households from a panel of about 41,000 of them. A cracker maker checks salt content by testing crackers, and a tested cracker cannot be sold. A university that wants students’ views on academic integrity could survey all of them, but would spend far more to learn very little more.

Those are the three reasons to sample: a census would be impossible (the population is too large or keeps changing), destructive (measuring uses up the item), or wasteful (a well-drawn sample answers the question almost as well, for a fraction of the cost). The price of sampling is that the answer is uncertain. The point of this chapter is that the uncertainty can be measured.

Types of Sampling

A probability sample is one in which every member of the population has a known, nonzero chance of being selected. The chances need not be equal.

A nonprobability sample is one in which the chance of selection is not known.

Only a probability sample supports the calculations in the rest of this chapter. If you do not know how members got into the sample, you cannot say how far the sample is likely to be from the population.

Throughout this section the population is real: the 215 economics faculty members at nine Big Ten universities in 1999, with their salaries (Baser and Pema, 2003). Because we have the whole population, we know the true mean salary, $93,457, and can see how well each design recovers it.

Simple random sampling

In a simple random sample every member of the population has the same chance of being chosen, and every possible sample of size \(n\) is equally likely. Members are drawn without replacement: nobody is chosen twice.

Systematic sampling

In systematic sampling, every \(k\)th member of a list is chosen, starting from a random position among the first \(k\). The systematic sampling constant is

\[k=\frac{N}{n},\]

where \(N\) is the population size and \(n\) the sample size.

Systematic sampling is easy to carry out by hand: every fifth customer through the door, every hundredth file in the cabinet. It behaves like a simple random sample as long as the list has no pattern that lines up with \(k\).

Periodicity is a pattern in the list that repeats with the same spacing as \(k\). It can make a systematic sample unrepresentative.

Here is periodicity on purpose. A Summerlin restaurant’s daily covers over a year have a weekly cycle, quiet on weekdays and busy on weekends. (The numbers are an illustration, not data.) Sample every 7th day and you sample the same weekday every time:

Stratified sampling

In a stratified sample, the population is divided into mutually exclusive groups, or strata, and a random sample is taken from each. The strata are chosen because the variable of interest differs across them.

Faculty salaries differ sharply by rank, so rank is a natural stratum. There are two ways to set the stratum sample sizes.

Proportional allocation makes the sample mirror the population. For a sample of 30:

Show the code 
alloc = proportional_allocation(RANK_COUNTS, 30)

With proportional allocation the ordinary sample mean is already the right estimate, because each stratum appears in its population share.

Disproportionate allocation samples some strata more heavily, usually to learn enough about a small group. With only 20 assistant professors, proportional allocation gives us 3 of them, too few to say anything about assistant professors. Sampling 10 from each rank fixes that, but now the sample over-represents the lower-paid ranks, and its plain average is biased.

The stratified estimate of the population mean weights each stratum mean by that stratum’s share of the population, \(W_h=N_h/N\):

\[\bar{x}_{\text{strat}}=\sum_h W_h\,\bar{x}_h .\]

Equivalently, each sampled observation from stratum \(h\) gets weight \(W_h/n_h\).

EQUAL = {"Assistant": 10, "Associate": 10, "Professor": 10}

Read the bias and standard error columns together. Bias is how far the design’s estimates sit from the truth on average; the standard error is how much they scatter from sample to sample. RMSE combines the two.

  • Proportional stratification beats a simple random sample of the same size: a standard error of $3,754 against $4,925. Making sure every rank is represented in its proper share removes the luck of drawing too many or too few full professors. That is what stratification is for.
  • The 10/10/10 sample’s plain average is too low by $16,248 on average. It is not a bad sample; it is a bad estimate, because it ignores how the sample was drawn. Weighting by \(W_h\) removes the bias.
  • Weighting each observation by \(W_h\), instead of each stratum mean, is a tempting mistake. With unequal stratum sample sizes it leaves a bias of $4,175, because it counts the heavily sampled strata twice: once through \(n_h\) and again through \(W_h\).

Cluster sampling

In cluster sampling, the population is divided into mutually exclusive groups, or clusters, each of which is ideally a small version of the whole population. A random sample of clusters is chosen, and everyone (or a random sample) within the chosen clusters is measured. Clusters are often geographic.

The nine universities are natural clusters. A researcher who must travel to collect data could visit 3 of the 9 campuses, chosen at random, and record every economist there. Note what that is not: visiting all nine campuses and sampling within each is stratified sampling, with campuses as strata.

Nonprobability sampling

A convenience sample is one whose members are chosen because they are easy to reach.

Customers who happen to walk in, your own students, an online poll, people who choose to write a review: all are convenience samples. They are fine for a quick reaction to a new product. They do not support the probability calculations in the rest of this chapter, because nobody knows how members got in.

A bigger convenience sample is not a better one. In 1936 the Literary Digest mailed ballots to names drawn from telephone directories and automobile registrations, people who in the Depression were disproportionately well off and Republican. About 2.4 million responded, and the Digest predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a landslide. Gallup, using a far smaller sample chosen to represent the electorate, called the winner.

The error had nothing to do with sample size and everything to do with who could be sampled. Twelve years later, in 1948, the major polls predicted Thomas Dewey would beat Harry Truman, in part because they used quota samples (interviewers filled demographic quotas with whomever they chose) and stopped polling weeks before an electorate that was still moving.

Sampling and Nonsampling Errors

A parameter is a number that describes a population, such as its mean \(\mu\). A statistic is a number calculated from a sample, such as the sample mean \(\bar{x}\). Statistics are used to estimate parameters.

The sampling error of the sample mean is the difference between the statistic and the parameter:

\[\text{sampling error}=\bar{x}-\mu .\]

A small population makes sampling error something you can see completely. A Henderson marketing office has ten employees, with these ages:

Show the code 
AGES = [24, 33, 28, 41, 38, 29, 26, 33, 37, 31]
mu_age = float(np.mean(AGES))
print("Ages:", AGES, f"   mu = {mu_age:.1f}")
Ages: [24, 33, 28, 41, 38, 29, 26, 33, 37, 31]    mu = 32.0

Take the first two employees as a sample: \(\bar{x}=(24+33)/2=28.5\), a sampling error of \(28.5-32.0=-3.5\) years. The last two give \((37+31)/2=34.0\), an error of \(+2.0\). But there is no need to pick samples one at a time. With ten people there are only 45 possible samples of two and 252 samples of five, and we can look at all of them:

Three lessons, each visible in the table:

  1. Sampling errors vary from sample to sample, and can be positive or negative. Which one you get is luck.
  2. They average out. The mean of all possible sample means is exactly \(\mu=\) 32.0, for both sample sizes.
  3. Larger samples make small errors more likely, not certain. 42% of samples of two land within two years of \(\mu\); 75% of samples of five do. But the unluckiest sample of five, {33, 41, 38, 33, 37}, is still off by +4.4 years.

In practice \(\mu\) is unknown, so we never know the sampling error of the sample we actually drew. What we can know, as the rest of this chapter shows, is how large sampling errors tend to be.

Nonsampling errors

Nonsampling errors arise from how data are collected rather than from which members happened to be sampled: ambiguous or leading questions, a sampling frame that leaves people out, people who choose whether to respond, recording mistakes, faulty instruments.

Sampling error shrinks as the sample grows. Nonsampling error does not. Asking a biased question of 10,000 people gives a more precise estimate of the wrong thing.

Application: what online reviews can and cannot tell you

A product has 25,596 online ratings: 88% five stars, 8% four, 2% three, 1% two, and 1% one. Treating these as a sample of customers, the average and its standard error are easy to compute:

Show the code 
N_REV = 25_596

print(f"{len(ratings):,} ratings: mean = {m_r:.3f}, s = {s_r:.3f}, "
      f"standard error s/sqrt(n) = {s_r / sqrt(len(ratings)):.4f}")
25,596 ratings: mean = 4.810, s = 0.612, standard error s/sqrt(n) = 0.0038

With a standard error of a few thousandths of a star, the sampling error is negligible. The real question is whether reviewers are a random sample of customers, and they are not: people choose whether to review, and the delighted and the furious are the most likely to bother. The sampling distribution of the mean is the probability distribution of \(\bar{x}\) over all possible samples of size \(n\) from a population.

Look at what happened. The population is flat: every price equally likely. The sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) for two diners is already peaked at $15, because an average of $15 can happen four ways and an average of $12 only one. With three diners (64 samples), it is starting to look like a bell. Averaging pulls values toward the middle. That is the Central Limit Theorem beginning to work, and we will meet it formally in a moment.

Now compute the mean and standard deviation of the sampling distribution, exactly as for any discrete distribution in Chapter 5:

The mean of the sample means equals the population mean, $15, every time. The standard deviation of the sample means shrinks, and it equals \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) exactly: 2.2361 divided by \(\sqrt{2}\) is 1.5811.

For samples of size \(n\) from a population with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\):

\[\mu_{\bar{x}}=\mu \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} .\]

\(\sigma_{\bar{x}}\) is the standard error of the mean: the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\). It measures how far a sample mean typically lands from \(\mu\).

Two consequences worth saying out loud.

The sample mean is unbiased: it is not systematically too high or too low. Any single sample can miss, but the misses average to zero.

The standard error shrinks with the square root of \(n\). Quadrupling the sample halves the standard error; to cut it by a factor of ten, you need a hundred times the data. Precision gets expensive, which is why sample size is a business decision and not only a statistical one.

Probabilities for a sample mean

When the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) is normal, probabilities for a sample mean come from the \(z\)-score of \(\bar{x}\):

\[z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{\bar{x}}}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} .\]

The denominator is the standard error, \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), not \(\sigma\). Dividing by \(\sigma\) answers a question about one person. Dividing by \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) answers a question about the average of n people. This is the most common mistake in the chapter, and it produces an answer that looks perfectly reasonable.

The Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT). For samples of size \(n\) drawn from any population with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) is approximately normal with mean \(\mu\) and standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), and the approximation improves as \(n\) grows.

  • If the population is itself normal, \(\bar{x}\) is exactly normal for any \(n\).
  • Otherwise, \(n\ge 30\) is the usual rule of thumb for “large enough.” The more skewed the population, the larger \(n\) must be.

This is the reason statistics is a required course. It says that averages behave predictably even when individuals do not, so a manager can make probability statements about an average without knowing the shape of the population it came from.

The interval that contains 95% of sample means

Taking it to the limit: a census

As the sample grows toward the whole population, the sample mean closes in on \(\mu\). Measure everyone and \(\bar{x}=\mu\): the sampling error is zero. The formula \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) does not quite know this, because it assumes an infinite population. The fix is in the next section.

Sampling from a Finite Population

So far the population has been effectively infinite: Nielsen’s 41,000 households are a sliver of 129 million. When the sample is a sizeable share of the population, the standard error formula needs an adjustment.

When sampling without replacement from a population of size \(N\), the standard error of the mean is

\[\sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}} .\]

The factor \(\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}\) is the finite population correction (FPC). Use it when the sample is more than 5% of the population, \(n/N>0.05\).

You have seen this factor before. In Chapter 5 it was the “shrink factor” that made the hypergeometric distribution less spread out than the binomial: every member you draw is one you cannot draw again, so a sample without replacement from a small population tells you more than the same-sized sample from a large one.

The Sampling Distribution of the Proportion

Many business questions are about a share: the share of households that watched, of graduates who found jobs, of orders that shipped late.

The sample proportion is

\[\hat{p}=\frac{x}{n},\]

where \(x\) is the number of successes in a sample of \(n\). For samples from a population with proportion \(p\), the sampling distribution of \(\hat{p}\) has

\[\mu_{\hat{p}}=p \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sigma_{\hat{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}},\]

where \(\sigma_{\hat{p}}\) is the standard error of the proportion. It is approximately normal when \(np\ge5\) and \(n(1-p)\ge5\), the same conditions as in Chapter 6, and

\[z=\frac{\hat{p}-p}{\sigma_{\hat{p}}} .\]

For a finite population with \(n/N>0.05\), multiply \(\sigma_{\hat{p}}\) by the finite population correction.

This is not a new idea. The number of successes \(x\) is binomial, and Chapter 6 showed it is approximately normal with mean \(np\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{np(1-p)}\). Divide everything by \(n\) and you have the formulas above. A proportion is a count on a different scale. (Your textbook writes the sample proportion as \(\bar{p}\); these notes use \(\hat{p}\), which is what Chapter 8 and most software use.)

The Bootstrap: A Standard Error from One Sample

Every calculation so far needed \(\sigma\) or \(p\), the population’s own parameters. In practice you rarely know them. The bootstrap estimates a standard error from the one sample you have.

The bootstrap treats the sample as a stand-in for the population. It draws many resamples of the same size from the sample, with replacement, computes the statistic for each, and uses the spread of those statistics to estimate the standard error.

The bootstrap is not a way of collecting data, and it is not a fifth kind of probability sample, although your textbook lists it beside the sampling designs. It is a way of analysing a sample you already have. Every resample comes from that one sample, never from the population.

# Why Sample?

::: {.blue}
A **population** is all possible subjects of interest in a study. A
**sample** is a subset of the population. A **census** records every member
of the population.
:::

Nielsen estimates the viewing habits of roughly 129 million US television
households from a panel of about 41,000 of them. A cracker maker checks salt
content by testing crackers, and a tested cracker cannot be sold. A
university that wants students' views on academic integrity could survey all
of them, but would spend far more to learn very little more.

Those are the three reasons to sample: a census would be **impossible**
(the population is too large or keeps changing), **destructive** (measuring
uses up the item), or **wasteful** (a well-drawn sample answers the question
almost as well, for a fraction of the cost). The price of sampling is that
the answer is uncertain. The point of this chapter is that the uncertainty
can be measured.

# Types of Sampling

::: {.blue}
A **probability sample** is one in which every member of the population has
a known, nonzero chance of being selected. The chances need not be equal.

A **nonprobability sample** is one in which the chance of selection is not
known.
:::

Only a probability sample supports the calculations in the rest of this
chapter. If you do not know how members got into the sample, you cannot say
how far the sample is likely to be from the population.

Throughout this section the population is real: the
`{python} str(len(faculty))` economics faculty members at nine Big Ten
universities in 1999, with their salaries (Baser and Pema, 2003). Because we
have the whole population, we know the true mean salary,
`{python} money(MU_FAC)`, and can see how well each design recovers it.

::: {.blue}

:::

## Systematic sampling

::: {.blue}
In **systematic sampling**, every $k$th member of a list is chosen, starting
from a random position among the first $k$. The **systematic sampling
constant** is

$$k=\frac{N}{n},$$

where $N$ is the population size and $n$ the sample size.
:::

Systematic sampling is easy to carry out by hand: every fifth customer
through the door, every hundredth file in the cabinet. It behaves like a
simple random sample as long as the list has no pattern that lines up with
$k$.

::: {.blue}
**Periodicity** is a pattern in the list that repeats with the same spacing
as $k$. It can make a systematic sample unrepresentative.
:::

Here is periodicity on purpose. A Summerlin restaurant's daily covers over a
year have a weekly cycle, quiet on weekdays and busy on weekends. (The
numbers are an illustration, not data.) Sample every 7th day and you sample
the *same weekday* every time:

```{python}
#| label: periodicity
rng = np.random.default_rng(20261003)
days = pd.date_range("2025-01-06", periods=364, freq="D")
weekday_level = {0: 210, 1: 200, 2: 215, 3: 240, 4: 340, 5: 380, 6: 300}
covers = pd.Series([weekday_level[d.dayofweek] + rng.normal(0, 25)
                    for d in days], index=days, name="covers")
true_mean = covers.mean()

rows = []
for k_label, n in [("k = 7 (every 7th day)", 52), ("k = 5 (every 5th day)", 72)]:
    for start in range(7 if n == 52 else 5):
        s = systematic_sample(covers, n, start=start)
        rows.append({"design": k_label,
                     "first day": s.index[0].day_name(),
                     "sample mean": s.mean()})
per = pd.DataFrame(rows)
print(f"True mean daily covers: {true_mean:.1f}\n")
tab = per.round(1).pivot_table(index="first day", columns="design",
                              values="sample mean", aggfunc="first")
order = ["Monday", "Tuesday", "Wednesday", "Thursday", "Friday", "Saturday", "Sunday"]
tab.reindex(order).fillna("(no such start)")
```

## Stratified sampling

::: {.blue}
In a **stratified sample**, the population is divided into mutually exclusive
groups, or **strata**, and a random sample is taken from each. The strata are
chosen because the variable of interest differs across them.
:::

Faculty salaries differ sharply by rank, so rank is a natural stratum. There
are two ways to set the stratum sample sizes.

**Proportional allocation** makes the sample mirror the population. For a
sample of 30:

```{python}
#| label: prop-alloc
alloc = proportional_allocation(RANK_COUNTS, 30)
alloc.round(3)
```

With proportional allocation the ordinary sample mean is already the right
estimate, because each stratum appears in its population share.

**Disproportionate allocation** samples some strata more heavily, usually to
learn enough about a small group. With only
`{python} str(RANK_COUNTS["Assistant"])` assistant professors, proportional
allocation gives us
`{python} str(int(alloc.loc["Assistant", "sample size"]))` of them, too few to
say anything about assistant professors. Sampling 10 from each rank fixes
that, but now the sample over-represents the lower-paid ranks, and its plain
average is biased.

::: {.blue}
The **stratified estimate** of the population mean weights each *stratum
mean* by that stratum's share of the population, $W_h=N_h/N$:

$$\bar{x}_{\text{strat}}=\sum_h W_h\,\bar{x}_h .$$

Equivalently, each sampled observation from stratum $h$ gets weight
$W_h/n_h$.
:::

```{python}
#| label: strat-one
EQUAL = {"Assistant": 10, "Associate": 10, "Professor": 10}
strat = stratified_sample(faculty, "rank", EQUAL, seed=11)
st = stratified_estimate(strat, "rank", "salary", RANK_COUNTS)
st.round(3)
```

```{python}
#| label: strat-one-stats
#| include: false
st_est = st.attrs["stratified estimate"]
st_raw = st.attrs["unweighted sample mean"]
```

The plain average of these 30 salaries is `{python} money(st_raw)`; the
stratified estimate is `{python} money(st_est)`; the truth is
`{python} money(MU_FAC)`. One sample proves little, so here is each design run
1,000 times:

```{python}

UNEQUAL = {"Assistant": 6, "Associate": 9, "Professor": 15}
```

::: {.charcoal}
Read the `bias` and `standard error` columns together. **Bias** is how far the
design's estimates sit from the truth *on average*; the **standard error** is
how much they scatter from sample to sample. RMSE combines the two.

- Proportional stratification beats a simple random sample of the same
  size: a standard error of `{python} money(se_prop)` against
  `{python} money(se_srs)`. Making sure every rank is represented in its
  proper share removes the luck of drawing too many or too few full
  professors. That is what stratification is for.
- The 10/10/10 sample's plain average is too low by
  `{python} money(abs(b_plain))` on average. It is not a bad sample; it is a bad *estimate*, because it ignores
  how the sample was drawn. Weighting by $W_h$ removes the bias.
- Weighting each *observation* by $W_h$, instead of each *stratum mean*, is
  a tempting mistake. With unequal stratum sample sizes it leaves a bias of
  `{python} money(b_wrong)`, because it counts the heavily sampled strata

## Cluster sampling

::: {.blue}
In **cluster sampling**, the population is divided into mutually exclusive
groups, or **clusters**, each of which is ideally a small version of the
whole population. A random sample of *clusters* is chosen, and everyone
(or a random sample) within the chosen clusters is measured. Clusters are
often geographic.
:::

The nine universities are natural clusters. A researcher who must travel to
collect data could visit **3 of the 9 campuses**, chosen at random, and record
every economist there. Note what that is *not*: visiting all nine campuses and
sampling within each is *stratified* sampling, with campuses as strata.

```{python}
    "cluster: 3 of 9 campuses, everyone":

    "simple random sample, n = 72":
        lambda fr, s: simple_random_sample(fr, 72, s)["salary"].mean(),
}, reps=1_000)
cl.round(0)
```

```{python}
#| include: false

se_cl_srs = float(cl.iloc[1]["standard error"])
```

Three random campuses average about 72 faculty, so the comparison is with a
simple random sample of 72. The cluster sample's standard error is
`{python} money(se_cl)` against `{python} money(se_cl_srs)`. Members of a
cluster resemble each other (Michigan pays more than Purdue), so 72
economists from three campuses carry less information than 72 drawn from all
nine.

::: {.brown}
**Stratified versus cluster.** The two are easy to confuse, because both
divide the population into groups.

| | Stratified | Cluster |
|---|---|---|
| Groups are | internally alike (all full professors) | mixed, each a mini-population |
| You sample | from **every** group | **some** groups, entirely |
| Purpose | more **accuracy** | lower **cost** |
| Precision vs. a simple random sample of the same size | usually better | usually worse |

A cluster sample is the right choice when the cost saved (three trips instead
of nine) buys a larger sample than the precision lost.
:::

## Nonprobability sampling

::: {.blue}
A **convenience sample** is one whose members are chosen because they are
easy to reach.
:::

Customers who happen to walk in, your own students, an online poll, people
who choose to write a review: all are convenience samples. They are fine for
a quick reaction to a new product. They do not support the probability
calculations in the rest of this chapter, because nobody knows how members
got in.

::: {.scarlet}
**A bigger convenience sample is not a better one.** In 1936 the *Literary
Digest* mailed ballots to names drawn from telephone directories and
automobile registrations, people who in the Depression were disproportionately
well off and Republican. About 2.4 million responded, and the *Digest*
predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a
landslide. Gallup, using a far smaller sample chosen to represent the
electorate, called the winner.

The error had nothing to do with sample size and everything to do with *who*
could be sampled. Twelve years later, in 1948, the major polls predicted
Thomas Dewey would beat Harry Truman, in part because they used quota
samples (interviewers filled demographic quotas with whomever they chose)
and stopped polling weeks before an electorate that was still moving.
:::

# Sampling and Nonsampling Errors

::: {.blue}
A **parameter** is a number that describes a population, such as its mean
$\mu$. A **statistic** is a number calculated from a sample, such as the
sample mean $\bar{x}$. Statistics are used to estimate parameters.

The **sampling error** of the sample mean is the difference between the
statistic and the parameter:

$$\text{sampling error}=\bar{x}-\mu .$$
:::

A small population makes sampling error something you can see completely. A
Henderson marketing office has ten employees, with these ages:

```{python}
#| label: ages
AGES = [24, 33, 28, 41, 38, 29, 26, 33, 37, 31]
mu_age = float(np.mean(AGES))
print("Ages:", AGES, f"   mu = {mu_age:.1f}")
```

Take the first two employees as a sample: $\bar{x}=(24+33)/2=28.5$, a
sampling error of $28.5-32.0=-3.5$ years. The last two give
$(37+31)/2=34.0$, an error of $+2.0$. But there is no need to pick samples
one at a time. With ten people there are only `{python} str(comb(10, 2))`
possible samples of two and `{python} str(comb(10, 5))` samples of five, and
we can look at all of them:

```{python}
#| label: all-errors
err2 = all_sample_errors(AGES, 2)
err5 = all_sample_errors(AGES, 5)
summary = pd.DataFrame({
    "possible samples": [len(err2), len(err5)],
    "mean of the sample means": [err2["sample mean"].mean(), err5["sample mean"].mean()],
    "share with |error| <= 2 years": [(err2["sampling error"].abs() <= 2).mean(),
                                      (err5["sampling error"].abs() <= 2).mean()],
    "largest negative error": [err2["sampling error"].min(), err5["sampling error"].min()],
    "largest positive error": [err2["sampling error"].max(), err5["sampling error"].max()],
}, index=["n = 2", "n = 5"])
summary.round(3)
```

```{python}
worst5 = err5.loc[err5["sampling error"].idxmax()]
```

::: {.brown}
Three lessons, each visible in the table:

1. **Sampling errors vary from sample to sample, and can be positive or
   negative.** Which one you get is luck.
2. **They average out.** The mean of all possible sample means is exactly
   $\mu=$ `{python} f"{mu_age:.1f}"`, for both sample sizes.
3. **Larger samples make small errors more likely, not certain.**
   `{python} pct(w2, 0)` of samples of two land within two years of $\mu$;
   `{python} pct(w5, 0)` of samples of five do. But the unluckiest sample of
   five, {`{python} worst5["sample"]`}, is still off by
   `{python} f"{worst5['sampling error']:+.1f}"` years.
:::

In practice $\mu$ is unknown, so we never know the sampling error of the
sample we actually drew. What we can know, as the rest of this chapter shows,
is how *large* sampling errors tend to be.

## Nonsampling errors

::: {.blue}
**Nonsampling errors** arise from how data are collected rather than from
which members happened to be sampled: ambiguous or leading questions, a
sampling frame that leaves people out, people who choose whether to respond,
recording mistakes, faulty instruments.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
Sampling error shrinks as the sample grows. **Nonsampling error does not.**
Asking a biased question of 10,000 people gives a more precise estimate of
the wrong thing.
:::

### Application: what online reviews can and cannot tell you

A product has 25,596 online ratings: 88% five stars, 8% four, 2% three, 1% two,
and 1% one. Treating these as a sample of customers, the average and its
standard error are easy to compute:

```{python}
#| label: reviews
N_REV = 25_596
shares = {5: 0.88, 4: 0.08, 3: 0.02, 2: 0.01, 1: 0.01}
ratings = np.repeat(list(shares), [round(N_REV * s) for s in shares.values()])
m_r, s_r = ratings.mean(), ratings.std(ddof=1)
print(f"{len(ratings):,} ratings: mean = {m_r:.3f}, s = {s_r:.3f}, "
      f"standard error s/sqrt(n) = {s_r / sqrt(len(ratings)):.4f}")
```

With a standard error of a few thousandths of a star, the sampling error is
negligible. The real question is whether reviewers are a random sample of
customers, and they are not: people choose whether to review, and the
delighted and the furious are the most likely to bother. Suppose, for
illustration, that among *all* customers the ratings would be spread
35/30/15/10/10% from five stars to one, and that a five-star customer writes a
review 25% of the time, a one-star customer 15%, and everyone else 3–5%:

```{python}
#| label: self-selection
population_ratings = np.repeat([5, 4, 3, 2, 1], [35, 30, 15, 10, 10])
review_prob = {5: 0.25, 4: 0.05, 3: 0.03, 2: 0.05, 1: 0.15}
sel = selection_bias_demo(population_ratings, review_prob, [100, 1_000, 10_000])
sel.round(3)
```

```{python}
#| label: self-selection-stats
#| include: false
bias_big = float(sel["bias"].iloc[-1])
se_big = float(sel["standard error s/sqrt(n)"].iloc[-1])
```

As the number of reviews grows from 100 to 10,000, the standard error falls
to `{python} f"{se_big:.3f}"` and the bias stays at about
`{python} f"{bias_big:+.2f}"` stars. A thousand times more reviews buys a
thousand times more confidence in a number that is still wrong.

::: {.scarlet}
One more caution about that example. Star ratings are **ordinal** data: the
gap between two and three stars need not equal the gap between four and five.
Averaging them treats them as interval data. It is universal practice, and
your textbook does it too, but know that you are doing it.
:::

# The Sampling Distribution of the Mean

## Every possible sample

A restaurant offers four entrées: vegetarian \$12, chicken \$14, fish \$16,
and steak \$18. A diner picks one at random, so the price is a discrete
distribution with each value equally likely.

```{python}
#| label: entree-pop
PRICES = [12, 14, 16, 18]
one = enumerate_sample_means(PRICES, 1)
print(f"Population: mu = {one.attrs['mu']:.2f}, sigma = {one.attrs['sigma']:.4f}")
_ = pmf_chart(PRICES, [0.25] * 4, title="Price of one randomly chosen entrée",
              xlab="Price ($)", ymax=0.42, figsize=(7, 3.6))
```

You and a friend each choose an entrée at random. That is a random sample of
size $n=2$ drawn *with replacement* (you may both choose fish). There are
$4\times4=16$ equally likely samples. Here is the average price for every one
of them, and the resulting distribution of the sample mean:

```{python}
#| label: entree-two
grid = pd.DataFrame([[(a + b) / 2 for b in PRICES] for a in PRICES],
                    index=pd.Index([f"you: ${a}" for a in PRICES]),
                    columns=[f"friend: ${b}" for b in PRICES])
print("Average price for each of the 16 samples:\n")
print(grid.to_string(), "\n")
two = enumerate_sample_means(PRICES, 2)
two.round(4)
```

```{python}
#| label: entree-charts
#| layout-ncol: 2
_ = pmf_chart(two.index, two["P(x-bar)"], title="Sampling distribution of x-bar, n = 2",
              xlab="Average price ($)", mean=15, ymax=0.42, figsize=(5.5, 3.8))
three = enumerate_sample_means(PRICES, 3)
_ = pmf_chart(three.index.round(2), three["P(x-bar)"],
              title="Sampling distribution of x-bar, n = 3",
              xlab="Average price ($)", mean=15, ymax=0.42, figsize=(5.5, 3.8), width=0.5)
```

::: {.blue}
The **sampling distribution of the mean** is the probability distribution of
$\bar{x}$ over all possible samples of size $n$ from a population.
:::

Look at what happened. The population is flat: every price equally likely.
The sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ for two diners is already peaked at
\$15, because an average of \$15 can happen four ways and an average of \$12
only one. With three diners
(`{python} str(three.attrs['samples'])` samples), it is starting to look like a
bell. Averaging pulls values toward the middle. That is the Central Limit
Theorem beginning to work, and we will meet it formally in a moment.

Now compute the mean and standard deviation of the sampling distribution,
exactly as for any discrete distribution in Chapter 5:

```{python}
#| label: entree-moments
pd.DataFrame({
    "population (n = 1)": [one.attrs["mean of x-bar"], one.attrs["sd of x-bar"], np.nan],
    "x-bar, n = 2": [two.attrs["mean of x-bar"], two.attrs["sd of x-bar"], two.attrs["sigma / sqrt(n)"]],
    "x-bar, n = 3": [three.attrs["mean of x-bar"], three.attrs["sd of x-bar"], three.attrs["sigma / sqrt(n)"]],
}, index=["mean", "standard deviation", "sigma / sqrt(n)"]).round(4)
```

The mean of the sample means equals the population mean, \$15, every time.
The standard deviation of the sample means shrinks, and it equals
$\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ exactly: `{python} f"{one.attrs['sigma']:.4f}"` divided by
$\sqrt{2}$ is `{python} f"{two.attrs['sigma / sqrt(n)']:.4f}"`.

::: {.blue}
For samples of size $n$ from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard
deviation $\sigma$:

$$\mu_{\bar{x}}=\mu \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} .$$

$\sigma_{\bar{x}}$ is the **standard error of the mean**: the standard
deviation of the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$. It measures how far a
sample mean typically lands from $\mu$.
:::

::: {.charcoal}
Two consequences worth saying out loud.

The sample mean is **unbiased**: it is not systematically too high or too
low. Any single sample can miss, but the misses average to zero.

The standard error shrinks with the **square root** of $n$. Quadrupling the
sample halves the standard error; to cut it by a factor of ten, you need a
hundred times the data. Precision gets expensive, which is why sample size is
a business decision and not only a statistical one.
:::

## Probabilities for a sample mean

::: {.blue}
When the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is normal, probabilities for a
sample mean come from the $z$-score of $\bar{x}$:

$$z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_{\bar{x}}}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} .$$
:::

::: {.scarlet}
The denominator is the **standard error**, $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, not $\sigma$.
Dividing by $\sigma$ answers a question about *one person*. Dividing by
$\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ answers a question about the *average of n people*. This is
the most common mistake in the chapter, and it produces an answer that looks
perfectly reasonable.
:::

# The Central Limit Theorem

::: {.blue}
**The Central Limit Theorem (CLT).** For samples of size $n$ drawn from any
population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the sampling
distribution of $\bar{x}$ is **approximately normal** with mean $\mu$ and
standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, and the approximation improves as $n$
grows.

- If the population is itself **normal**, $\bar{x}$ is exactly normal for
  **any** $n$.
- Otherwise, $n\ge 30$ is the usual **rule of thumb** for "large enough." The
  more skewed the population, the larger $n$ must be.
:::

This is the reason statistics is a required course. It says that averages
behave predictably even when individuals do not, so a manager can make
probability statements about an average without knowing the shape of the
population it came from.

## Watching it happen

Three populations: a flat one (the entrées), a right-skewed one (exponential
waiting times from Chapter 6, mean 4 minutes), and Nevada adjusted gross
income, which is strongly right-skewed. For each, we draw 5,000 *fresh*
samples of each size from the population and plot the sample means, with the
normal curve the CLT predicts on top.

```{python}
#| label: clt-pops
rng = np.random.default_rng(20261003)
POPS = {
    "Entrée prices (flat)": np.repeat(PRICES, 2_500),
    "Waiting times (exponential)": rng.exponential(4.0, 100_000),
    "Nevada AGI (right-skewed)": agi.to_numpy(),
}
pd.DataFrame({name: {"mean": p.mean(), "sd": p.std(),
                     "median": np.median(p)}
              for name, p in POPS.items()}).T.round(3)
```

```{python}
#| label: clt-grid
#| layout-ncol: 3
for name, pop in POPS.items():
    mu_p, sd_p = pop.mean(), pop.std()
    for n in (2, 10, 30):
        means = simulate_sample_means(pop, n, reps=5_000)
        se_p = sd_p / sqrt(n)
        gx = np.linspace(mu_p - 4 * se_p, mu_p + 4 * se_p, 400)
        if name.startswith("Entr"):
            # Averages of $2-spaced prices sit on a grid of step 2/n: one bar per
            # possible value, so the bar areas are probabilities.
            step = 2 / n
            bins = np.arange(12 - step / 2, 18 + step, step)
        else:
            bins = 40
        _ = density_histogram(means, gx, normal_pdf(gx, mu_p, se_p), bins=bins,
                              title=f"{name.split(' (')[0]}, n = {n}",
                              xlab="sample mean", curve_label="CLT normal",
                              bar_label="5,000 sample means", figsize=(4.2, 3.2))
```

Read down each column. The flat population's means are close to normal by
$n=10$. The waiting times need more: at $n=2$ the means are still skewed,
and by $n=30$ the histogram and the curve nearly agree. Nevada income is the
hardest case.

## When $n=30$ is not enough

The normal model says 2.5% of sample means fall more than 1.96 standard
errors above $\mu$, and 2.5% more than 1.96 below. For Nevada AGI, here is
what actually happens:

```{python}

mu_a, sd_a = float(agi.mean()), float(agi.std(ddof=0))
agi_rows = {}
for n in (30, 100, 400, 1_600):
    m = simulate_sample_means(agi, n, reps=5_000)
    agi_rows[f"n = {n}"] = tail_check(m, mu_a, sd_a / sqrt(n))
agi_tail = pd.DataFrame(agi_rows).T
agi_tail.round(4)
```

```{python}

#| include: false
lo30, hi30 = agi_tail.loc["n = 30", ["below mu - 1.96 SE", "above mu + 1.96 SE"]]
```

::: {.charcoal}
At $n=30$, `{python} pct(hi30, 1)` of sample means land above the upper cut-off
and only `{python} pct(lo30, 1)` below the lower one. The normal model says
2.5% each. A skewed population produces a skewed sampling distribution at
moderate $n$: occasional samples catch a few very high incomes and their
averages jump up, while no sample can catch incomes below zero. The tails
even out by a few hundred.

The practical rule: $n\ge30$ is a starting point, not a guarantee. For
incomes, insurance claims, hospital bills, order sizes, and anything else
with a long right tail, look at the data and expect to need more. (The AGI
file is simulated from IRS zip-code counts; its shape is real, its rows are
not. See `data/README.md`.)
:::

::: {.scarlet}
The CLT is about **averages**, not individuals. It says nothing about the
probability that *one* Nevada household earns more than a given amount;
that depends entirely on the skewed population. It lets you say how the
*average of n households* behaves.
:::

# How Much Do Sample Means Vary?

## The interval that contains 95% of sample means

::: {.blue}
If $\bar{x}$ is normal with mean $\mu$ and standard error
$\sigma_{\bar{x}}$, then 95% of all sample means fall within

$$\mu\pm1.96\,\sigma_{\bar{x}}=\mu\pm1.96\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} .$$

The 1.96 is `NORM.S.INV(0.975)`: it leaves 2.5% in each tail.
:::

A navigation app claims the average morning commute on a route is 50 minutes,
with a standard deviation of 14 minutes. If the claim is true, where should
the average of 42 trips fall?

```{python}

drive.to_frame("n = 42").round(4)
```

```{python}

_ = density_chart(dg, normal_pdf(dg, 50, se_d),
                  shade=(drive["lower"], drive["upper"]), area=0.95,
                  title="95% of sample means of 42 trips, if the claim is true",
                  xlab="Average commute (minutes), x-bar", mark=[50])
```

If the claim is true, 95% of samples of 42 trips will average between
`{python} f"{drive['lower']:.1f}"` and `{python} f"{drive['upper']:.1f}"`
minutes.

## The effect of sample size

```{python}

sizes.round(2)
```

```{python}

                  xlab="Average commute (minutes), x-bar")
```

Going from 42 trips to 168, four times the data, cuts the standard error and
the interval's width exactly in half. The sampling distribution gets taller
and narrower: sample means crowd around $\mu$.

::: {.brown}
**Toward Chapter 8.** In practice we do not know $\mu$; we know $\bar{x}$.
But if $\bar{x}$ is within $1.96\,\sigma_{\bar{x}}$ of $\mu$ in 95% of samples,
then $\mu$ is within $1.96\,\sigma_{\bar{x}}$ of $\bar{x}$ in 95% of samples.
Turn this interval around and it becomes a **confidence interval**, the
subject of Chapter 8.
:::

## Taking it to the limit: a census

As the sample grows toward the whole population, the sample mean closes in
on $\mu$. Measure everyone and $\bar{x}=\mu$: the sampling error is zero. The
formula $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ does not quite know this, because it assumes an
infinite population. The fix is in the next section.

Back to the commute. You time 42 trips and they average **45 minutes**. Is

how surprising the data would be.**


If the true mean were 50 minutes, a sample of 42 trips would average 45

probably *less* than 50 minutes. The 45 is also outside the 95% interval,

data cannot single out 50. Your textbook says "support the claim" in several
places. In Chapter 9 the distinction becomes the difference between

to unlearn it then.

# Sampling from a Finite Population

So far the population has been effectively infinite: Nielsen's 41,000
households are a sliver of 129 million. When the sample is a sizeable share
of the population, the standard error formula needs an adjustment.

::: {.blue}
When sampling **without replacement** from a population of size $N$, the
standard error of the mean is

$$\sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\sqrt{\frac{N-n}{N-1}} .$$

The factor $\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$ is the **finite population correction
(FPC)**. Use it when the sample is more than 5% of the population,
$n/N>0.05$.
:::

You have seen this factor before. In Chapter 5 it was the "shrink factor"
that made the hypergeometric distribution less spread out than the binomial:
every member you draw is one you cannot draw again, so a sample without
replacement from a small population tells you more than the same-sized
sample from a large one.

## Why the correction exists

A pool-service company in Henderson has 100 residential customers. Their
service ratings have historically averaged 7.2 on a 10-point scale, with a
standard deviation of 0.7. Here is the standard error for several sample
sizes, with and without the correction:

```{python}
#| label: fpc-table
fpc_tab = fpc_table(0.7, 100, [10, 40, 60, 80, 100])
fpc_tab.round(4)
```

Look at the last row. A sample of all 100 customers is a census: its mean
*is* $\mu$, and its sampling error is zero. The uncorrected formula still
reports a standard error of 0.07. The correction brings it to exactly zero,
and scales it down smoothly in between. Without it, you would overstate the
uncertainty in a large sample from a small population.

```{python}
#| label: fpc-sim
#| include: false
rng = np.random.default_rng(20261003)
pool_pop = np.clip(rng.normal(7.2, 0.7, 100), 1, 10)
pool_pop = (pool_pop - pool_pop.mean()) / pool_pop.std() * 0.7 + 7.2
sim_means = simulate_sample_means(pool_pop, 40, reps=10_000, replace=False)
```

A check by simulation: drawing 10,000 samples of 40 *without replacement*
from a made-up population of 100 ratings with $\sigma=0.7$, the sample means
have a standard deviation of `{python} f"{sim_means.std():.4f}"`. The
corrected formula says `{python} f"{se_mean(0.7, 40, 100):.4f}"`; the
uncorrected one says `{python} f"{0.7 / sqrt(40):.4f}"`.

And when the population is large, the correction disappears on its own. For
$n=30$ from $N=10{,}000$ it is `{python} f"{fpc(30, 10_000):.4f}"`, which is
why it can be dropped below the 5% threshold.

# The Sampling Distribution of the Proportion

Many business questions are about a **share**: the share of households that
watched, of graduates who found jobs, of orders that shipped late.

::: {.blue}
The **sample proportion** is

$$\hat{p}=\frac{x}{n},$$

where $x$ is the number of successes in a sample of $n$. For samples from a
population with proportion $p$, the sampling distribution of $\hat{p}$ has

$$\mu_{\hat{p}}=p \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sigma_{\hat{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}},$$

where $\sigma_{\hat{p}}$ is the **standard error of the proportion**. It is
approximately normal when $np\ge5$ and $n(1-p)\ge5$, the same conditions as
in Chapter 6, and

$$z=\frac{\hat{p}-p}{\sigma_{\hat{p}}} .$$

For a finite population with $n/N>0.05$, multiply $\sigma_{\hat{p}}$ by the
finite population correction.
:::

This is not a new idea. The number of successes $x$ is binomial, and Chapter
6 showed it is approximately normal with mean $np$ and standard deviation
$\sqrt{np(1-p)}$. Divide everything by $n$ and you have the formulas above. A
proportion is a count on a different scale. (Your textbook writes the sample
proportion as $\bar{p}$; these notes use $\hat{p}$, which is what Chapter 8
and most software use.)

## Application: Super Bowl viewership

A network sold advertising on the claim that 45% of US households would watch

```{python}
      f"both at least 5, so the normal approximation is licensed.")
print(f"standard error = sqrt(0.45 x 0.55 / 200) = {se_proportion(p_sb, n_sb):.4f}")
```

```{python}

```

```{python}

sb_exact = float(sb.iloc[4])
sb_cc = float(sb["normal, with continuity correction"])
sb_no = float(sb["normal, no correction"])
```

::: {.scarlet}
**The continuity correction still matters.** Your textbook drops it in this
chapter, saying samples are large enough for it not to matter. For this very
example it matters: the exact binomial answer is
`{python} f"{sb_exact:.4f}"`, the normal approximation *with* the correction
is `{python} f"{sb_cc:.4f}"`, and without it `{python} f"{sb_no:.4f}"`, two
percentage points off.

The policy in these notes:

- When the question is about a **count** ("84 or fewer households," "more than
  58 buyers"), apply the continuity correction exactly as in Chapter 6.
  On the proportion scale, half a count is $0.5/n$.
- When the question is about a **proportion or percentage** ("less than 40% of
  the sample"), use the normal curve directly.
- When software can compute the exact binomial, as it always can, that is
  the number to report. The approximation is for understanding.
:::

::: {.charcoal}
Now interpret. If 45% of households really watched, a sample showing 42% or
less would turn up about `{python} pct(sb_exact, 0)` of the time, roughly one
sample in five. That is not surprising. The sample **fails to contradict** the
network's claim.

That is all it does. Your textbook says this result "supports" the claim, and
it does not: 42% is also perfectly consistent with a true share of 40% or
43%. A large tail probability means the data have not given you a reason to
doubt the claim. It does not mean they have given you a reason to believe it.
:::

## Application: a finite population of graduates

A college with 770 graduates claims 70% landed jobs in their field. A
random survey of 120 graduates finds 97 did, $\hat{p}=0.808$. The sample is
`{python} pct(120 / 770, 1)` of the population, above 5%, so the finite
population correction applies.

```{python}

gbc.to_frame("97 or more of 120, N = 770")
```

```{python}

#| include: false

```

The exact answer, which for sampling without replacement from 770 graduates
is hypergeometric, is `{python} f"{gbc_exact:.4f}"`: a result this good would
almost never happen if the true rate were 70%. The college is, if anything,
understating its placement rate. All three methods agree on "very rare,"
though in a tail this far out they differ in the details; that is a reason to
report the exact number.

# The Bootstrap: A Standard Error from One Sample

Every calculation so far needed $\sigma$ or $p$, the population's own
parameters. In practice you rarely know them. The **bootstrap** estimates a
standard error from the one sample you have.

::: {.blue}
The **bootstrap** treats the sample as a stand-in for the population. It
draws many **resamples** of the same size *from the sample*, with
replacement, computes the statistic for each, and uses the spread of those
statistics to estimate the standard error.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
The bootstrap is not a way of collecting data, and it is not a fifth kind of
probability sample, although your textbook lists it beside the sampling
designs. It is a way of **analysing** a sample you already have. Every
resample comes from that one sample, never from the population.
:::

## Application: female shoppers

A store manager examines a random sample of 100 transactions and finds 58
were made by women. How precise is $\hat{p}=0.58$?

```{python}
#| label: boot-prop
shoppers = np.array([1] * 58 + [0] * 42)
boot_p = bootstrap(shoppers, np.mean, reps=10_000)
bi = bootstrap_interval(boot_p)
print(f"bootstrap standard error = {bi['bootstrap standard error']:.4f}")
print(f"formula sqrt(p-hat (1 - p-hat) / n) = {sqrt(0.58 * 0.42 / 100):.4f}")
print(f"middle 95% of bootstrap proportions: {bi['lower']:.3f} to {bi['upper']:.3f}")
```

```{python}
#| label: boot-prop-chart
bg = np.linspace(0.38, 0.78, 400)
_ = density_histogram(boot_p, bg, normal_pdf(bg, 0.58, sqrt(0.58 * 0.42 / 100)),
                      bins=30, title="10,000 bootstrap proportions",
                      xlab="Share of transactions by women", curve_label="normal, formula SE",
                      bar_label="bootstrap resamples")
```

The bootstrap standard error matches the formula, with no formula needed.
That is the point: the same resampling works for a median, a ratio, a 90th
percentile, or any statistic that has no tidy standard error formula. The
middle 95% of the bootstrap proportions is a first look at the confidence
interval of Chapter 8.

# Which Formula Is It?

| The question is about | Center | Standard error | Correction if $n/N>0.05$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| one individual value $x$ | $\mu$ | $\sigma$ (not a standard error) | none |
| a sample mean $\bar{x}$ | $\mu$ | $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ | $\times\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$ |
| a sample proportion $\hat{p}$ | $p$ | $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ | $\times\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$ |
| a count of successes $x$ | $np$ | $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ | continuity correction $\pm0.5$ |

chosen hotels exceeds \$163?

**(c)** 58% of Generation Y pay their bills on time. In a sample of 200, what
is the probability that 125 or fewer do?

**(d)** A college has 240 employees on its health plan, with mean
out-of-pocket cost \$1,880 and standard deviation \$515. An audit samples 30.
What is the probability the sample mean is below \$1,825?
:::
