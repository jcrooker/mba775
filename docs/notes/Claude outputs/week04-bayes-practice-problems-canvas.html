The method in one table

Every problem below gives you a conditional probability in one direction and asks for it in the other. Use the same four columns each time:

State of the worldPrior P(Ai)Likelihood P(B | Ai)Joint = prior × likelihoodPosterior = joint ÷ total
A1, A2, …sums to 1given in the problemtotal = P(B)sums to 1

Common mistake: P(B | A) and P(A | B) are different numbers. The event you were told happened goes in the denominator. See the Bayes’ Theorem section of the Chapter 4 lecture notes.

Problem 1: Flagged credit card transactions

Is a flag really fraud?

A fraud screen flags a transaction. How likely is it that the charge is actually fraudulent? The screen’s accuracy is not the answer. When the base rate is small, the prior does a lot of the work, and the gap between P(flag | fraud) and P(fraud | flag) is the whole lesson.

Problem 2: Webinar attendance and lead conversions

Two routes, same answer

Marketing knows how often webinar attendees and non-attendees convert. A lead converts: what is the probability they attended? Video 2 solves it with the formula. Video 3 solves it again with the four-column Bayes table (prior, likelihood, joint, posterior). Watch both and confirm they agree.

Problem 3: Tracing a defective part to its supplier

More than two states of the world

Parts come from several suppliers, each with its own share of the volume and its own defect rate. A part fails inspection. Which supplier most likely made it? The denominator, P(defective), sums over every supplier that could have produced the part.

Problem 4: Loyalty members and redemption offers

Reverse the conditional

The data says how often loyalty members and non-members redeem an offer. The manager wants the other direction: given a customer redeemed, how likely is it they are a loyalty member? This is the move Bayes’ Theorem exists to make.

Problem 5: Unfavorable test markets and product success

Revising a subjective prior

Management has a prior belief that a new product will succeed. Then a test market comes back unfavorable. How much should that one piece of imperfect evidence lower the odds? The prior is subjective here, but the update follows the same rules.

Problem 6: AI detectors and academic dishonesty

When a false positive is costly

An AI detector flags a student’s essay. What is the probability the student actually cheated? Even an accurate-sounding detector can produce many false accusations when most students are honest, and here a false positive has real consequences for a person.
