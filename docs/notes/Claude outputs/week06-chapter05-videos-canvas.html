Part 1 · Discrete Probability Distributions

Rules, mean, variance, and comparing distributions

01

Rules of a Discrete Probability Distribution

Three rules, every time

A random variable is either counted (discrete) or measured (continuous). Every discrete probability distribution obeys three rules: outcomes are mutually exclusive, each P(x) lies between 0 and 1, and the probabilities sum to 1. Watch for the bakery's candidate C — it sums to 1, but one entry is negative.

Where distributions come from

03

The mean of a discrete probability distribution

μ = Σ x P(x)

The mean is a weighted average with the probabilities as weights. For the production line it is 2.43 defective units — a value no sample will ever contain. It describes the long run, and a 100,000-draw simulation shows the law of large numbers pulling the running average to it.

The variance and standard deviation of a discrete probability distribution

σ² = Σ(x−μ)² P(x)

Two routes to the same variance (1.7051): the definition and the shortcut Σx²P(x) − μ². Report the standard deviation, 1.31 defective units, because it is in the units of the data. Includes the Excel SUMPRODUCT setup.

Comparing two discrete probability distributions

Same mean, different story

Henderson and Summerlin have similar mean star ratings (4.06 vs. 3.85), but standard deviations of 1.05 vs. 1.62 reveal that Summerlin is a store customers either love or leave. Plus a caution: averaging ordinal ratings treats them as interval data.

Decisions in dollars — and the risk behind them

Application: Expected monetary value

EMV = the mean in dollars

RAD Construction's Pike Creek Road bridge pays $120K early, $100K on time, $90K late. Its expected monetary value is $103,500 — a profit RAD will never actually earn. What does that number mean for a firm that bids on many projects?

Choosing among project alternatives and the Coefficient of Variation

Reward vs. risk

When the state's modified contract has the same EMV but a larger spread, the coefficient of variation (CV = σ/μ) breaks the tie. We also choose how many croissants a coffee cart should stock: the highest EMV is not the smoothest ride, and insurance shows when a worse EMV can be the right call.

Counting successes in n independent trials

The Binomial Distribution

Fixed n, two outcomes, constant p, independence

The four characteristics of a binomial experiment — and why “success” is a label, not a compliment. We build the formula from Chapter 4: three customers, p = 0.4, and the number of arrangements times the probability of each.

09

Applications with the Binomial Distribution

Translate the English first

Most binomial mistakes happen before any math. “Fewer than 4,” “at most 4,” and “at least 4” are different events, and “at least 4” is 1 − P(X ≤ 3), not 1 − P(X ≤ 4). Practice reading answers straight off a cumulative table.

▶ Watch Video 09

10

The mean, variance and standard deviation of a binomial distribution

μ = np, σ = √(npq)

The general formulas collapse into two short ones. With 50 customers and p = 0.4, we expect 20 big spenders, give or take 3.46 — the benchmark for judging whether an observed count is surprising.

11

Analyzing customer behavior with a Binomial Distribution

Your first hypothesis test

Headquarters says 40% of customers spend $100 or more. Yesterday, only 5 of 50 did. P(X ≤ 5) is about 1 in 370,000. Either it was a freak day, or Headquarters is wrong — and we see why we use the tail (“5 or fewer”) rather than “exactly 5.”

12

Further applications with the binomial distribution

Reading the output

A bank mails credit-line offers to 15 customers (p = 0.30). Using only the table: exactly, at most, fewer than, at least, more than, and “between 3 and 6 inclusive.” Complements must sum to 1 — run that check every time. Excel's BINOM.DIST reproduces it all.

▶ Watch Video 12

13

Shapes of the binomial distribution

Skew depends on p

With p below 0.5 the distribution skews right; above 0.5 it skews left; at 0.5 it is symmetric. As n grows, the binomial starts to look like the bell curve — a preview of Chapter 6.

▶ Watch Video 13

14

Can we use the binomial distribution to model Nevada economic health?

Testing the independence assumption

Is the number of low-growth quarters in a Nevada year binomial with n = 4, p = 0.25? The means match, but the observed variance (1.80) is more than double npq (0.75). Bad quarters arrive in clumps — and a binomial model quietly understates the extremes that sink a business.

Part 4 · The Poisson Distribution

Counting occurrences over an interval

15

The Poisson Distribution

Counts over an interval, not trials

Customers per hour, calls per ten minutes, flaws per square yard. The Poisson is set by a single number, λ: the mean and the variance are both λ, the mean need not be a whole number, and there is no upper limit on x.

▶ Watch Video 15

16

Application: Customers arriving to the store in the next hour

“More than” needs the complement

If 4 extra customers are expected in an added hour, P(more than 8) = 1 − P(X ≤ 8) = 0.0214. Don't forget x = 0: it is an outcome too, and leaving it out costs almost 2 percentage points.

▶ Watch Video 16

17

Application: Changing the interval for a Poisson data generating process

Rescale λ, never the probability

At 4 customers an hour, P(exactly 4 in an hour) = 0.1954 but P(exactly 2 in 30 minutes) = 0.2707. Half the time is not half the probability. When the interval changes, the first line of your work is a new λ.

▶ Watch Video 17

18

Approximating the binomial distribution with the Poisson

n ≥ 20 and p ≤ 0.05 ⇒ λ = np

A warehouse mis-packs 3% of orders; 40 are inspected. The binomial gives 0.2206 for exactly 2, the Poisson 0.2169. The real payoff is the idea: rare events (typos, fraud, warranty claims) are Poisson because they are binomials with a huge n and a tiny p.

Part 5 · The Hypergeometric Distribution

Small populations, no replacement

19

The Hypergeometric distribution

Sampling without replacement

When you sample from a small population, p changes with every draw. Pick 3 of 12 store managers: P(none inexperienced) = 0.5455. Then a casino audits 6 of 25 cabinets and 5 belong to one vendor — a 1-in-180 result. Does the vendor have a case?
