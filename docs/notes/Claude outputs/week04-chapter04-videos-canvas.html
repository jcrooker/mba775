Part 1 · Three Kinds of Probability

Classical, empirical, and subjective — and how they connect

01

Introduction to Probability

The hinge of the course

A probability is a number between 0 and 1. We set up the vocabulary the rest of the semester runs on: experiment, sample space, event, and simple event — and preview the three ways to put a number on a probability.

02

Classical probability and a worked example

Equal likelihood · the right sample space

Classical probability works when every outcome is equally likely. What is the chance of rolling a 7 with two dice? Not 1/11 — the eleven sums aren’t equally likely. The 36 ordered pairs are, and 6 of them make a 7: P = 0.1667. When a probability feels hard, the sample space is usually the problem.

03

Empirical probability

Count what actually happened

Empirical probability is a relative frequency: how often an event occurred divided by the number of observations. It is only as good as the data behind it — and the definitions you choose.

Empirical Probability Application: Nevada Economy

Defining the states

We cut Nevada’s GDP growth and unemployment at their quartiles into Low, Middle, and High quarters. Note what just happened: “low growth” is a definition we chose, so “P(low growth) = 25%” is nearly circular. The interesting probabilities are the conditional ones.

05

The Law of Large Numbers

Empirical → classical, eventually

Simulate a million dice rolls and the empirical probability of a 7 lands at 0.16650 against a classical 0.16667. But convergence is slow: at 10 or 100 rolls it’s visibly wrong. “We ran a pilot with 40 customers” is the left end of that chart.

06

Subjective probability

Judgment someone owns

Most consequential business probabilities are subjective: will the drug pass its trial, will the Fed cut rates? That doesn’t make them arbitrary — it makes them claims someone is responsible for. Can you classify the Raiders’ playoff odds, a heart from a shuffled deck, and Strip hotel cancellations?

Part 2 · Rules and Two Events at Once

The basic rules, contingency tables, and the addition rule

07

Basic rules of probability

Five rules · the complement

Probabilities lie in [0, 1], and the simple events sum to 1 — the check to run on any table you’re handed. Rule 5, the complement, does more work than its length suggests: “at least one” is almost always 1 − P(none). And why competent forecasters never say 0% or 100%.

▶ Watch Video 07

08

Contingency tables

Joint vs. marginal

A contingency table records how two categorical variables occur together. Pairing each Nevada quarter with the economy six months later, the body cells are joint probabilities and the margins are marginal probabilities. “And” means intersection; “or” means union — and always includes both.

▶ Watch Video 08

09

Addition rules and mutually exclusive events

Don’t count the overlap twice

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). In the Nevada example the right answer is 21.79%; forgetting to subtract the 2 quarters in both events gives 24.36%. Mutually exclusive events have no overlap, so there’s nothing to subtract.

Part 3 · Conditional Probability and Independence

What new information does to a probability

10

Conditional probability

P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A)

The conditioning event goes in the denominator. From the same 2 Nevada quarters we get 28.57% (the forecasting question: how worried should I be about six months from now?) and 16.67% (the diagnostic question). Confusing the two is the error behind most misread medical tests.

▶ Watch Video 10

11

Independence and dependence

Did the probability move?

Events are independent when P(A|B) = P(A). A million dice rolls show dice have no memory; Nevada’s economy does — which is why forecasting is a job. And a twist: mutually exclusive events are not independent. They’re nearly opposites.

▶ Watch Video 11

12

The multiplication rule

P(A and B) = P(A) P(B|A)

An auditor pulls 2 of 32 invoices, 9 of which have errors. Without replacement, P(both bad) = 9/32 × 8/31 = 0.0726; with replacement it’s 0.0791. Plus the conjunction fallacy: adding an “and” can make a story feel more plausible while making it less likely.

Part 4 · Probability Trees and Bayes’ Theorem

Revising beliefs in light of evidence

13

Probability trees

Multiply along the branches

A probability tree draws marginal and joint probabilities as branches, making the multiplication rule literal: the probability at the end of a path is the product along it. We build one for Nevada’s most common economic states.

▶ Watch Video 13

14

Bayes' Theorem

Prior → likelihood → posterior

Bayes’ Theorem is what to do when you know the conditional probability in one direction and need it in the other. Build the four-column Bayes table — prior, likelihood, joint, posterior — and it recovers the 0.2857 we read straight off the contingency table. Same answer, different route.

Part 5 · Counting Principles

Sizing the sample space

15

Counting principles for classical probability calculations

Does order matter?

The fundamental counting principle (4,032 possible groups of regional managers), factorials (40,320 interview schedules), and the one question that separates permutations from combinations: would swapping two choices change the outcome? Electing a Senior and Associate manager: 132. Two managers to Hawaii: 21. And why your lottery numbers are never “due” (1 in 13,983,816).
