Seeing the pattern a table hides

Displaying descriptive statistics

Why we visualize

Senior leaders can usually draw insight from raw data. What they lack is time. Visualization presents data so its important features are clear. We preview the chapter’s chart types and the judgment calls each one involves.

Visualizing patterns in data: a demo with extreme poverty and real gdp per capita

A table hides the structure

Two centuries of world GDP per capita alongside the share of people living in extreme poverty. In 1820, nine people in ten lived in extreme poverty; by 2015, about one in ten did. A ten-row table holds the facts, but only the chart shows the shape of the relationship. We also cover why the axis is logarithmic and what “constant international-$ at 2011 prices” actually means.

Part 2 · Case Study: Home Prices and Population Growth

When a chart looks like a pattern — and when it really is one

03

Identifying home price and population growth rate relationships by US State

A chart that only looks like a pattern

Across the 50 states, faster population growth seems to go with higher house price indexes. Then look at which states are at the top. A house price index set to 1980 = 100 measures cumulative appreciation since 1980, not what a house costs today. The chart compares a 45-year change with a 10-year change.

▶ Watch Video 03

04

Visualizing the relationship between the change in home price indices by State and the State's population growth rate

Compare like with like

Swap the index level for its ten-year percentage change, so both variables cover the same decade. The relationship gets much stronger. The scatter also shows the exceptions that binned averages hide. Two cautions follow: this is still correlation, not causation, and the time window you pick is itself a choice.

Part 3 · Displaying Quantitative Data

Frequency distributions, histograms, and ogives

05

Guidelines for displaying quantitative Data

Counted vs. measured

Discrete data are counted; continuous data are measured. The test: how many values are possible inside an interval? That distinction decides whether bars get gaps, how a frequency distribution is built, and later, which probability chapter (5 or 6) applies. Also: never delete an empty class.

▶ Watch Video 05

06

Grouping Data and Histograms

Bins, frequencies, and shape

When values must be grouped, a frequency distribution counts observations in each bin, with relative and cumulative frequencies alongside. The histogram reveals skew: a few fast-growing states pull the mean above the median. Bin width can quietly change the story the chart tells.

▶ Watch Video 06

07

The Ogive and determining the number of classes for grouped data

2k ≥ n

The ogive plots cumulative relative frequency, and the point where it crosses 50% is the median. How many classes? With 50 states, 25 = 32 < 50 ≤ 64 = 26, so k = 6. Then the rules for grouped classes: include every value, keep classes mutually exclusive, avoid empty classes, and avoid open-ended ones where you can.

Part 4 · Qualitative and Two-Variable Displays

Pareto and pie charts, contingency tables, stem-and-leaf, scatter plots

08

Pareto Charts and Pie Charts

Sorted bars, honest bases

A Pareto chart sorts categories from most to least frequent and adds a cumulative line, so it answers “how few categories account for how much?” Pie charts stop working beyond three or four slices. Every pie needs a stated base: if a question was asked of only some respondents, the caption has to say so.

▶ Watch Video 08

09

Contingency Tables and Stem-Leaf Diagrams

Two variables, every observation

A contingency table shows two categorical variables at once. A heatmap of a small sample can look authoritative while carrying very little information, so check the counts in each cell. John Tukey’s stem-and-leaf display keeps every observation visible. His rule: don’t cut your leafless stems.

▶ Watch Video 09

10

Scatter plots

x is given, y responds

A scatter plot shows every observation without summarizing, which is why analysts draw one first. Putting a variable on the horizontal axis is a claim that it is the independent variable, and you make that claim before running any statistics. Regression in Chapter 10 will ask you to defend it. Connect the points over time and you have a line chart.
