The role technology plays in statistics

Hardcore statisticians are perfectly happy to receive large quantities of data in tabular formats. Likely this is because they know how to import the data into their preferred statistical interface and can perform their own analysis on it. Artificial intelligence tools are cutting the gap between what requires a hardcore statistician and what an interested person can do.

Senior leaders often have the analytical ability to draw insight from raw data. What they do not have is the time. Even traditional descriptive statistics — mean, median, quartiles, minimum and maximum — take more effort to interpret than many will spend. That is a particular challenge when your goal is to persuade. We do not want our audience’s eyes to glaze over. We want to make the case from the data as elegantly as possible.

This brings us to data visualization: presenting data so that its important features are clear. This takes skill. There are many chart types to choose from, and some are far better than others at showing a particular pattern. AI is a genuine boon to producing them — and no help at all in deciding which one is honest.

Index, 1980 Q1 = 100. Every state starts at 100 in 1980 by construction. So a state’s index level does not measure what a house costs there. It measures how much prices have risen since 1980 — cumulative appreciation over forty-five years.

Massachusetts is at the top because Massachusetts has appreciated the most since 1980, not because it is expensive today. The chart is not a housing price chart at all. It is a chart of forty-five-year growth, being compared against ten-year growth.

This is the same failure as the consumer confidence series later in this chapter: two correct numbers, describing the same reality, that are not comparable because they were constructed differently. Here it is worse in one way — nothing in the file says so. The column is labelled HPI and every value in it is right.

The fix is to compare like with like

gPOP is a ten-year change. So compare it to the ten-year change in the index, which is a percentage and is comparable across states.

And now be careful again

A correlation of +0.71 is strong enough that people will start saying causal things about it. It still does not license them.

Show the code 
_ = scatter(states["gPOP"], states["gHPI"],
            labels=states["Member"],
            title="Ten-year house price growth and population growth, US states",
            xlab="Population growth over the decade (%)",
            ylab="House price growth over the decade (%)")

The scatter shows what the bar chart cannot: real spread, and obvious exceptions. Maine and New Hampshire grew slowly and appreciated enormously. North Dakota grew faster than average and appreciated less than almost anyone. Compare this with the binned bar chart above, where those states disappeared into an average.

Binning averages away the disagreement. Both charts are honest; the scatter is more informative about how much confidence the pattern deserves.

And the strongest claim either one supports is still: states with faster population growth tended to see faster house price growth over the same decade. Not that population growth raised prices. People may be moving toward places where prices are rising, prices may be rising where people are moving, or a third factor — climate, taxes, industry, land-use rules — may drive both. The chart cannot tell you which, and neither can r = 0.71.

Over five years the relationship all but disappears — and the second table says why. After 2021 essentially every state’s prices rose, and they rose by similar amounts, so there is very little variation left across states for anything to explain.

A relationship that is strong over one window and absent over another has not been discovered. It has been found in a particular window, and the window belongs in the sentence: “over the decade to 2026.” Anyone who reports the number without the window is reporting something they cannot support.

Displaying quantitative data

A frequency distribution counts how many values fall in each range, or bin. Here is population growth across the 50 states.

The PCT column is the relative frequency: the count in each class divided by the total. There are 50 states, so a class holding 17 of them is \(17/50 = 34\%\).

The CUM_PCT column is the cumulative relative frequency, totalling as you move down. Its interpretation is direct: 44% of states grew by 5% or less over the decade, and 72% grew by 10% or less. Only one state exceeded 20%.

The mean sits above the median, by 0.83 points.

Fastest growing: ID at 22.8%

This is an asymmetrical distribution. A symmetrical one would be a mirror image on either side of the middle. Here most of the mass sits at the left and a thin tail stretches right — a few fast-growing states pull the mean above the median. That gap between mean and median is the skew, expressed as a number.

When a distribution is skewed, the mean and the median answer different questions. The median describes the typical state. The mean is pulled by the extremes. Reporting only one, without saying which, is how a summary becomes misleading without containing a false number.

The ogive

The ogive plots the cumulative relative frequency. A point on the line reads: this percent of observations fall at or below that value.

Show the code 
_ = ogive(states["gPOP"], bins=np.arange(-5, 30, 5),
          title="Cumulative distribution of state population growth",
          xlab="Population growth over the decade (%)")

The dotted line marks 50%. Where the curve crosses it is the median. A steep section means many observations packed into a narrow range; a flat section means few.

How many classes?

For grouped data we have to choose the number of classes. A common rule-of-thumb is to find \(k\) such that

\[2^k \geq n,\]

where \(n\) is the number of observations and \(k\) the number of classes. With 50 states, \(2^5 = 32\) and \(2^6 = 64\), so \(k = 6\).

The class width then follows from the range divided by the number of classes. This is a rule of thumb, not a law — and the choice has consequences, as the next section shows.

Rules when constructing grouped classes

  1. Equal-size classes. Unequal widths make patterns misleading.
  2. Mutually exclusive classes. Boundaries must not overlap.
  3. Include all data values. Fifty observations, fifty observations placed.
  4. Avoid empty classes. Too-narrow widths produce classes with no data.
  5. Avoid open-ended classes where possible — though sometimes an age class of “19+” beats fifty empty classes between 19 and 70.

Pareto charts

A Pareto chart is a bar chart sorted from most to least frequent, with a cumulative percentage line. Named for Vilfredo Pareto, it answers “how few categories account for how much?”

We use the Monmouth University National Poll #240, January 2021. Question 16 asked which statement came closest to the respondent’s view:

  1. Our system of government is basically sound and essentially needs no changes
  2. Our system is basically sound, but needs some improvement
  3. Our system is not too sound and needs many improvements
  4. Our system is not sound at all and needs significant changes
Show the code 
_ = pareto_chart(poll["q16_system_of_government"],
                 title="Views on the American system of government",
                 xlab="Response", ylab="Respondents")

Read the black line, not just the bars. The two most common answers account for over three-quarters of respondents. A summary that reported only the modal response would miss that the second category is nearly as large.

The honest version says what the base is: “Among the 551 respondents with a partner…”

The same thing, measured on two different scales

Scatter plots

A scatter plot displays two variables with no summarising at all. Every observation is shown. Most analysts draw one first when meeting a new data set.

Which variable goes on which axis

Before drawing one you have to decide which variable goes where, and the convention carries a claim.

The independent variable goes on the horizontal axis. It is the one you are treating as given. The dependent variable goes on the vertical axis. It is the one you believe responds:

\[\text{independent } (x) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{dependent } (y)\]

The arrow only points one way. The textbook’s example is television size and price: bigger televisions cost more, so size is \(x\) and price is \(y\) — and cutting the price of a television does not shrink it.

Notice what you have just done. Putting population growth on the horizontal axis and house price growth on the vertical is a decision about which one you think responds to which, and it is made before any statistics happen. Nothing in the data forces it. Someone who believes people move toward places where prices are already rising would draw the axes the other way round and get an identical-looking cloud with an identical correlation.

The axis convention is where regression begins — Chapter 10 will ask you to defend the choice rather than assume it. For now: make the choice deliberately, and say out loud that you made it.

What to check in any chart

  1. Does the axis start at zero? Required for bars; often wrong for lines.
  2. What is the base? Percent of what, and who was excluded.
  3. How many observations per bar or cell? A tall bar over four observations is noise.

  5. How old is the data? Check the last observation date.
  6. Does the caption claim more than the chart shows? Correlation drawn as a chart is still correlation.

A prompt that names every decision

Prompt

Build me an Excel workbook from state_hpi_ur_pop.csv.

Sheet 1, Data: the raw file, headers in row 1, nothing else.

Sheet 2, Distribution: a frequency distribution of the gPOP column. Use six classes of width 5 running from −5 to 25. Columns: class label, lower bound, upper bound, frequency, relative frequency, cumulative relative frequency, plus a total row.

Classes must be mutually exclusive: half-open, “at least the lower bound and strictly less than the upper”, so no state can fall in two classes.

Use live Excel formulas throughout — COUNTIFS referencing the bound cells, SUM for the total. No pasted values anywhere: I want to change a bound and watch the table and the charts update.

Sheet 3: a histogram of that distribution with no gaps between the bars — the data are continuous — with the axis starting at zero and both axes labelled; and an ogive of the cumulative relative frequency.

Finally, tell me which cells hold formulas and which hold constants.

Read that back and notice what it is. It is not a description of a spreadsheet. It is the whole of section 2.2: the number of classes, the class width, the boundary rule, the gap convention. You cannot write that prompt without knowing the statistics. That is the point, and it is why the prompt is the assessed artefact rather than the workbook.

## The role technology plays in statistics

Hardcore statisticians are perfectly happy to receive large quantities of data
in tabular formats. Likely this is because they know how to import the data
into their preferred statistical interface and can perform their own analysis
on it. Artificial intelligence tools are cutting the gap between what requires
a hardcore statistician and what an interested person can do.

Senior leaders often have the analytical ability to draw insight from raw
data. What they do not have is the time. Even traditional descriptive
statistics — **mean**, **median**, **quartiles**, **minimum** and **maximum** —
take more effort to interpret than many will spend. That is a particular
challenge when your goal is to *persuade*. We do not want our audience's eyes
to glaze over. We want to make the case from the data as elegantly as
possible.

This brings us to **data visualization**: presenting data so that its
important features are clear. This takes skill. There are many chart types to
choose from, and some are far better than others at showing a particular
pattern. AI is a genuine boon to producing them — and no help at all in
deciding which one is honest.

to" is.

```{python}
#| label: correlations
for col, label in [("UR", "unemployment rate"),
                   ("POPN", "population"),
                   ("gPOP", "population growth")]:
    print(f"Correlation of house price INDEX with {label:<20} "
          f"r = {states['HPI'].corr(states[col]):+.3f}")
```

A correlation of +0.30 is a real relationship and a weak one. That is a fine
place to stop — most analyses do. Let us not stop there, because there is
something wrong with the question.

### Look at which states are at the top

```{python}
#| label: hpi-extremes
top = states.nlargest(6, "HPI")[["Member", "HPI", "gPOP"]]
bottom = states.nsmallest(6, "HPI")[["Member", "HPI", "gPOP"]]

print(fast.to_string(index=False))
```

Massachusetts, New York and Rhode Island lead a chart supposedly about
housing, and they are among the slowest-growing states in the country.
Texas grew 15.4% and sits in the bottom half. Idaho grew 22.8% — the fastest
in the nation — and is nowhere near the top.

Something is wrong. Read the series documentation.

```{python}
#| label: hpi-provenance
print("Series     : {ST}STHPI, one per state")
print("Name       : All-Transactions House Price Index")
print("Source     : U.S. Federal Housing Finance Agency, via FRED")
print("Units      : Index, 1980 Q1 = 100")
print("Frequency  : Quarterly")
```

::: {.scarlet}
**Index, 1980 Q1 = 100.** Every state starts at 100 in 1980 by construction.
So a state's index *level* does not measure what a house costs there. It
measures **how much prices have risen since 1980** — cumulative appreciation
over forty-five years.

Massachusetts is at the top because Massachusetts has appreciated the most
since 1980, not because it is expensive today. The chart is not a housing
price chart at all. It is a chart of forty-five-year growth, being compared
against ten-year growth.
:::

This is the same failure as the consumer confidence series later in this
chapter: two correct numbers, describing the same reality, that are not
comparable because they were constructed differently. Here it is worse in one
way — nothing in the file says so. The column is labelled `HPI` and every
value in it is right.

### The fix is to compare like with like

`gPOP` is a **ten-year change**. So compare it to the **ten-year change in the
index**, which is a percentage and is comparable across states.

```{python}
#| label: hpi-change-by-gpop
_ = binned_bar(states["gPOP"], states["gHPI"], bins=6,
               title="Average ten-year house price growth by population growth",
               xlab="Population growth over the decade (%)",
               ylab="House price growth over the decade (%)")
```

```{python}
#| label: correlations-fixed
pairs = [("HPI", "gPOP", "house price INDEX LEVEL", "population growth"),
         ("gHPI", "gPOP", "house price CHANGE (10y)", "population growth"),
         ("gHPI", "UR", "house price CHANGE (10y)", "unemployment rate")]
for ycol, xcol, ylab, xlab in pairs:
    print(f"{ylab:<26} vs {xlab:<20} r = {states[ycol].corr(states[xcol]):+.3f}")
```

::: {.charcoal}
Same fifty states. Same source. Same download. The correlation went from
**+0.30 to +0.71** because the units changed, and nothing else did.

The unemployment rate is still flat against the new variable — r of about
zero. That matters: it says the improvement is not an artefact of making
everything correlate with everything. One pairing got much stronger and the
other did not move.
:::

### And now be careful again

A correlation of +0.71 is strong enough that people will start saying causal
things about it. It still does not license them.

```{python}
#| label: scatter-fixed
_ = scatter(states["gPOP"], states["gHPI"],
            labels=states["Member"],
            title="Ten-year house price growth and population growth, US states",
            xlab="Population growth over the decade (%)",
            ylab="House price growth over the decade (%)")
```

The scatter shows what the bar chart cannot: real spread, and obvious
exceptions. Maine and New Hampshire grew slowly and appreciated enormously.
North Dakota grew faster than average and appreciated less than almost anyone.
Compare this with the binned bar chart above, where those states disappeared
into an average.

::: {.charcoal}
**Binning averages away the disagreement.** Both charts are honest; the
scatter is more informative about how much confidence the pattern deserves.

And the strongest claim either one supports is still: *states with faster
population growth tended to see faster house price growth over the same
decade.* Not that population growth raised prices. People may be moving toward
places where prices are rising, prices may be rising where people are moving,
or a third factor — climate, taxes, industry, land-use rules — may drive both.
**The chart cannot tell you which, and neither can r = 0.71.**
:::

### The window is a choice too

One more thing to be suspicious of. Ten years was not handed to us; we picked
it. Here is the same comparison over five years.

```{python}
#| label: window-choice
print("Correlation of population growth with house price growth")
for col, window in [("gHPI", "ten years"), ("gHPI5", "five years")]:
    print(f"  measured over {window:<11} r = {states['gPOP'].corr(states[col]):+.3f}")

print()
print("Spread of house price growth across the fifty states:")
for col, window in [("gHPI", "ten years"), ("gHPI5", "five years")]:
    print(f"  over {window:<11} {states[col].min():6.1f}% to {states[col].max():6.1f}%   "
          f"(standard deviation {states[col].std():.1f})")
```

Over five years the relationship all but disappears — and the second table
says why. After 2021 essentially every state's prices rose, and they rose by
similar amounts, so there is very little variation left across states for
anything to explain.

::: {.scarlet}
A relationship that is strong over one window and absent over another has not
been discovered. It has been *found in a particular window*, and the window
belongs in the sentence: "over the decade to 2026." Anyone who reports the
number without the window is reporting something they cannot support.
:::

## Displaying quantitative data

A **frequency distribution** counts how many values fall in each range, or
*bin*. Here is population growth across the 50 states.

```{python}
#| label: freq-table
frequency_table(states["gPOP"], bins=np.arange(-5, 30, 5), cumulative=True)
```

The **PCT** column is the *relative frequency*: the count in each class divided
by the total. There are 50 states, so a class holding 17 of them is
$17/50 = 34\%$.

The **CUM_PCT** column is the *cumulative relative frequency*, totalling as
you move down. Its interpretation is direct: **44% of states grew by 5% or
less over the decade, and 72% grew by 10% or less.** Only one state exceeded
20%.

A **histogram** draws the same information.

```{python}
#| label: histogram
_ = histogram(states["gPOP"], bins=np.arange(-5, 30, 5),
              title="Distribution of state population growth",
              xlab="Population growth over the decade (%)")
```

```{python}
#| label: skew-check
g = states["gPOP"]
print(f"mean   {g.mean():6.2f}")
print(f"median {g.median():6.2f}")
print(f"The mean sits {'above' if g.mean() > g.median() else 'below'} the median, "
      f"by {abs(g.mean() - g.median()):.2f} points.")
print(f"\nFastest growing: {states.loc[g.idxmax(), 'Member']} at {g.max():.1f}%")
print(f"Slowest growing: {states.loc[g.idxmin(), 'Member']} at {g.min():.1f}%")
```

This is an **asymmetrical** distribution. A *symmetrical* one would be a mirror
image on either side of the middle. Here most of the mass sits at the left and
a thin tail stretches right — a few fast-growing states pull the mean above the
median. That gap between mean and median *is* the skew, expressed as a number.

::: {.charcoal}
When a distribution is skewed, the mean and the median answer different
questions. The median describes the typical state. The mean is pulled by the
extremes. Reporting only one, without saying which, is how a summary becomes
misleading without containing a false number.
:::

### The ogive

The **ogive** plots the cumulative relative frequency. A point on the line
reads: this percent of observations fall at or below that value.

```{python}
#| label: ogive
_ = ogive(states["gPOP"], bins=np.arange(-5, 30, 5),
          title="Cumulative distribution of state population growth",
          xlab="Population growth over the decade (%)")
```

The dotted line marks 50%. Where the curve crosses it is the median. A steep
section means many observations packed into a narrow range; a flat section
means few.

## How many classes?

For grouped data we have to choose the number of classes. A common
rule-of-thumb is to find $k$ such that

$$2^k \geq n,$$

where $n$ is the number of observations and $k$ the number of classes. With 50
states, $2^5 = 32$ and $2^6 = 64$, so $k = 6$.

The class width then follows from the range divided by the number of classes.
This is a *rule of thumb*, not a law — and the choice has consequences, as the
next section shows.

### Rules when constructing grouped classes

1. **Equal-size classes.** Unequal widths make patterns misleading.
2. **Mutually exclusive classes.** Boundaries must not overlap.
3. **Include all data values.** Fifty observations, fifty observations placed.
4. **Avoid empty classes.** Too-narrow widths produce classes with no data.
5. **Avoid open-ended classes** where possible — though sometimes an age class
   of "19+" beats fifty empty classes between 19 and 70.

## Pareto charts

A **Pareto chart** is a bar chart sorted from most to least frequent, with a
cumulative percentage line. Named for
[Vilfredo Pareto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilfredo_Pareto), it answers
"how few categories account for how much?"

We use the *Monmouth University National Poll #240*, January 2021. Question 16
asked which statement came closest to the respondent's view:

> 1. Our system of government is basically sound and essentially needs no changes
> 2. Our system is basically sound, but needs some improvement
> 3. Our system is not too sound and needs many improvements
> 4. Our system is not sound at all and needs significant changes

```{python}
#| label: pareto
_ = pareto_chart(poll["q16_system_of_government"],
                 title="Views on the American system of government",
                 xlab="Response", ylab="Respondents")
```

Read the black line, not just the bars. The two most common answers account for
over three-quarters of respondents. A summary that reported only the modal
response would miss that the second category is nearly as large.

## Stem and leaf display

[John Tukey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Tukey) originated the **stem
and leaf display** in the 1970s, when it could be built by hand. Its virtue is
that every individual observation stays visible, unlike a histogram.

```{python}
#| label: cci-two-files
for name, frame in [("consumer_confidence.csv", cci),
                    ("consumer_confidence_discontinued.csv", cci_old)]:
    print(f"{name}")
    print(f"   rows              {len(frame):,}")
    print(f"   first observation {frame.date.min().date()}")
    print(f"   last observation  {frame.date.max().date()}")
    print(f"   any missing       {int(frame.cci.isna().sum())}")
    print()
```

Two files. Same concept — US consumer confidence, monthly, from 1960. Both
downloaded without error. Both parsed cleanly. Neither has a missing value.
Every number in both is correct.

One of them stopped being published.

```{python}
#| label: cci-staleness
today = pd.Timestamp.today()
for name, frame in [("consumer_confidence.csv", cci),
                    ("consumer_confidence_discontinued.csv", cci_old)]:
    months = (today - frame.date.max()).days / 30.4
    verdict = "CURRENT" if months < 7 else f"STALE by {months:.0f} months"
    print(f"{name:<42} {verdict}")
```

::: {.scarlet}
Nothing in the file contents distinguishes a live series from a dead one. Row
counts look similar, values look plausible, no errors are raised.
**The last observation date is the only tell**, and checking it is the
verification almost nobody performs.

A retired series produces a perfectly reasonable-looking analysis of a world
that no longer exists.
:::

### The same thing, measured on two different scales

The two series do not merely resemble each other. Over the months they both
cover, they move together almost perfectly.

```{python}
#| label: cci-overlap
overlap = cci.merge(cci_old, on="date", suffixes=("_new", "_old")).dropna()

print(f"Overlapping months: {len(overlap):,}")
print(f"Correlation over the overlap: {overlap.cci_new.corr(overlap.cci_old):.3f}")
print()
print(f"{'':<12}{'current':>12}{'retired':>12}")
for label, a, b in [("minimum", cci.cci.min(), cci_old.cci.min()),
                    ("mean", cci.cci.mean(), cci_old.cci.mean()),
                    ("maximum", cci.cci.max(), cci_old.cci.max())]:
    print(f"{label:<12}{a:>12.2f}{b:>12.2f}")
print()
print("The same four months, read from each series:")
print(overlap.tail(4).to_string(index=False))
```

A correlation of 0.992 says these two series carry the same information. The
summary table says they are built differently. The retired series is a
*standardised index*, normalised to a long-run mean of exactly 100, never
straying more than about three points from it. The current series is a
**percentage balance** — roughly, the share of optimistic responses minus the
share of pessimistic ones — and it ranges from the high forties to above 120.

These are not the same measurement rescaled. They are two different ways of
turning the same survey into a number.

::: {.scarlet}
In October 2023 one of these series reads about 69 and the other about 98.
Same month, same concept, same underlying survey.

A student who saw 98 in an old report and 69 in a new one would conclude that
consumer confidence had collapsed. Nothing collapsed. **The normalisation
changed.**
:::

This is why *units* belong in the provenance record alongside the series name
and the date range. "Consumer confidence was 69" is not a fact until you say
which measure, constructed how. Two correct numbers describing the same
reality can differ by thirty points, and neither is wrong.

of this survey — below 2008, below 2020. Look at where the other low readings
cluster and what was happening then.

::: {.charcoal}
**A question to think about.** Consumer confidence measures how households
*feel* about the economy. Other indicators — employment, asset prices, output
— measure what is *happening* to it. These usually move together. When they
diverge sharply, one of three things is true: the sentiment measure is picking
up something the hard data has not registered yet, the hard data is capturing
something sentiment ignores, or the two are responding to different events
entirely.

Which chart would you build to tell them apart? What would you need on the
same axis, and over what period?
:::

The discontinued series is `CSCICP03USM665S`; the current one is
`USACSCICP02STSAM`. If you had built a forecast on the first one in early 2024
it would have looked fine, right up until someone asked what happened last

We use the current series from here on.

## Scatter plots

A **scatter plot** displays two variables with no summarising at all. Every
observation is shown. Most analysts draw one first when meeting a new data set.

### Which variable goes on which axis

Before drawing one you have to decide which variable goes where, and the
convention carries a claim.

The **independent variable** goes on the horizontal axis. It is the one you
are treating as given. The **dependent variable** goes on the vertical axis.
It is the one you believe responds:

$$\text{independent } (x) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{dependent } (y)$$

The arrow only points one way. The textbook's example is television size and
price: bigger televisions cost more, so size is $x$ and price is $y$ — and
cutting the price of a television does not shrink it.

::: {.charcoal}
Notice what you have just done. Putting population growth on the horizontal
axis and house price growth on the vertical is a *decision about which one you
think responds to which*, and it is made before any statistics happen. Nothing
in the data forces it. Someone who believes people move toward places where
prices are already rising would draw the axes the other way round and get an
identical-looking cloud with an identical correlation.

The axis convention is where regression begins — Chapter 10 will ask you to
defend the choice rather than assume it. For now: make the choice
deliberately, and say out loud that you made it.
:::

### The same states, two variables, one substitution

```{python}
#| label: scatter-pair
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4.6))
for ax, (col, label) in zip(axes, [("HPI", "House price INDEX LEVEL"),
                                   ("gHPI", "House price CHANGE over the decade (%)")]):
    ax.scatter(states["gPOP"], states[col], color=UNLV_SCARLET, alpha=0.8)
    ax.set_xlabel("Population growth over the decade (%)")
    ax.set_ylabel(label)
    ax.set_title(f"{label}\nr = {states[col].corr(states['gPOP']):+.3f}", fontsize=10)
    ax.grid(color="#e6e6e6")
    ax.set_axisbelow(True)
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()
```

The left panel is a cloud. The right panel is a relationship. The horizontal
axis is identical in both; only the vertical variable changed, and it changed
from an index level to a percentage change of that same index.

**A scatter plot cannot rescue a badly chosen variable — but it shows you
faster than anything else that you have chosen one.** This is why analysts draw
scatter plots first.

## Charts that mislead

Everything below is technically correct and still misleading. These are the
failures to watch for — in other people's charts, and in your own.

### Truncating the axis

```{python}
#| label: truncated-axis
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

subset = states.nlargest(6, "gHPI")
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4))

for ax, (lo, hi), label in zip(
        axes, [(0, None), (subset.gHPI.min() - 5, None)],
        ["Axis starts at zero", "Axis starts near the minimum"]):
    ax.bar(subset["Member"], subset["gHPI"], color=UNLV_SCARLET, alpha=0.85)
    ax.set_ylim(lo, hi)
    ax.set_title(label)
    ax.set_ylabel("House price growth over the decade (%)")
    ax.grid(axis="y", color="#e6e6e6")
    ax.set_axisbelow(True)
    ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False)

fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print(f"Highest: {subset.gHPI.max():.1f}%   "
      f"Lowest of these six: {subset.gHPI.min():.1f}%")
print(f"Actual gap between them: {subset.gHPI.max() - subset.gHPI.min():.1f} "
      f"percentage points")
```

Same six states, same six numbers. The right-hand chart makes the gap look
enormous. For bar charts, where length encodes the value, the axis should start
at zero.

### Letting bin width choose the story

```{python}
#| label: bin-width

    print(f"\n--- {k} bins ---")
    t = frequency_table(states["gPOP"], bins=k)
    print(t.to_string(index=False))
```

```{python}

for ax, k in zip(axes, [3, 6, 15]):
    ax.hist(states["gPOP"], bins=k, color=UNLV_SCARLET, alpha=0.85,
            edgecolor="white")
    ax.set_title(f"{k} bins")
    ax.set_xlabel("Population growth (%)")
    ax.grid(axis="y", color="#e6e6e6")
```

Three bins say the distribution is smooth. Fifteen bins say it is lumpy and
possibly bimodal. Same fifty states. **Nobody looking at one histogram can tell
you which bin width you tried first.**

### What to check in any chart

::: {.charcoal}
1. **Does the axis start at zero?** Required for bars; often wrong for lines.
2. **What is the base?** Percent *of what*, and who was excluded.
3. **How many observations per bar or cell?** A tall bar over four
   observations is noise.
4. **Would a different bin width tell a different story?**
5. **How old is the data?** Check the last observation date.
6. **Does the caption claim more than the chart shows?** Correlation drawn as
   a chart is still correlation.
:::

### A loose prompt and what it returns

Three things went wrong, and none of them is visible in the chart:

### The checklist

Every workbook in this course carries these six checks, as live formulas
rather than as advice. Run them on anything an assistant builds you — and on
anything you build yourself.

| # | Check | Rule it enforces |
|---|-------|------------------|
| 1 | Frequencies sum to $n$, taken from the source file | Include all data values |
| 2 | Relative frequencies sum to 1.00 | |
| 3 | Cumulative column ends at 1.00 | |
| 4 | Frequency cells hold **formulas**, not typed numbers | Change a bound, watch it move |
| 5 | No value falls in two classes | Mutually exclusive classes |
| 6 | No empty classes | |

Six checks, under a minute, and they are all rules from this chapter rather
than rules about software.
