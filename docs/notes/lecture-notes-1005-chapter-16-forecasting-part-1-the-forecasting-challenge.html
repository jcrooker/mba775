What Forecasting Is

Qualitative forecasting relies primarily on a knowledgeable person’s intuition and judgment to predict events. Quantitative forecasting uses historical data and mathematics.

Every business decision that takes time to carry out — hiring, ordering inventory, building a plant, scheduling staff for a convention weekend — is a bet on a number nobody knows yet. A forecast is the number you bet on, and a forecasting method is a rule for producing it that you could apply again next period. The second half of that sentence is what separates a method from a guess.

A time series is a group of data values, each associated with a specific point in time.

Donnelly’s running example is Brandywine Ford, a dealership that wants to forecast next quarter’s new-car sales from the last twelve quarters (Table 16.1). We will use it throughout, because you can check every number against the book. Then we will do the same thing to the CPI.

The Components of a Time Series

A trend component is present if a general upward or downward movement can be seen in the data.

A seasonal component is present if a consistent pattern can be associated with the calendar. Seasonal patterns repeat within a year, so you need data more often than annual to see them.

A cyclical component is movement that results from swings in the economy over several years — longer than a season.

The random component is whatever is left: movement that cannot be attributed to trend, season, or cycle. It is often called noise.

Look back at the car sales. The line drifts upward (trend), and every second and fourth quarter spikes (seasonal). Now look at the five challenge series. The CPI is almost pure trend. Initial claims are almost pure noise around a level. Visitor volume has a calendar in it — you can see March and October. The Nevada house price index trends, but not in a straight line, and it has the 2008 cycle in its long history.

Every method in Chapter 16 is a bet about which components are present. The methods in this note assume there is a level and some noise, and nothing else. When that is wrong — when there is a trend or a season — they fail in a predictable direction, and you will see it happen to the CPI below. Knowing how a method fails is most of knowing when to use it.

Smoothing Methods

The goal of a smoothing method is to average out the random movements in a time series, because they are unpredictable. If the forecast is an average of recent values, the ups and downs of the random component should cancel.

Smoothing works best when the series is fairly consistent over time: no significant trend, seasonal, or cyclical component. Chapter 16 covers four smoothing methods. We take the first two now and add a fifth that the book skips, because it is the one every other method has to beat.

The Naive Forecast

The naive forecast for period \(t\) is the actual value from period \(t-1\):

\[F_t = A_{t-1}.\]

“Next quarter will be like this quarter.” It sounds like an insult to forecasting, and for many series it is remarkably hard to beat — the CPI, as you are about to see, is one of them. A method that cannot outperform the naive forecast has not earned its complexity. Always compute it first.

The Simple Moving Average

A simple moving average (SMA) forecast averages the most recent \(p\) values of the series:

\[\text{SMA}(p, t) = \frac{A_{t-1} + A_{t-2} + \cdots + A_{t-p}}{p}.\]

Donnelly chooses \(p = 3\) and forecasts the first quarter of 2019 from the last three quarters of 2018:

Show the code 
a = CAR_SALES.values
show_math(r"\text{SMA}(p=3,\ 2019\text{Q1}) = \frac{%d + %d + %d}{3} = \frac{%d}{3} = %.1f \approx %d"
          % (a[-3], a[-2], a[-1], a[-3:].sum(), a[-3:].mean(), round(a[-3:].mean())))

\[\text{SMA}(p=3,\ 2019\text{Q1}) = \frac{151 + 141 + 142}{3} = \frac{434}{3} = 144.7 \approx 145\]

Now the question your boss asks: how good is that number likely to be? The only honest way to answer is to pretend you had used the method all along, and measure how it did. Apply the three-period average to the history: the first forecast it can make is for period 4 (it needs periods 1–3), and so on.

The mistake students make most: assigning the average of periods 1–3 to period 3. A forecast is for a period you do not yet know. The average of periods 1, 2 and 3 is the forecast for period 4. If you slide it back one row the method looks far better than it is, because it is being credited with knowing the value it was supposed to predict.

Measuring Accuracy: the Mean Absolute Deviation

The mean absolute deviation (MAD) is the average size of the forecasting error over the periods that have a forecast (Formula 16.1):

\[\text{MAD} = \frac{\sum |A_t - F_t|}{n},\]

where \(A_t\) is the actual value in period \(t\), \(F_t\) the forecast for period \(t\), and \(n\) the number of forecasting errors.

The absolute value matters. If the forecast is 10 too high one quarter and 10 too low the next, the average error is zero — which would be a lie about a method that missed by 10 every time. The average absolute error is 10.

Show the code 
t3 = error_table(CAR_SALES, sma3).dropna()
show_math(r"\text{MAD}_{\text{SMA}(3)} = \frac{%s}{%d} = \frac{%.1f}{%d} = %.1f"
          % (" + ".join(f"{v:.1f}" for v in t3["abs_error"]), len(t3),
             t3["abs_error"].sum(), len(t3), t3["abs_error"].mean()))

\[\text{MAD}_{\text{SMA}(3)} = \frac{19.3 + 8.3 + 43.0 + 13.7 + 13.3 + 23.7 + 18.0 + 0.3 + 3.7}{9} = \frac{143.3}{9} = 15.9\]

A three-period moving average would have missed by about 15.9 cars a quarter. Whether that is acceptable is a business question, not a statistical one. Whether it is better than the alternatives is a statistical one, and it is answered by computing the alternatives.

All else equal, choose the forecast with the lower MAD. Increasing \(p\) always smooths more — the forecast moves less from period to period and responds less to the latest data. Whether that lowers the error depends entirely on the series. Here it helps a lot, and it is worth asking why: a four-quarter average contains one of every season, so the seasonal spikes cancel inside the average. A three-quarter average never does. That is not a coincidence you should count on for a different series.

The dashed lines are smoother than the actual series: that is the point of the method, and also its weakness. Every peak is forecast late and low; every trough is forecast late and high. A moving average lags.

The Weighted Moving Average

A weighted moving average (WMA) applies weights to the \(p\) most recent values, conventionally giving the most recent value the largest weight. The denominator is the sum of the weights:

\[\text{WMA}(p=3, t) = \frac{3A_{t-1} + 2A_{t-2} + 1A_{t-3}}{3 + 2 + 1}.\]

The reasoning is that last month probably tells you more about next month than the month before it did. For Brandywine Ford with weights 3, 2, 1:

Show the code 
show_math(r"\text{WMA}(2019\text{Q1}) = \frac{(3)(%d) + (2)(%d) + (1)(%d)}{6} = \frac{%d}{6} = %.1f"
          % (a[-1], a[-2], a[-3], 3*a[-1] + 2*a[-2] + a[-3], (3*a[-1] + 2*a[-2] + a[-3]) / 6))

\[\text{WMA}(2019\text{Q1}) = \frac{(3)(142) + (2)(141) + (1)(151)}{6} = \frac{859}{6} = 143.2\]

Two More Yardsticks

MAD is the textbook’s measure and the one you should be able to compute by hand. Two companions are worth knowing because the challenge is scored on more than one series, and they answer slightly different questions.

The root mean squared error squares the errors before averaging, so one large miss counts for more than several small ones: \(\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\sum (A_t - F_t)^2 / n}\).

The mean absolute percentage error divides each error by the actual value, so it is unit-free: \(\text{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum |A_t - F_t| / |A_t|\). A MAPE of 2% on the CPI and a MAPE of 4% on jobless claims can be compared; a MAD of 1.2 index points and a MAD of 8,000 claims cannot.

Backtest: what the challenge will do to you

The table above judges each method on the whole sample at once. The challenge does something harsher: every period it asks for one forecast, then reveals the answer. The fair test of a method is therefore to replay that — for each of the last 24 months, fit the method on the data available at the time, forecast one month ahead, and record the miss.

# What Forecasting Is

::: {.blue}
**Qualitative forecasting** relies primarily on a knowledgeable person's
intuition and judgment to predict events. **Quantitative forecasting** uses
historical data and mathematics.
:::

Every business decision that takes time to carry out — hiring, ordering
inventory, building a plant, scheduling staff for a convention weekend — is a
bet on a number nobody knows yet. A forecast is the number you bet on, and a
forecasting *method* is a rule for producing it that you could apply again next
period. The second half of that sentence is what separates a method from a
guess.

::: {.blue}
A **time series** is a group of data values, each associated with a specific
point in time.
:::

Donnelly's running example is Brandywine Ford, a dealership that wants to
forecast next quarter's new-car sales from the last twelve quarters (Table

## The Components of a Time Series

::: {.blue}
A **trend component** is present if a general upward or downward movement can
be seen in the data.

A **seasonal component** is present if a consistent pattern can be associated
with the calendar. Seasonal patterns repeat within a year, so you need data
more often than annual to see them.

A **cyclical component** is movement that results from swings in the economy
over several years — longer than a season.

The **random component** is whatever is left: movement that cannot be
attributed to trend, season, or cycle. It is often called *noise*.
:::

Look back at the car sales. The line drifts upward (trend), and every second
and fourth quarter spikes (seasonal). Now look at the five challenge series.
The CPI is almost pure trend. Initial claims are almost pure noise around a
level. Visitor volume has a calendar in it — you can see March and October. The
Nevada house price index trends, but not in a straight line, and it has the
2008 cycle in its long history.

::: {.charcoal}
Every method in Chapter 16 is a bet about which components are present. The
methods in *this* note assume there is a level and some noise, and nothing
else. When that is wrong — when there is a trend or a season — they fail in a
predictable direction, and you will see it happen to the CPI below. Knowing
*how* a method fails is most of knowing when to use it.
:::

# Smoothing Methods

::: {.blue}
The goal of a **smoothing method** is to average out the random movements in
a time series, because they are unpredictable. If the forecast is an average
of recent values, the ups and downs of the random component should cancel.
:::

Smoothing works best when the series is fairly consistent over time: no
significant trend, seasonal, or cyclical component. Chapter 16 covers four
smoothing methods. We take the first two now and add a fifth that the book
skips, because it is the one every other method has to beat.

## The Naive Forecast

::: {.blue}
The **naive forecast** for period $t$ is the actual value from period $t-1$:

$$F_t = A_{t-1}.$$
:::

"Next quarter will be like this quarter." It sounds like an insult to
forecasting, and for many series it is remarkably hard to beat — the CPI, as
you are about to see, is one of them. A method that cannot outperform the naive
forecast has not earned its complexity. Always compute it first.

```{python}
#| label: naive-cars
nv = naive_forecast(CAR_SALES)
error_table(CAR_SALES, nv).round(1)[["actual", "forecast", "error", "abs_error"]]
```

The naive forecast for 2019Q1 is `{python} num(nv.iloc[-1], 0)` cars — the
2018Q4 figure. Notice the pattern in the error column: it alternates sign,
because the series alternates high and low quarters and the naive forecast is
always one step behind. That is the seasonal component punishing a method that
does not know about seasons.

## The Simple Moving Average

::: {.blue}
A **simple moving average** (SMA) forecast averages the most recent $p$ values
of the series:

$$\text{SMA}(p, t) = \frac{A_{t-1} + A_{t-2} + \cdots + A_{t-p}}{p}.$$
:::

Donnelly chooses $p = 3$ and forecasts the first quarter of 2019 from the last
three quarters of 2018:

```{python}
#| label: sma3-math
#| output: asis
a = CAR_SALES.values
show_math(r"\text{SMA}(p=3,\ 2019\text{Q1}) = \frac{%d + %d + %d}{3} = \frac{%d}{3} = %.1f \approx %d"
          % (a[-3], a[-2], a[-1], a[-3:].sum(), a[-3:].mean(), round(a[-3:].mean())))
```

Now the question your boss asks: *how good is that number likely to be?* The
only honest way to answer is to pretend you had used the method all along, and
measure how it did. Apply the three-period average to the history: the first
forecast it can make is for period 4 (it needs periods 1–3), and so on.

```{python}
#| label: sma3-table
sma3 = sma_forecast(CAR_SALES, 3)
error_table(CAR_SALES, sma3).round(1)[["actual", "forecast", "error", "abs_error"]]
```

::: {.scarlet}
**The mistake students make most:** assigning the average of periods 1–3 to
period 3. A forecast is for a period you do not yet know. The average of
periods 1, 2 and 3 is the forecast *for period 4*. If you slide it back one row
the method looks far better than it is, because it is being credited with
knowing the value it was supposed to predict.
:::

## Measuring Accuracy: the Mean Absolute Deviation

::: {.blue}
The **mean absolute deviation** (MAD) is the average size of the forecasting
error over the periods that have a forecast (Formula 16.1):

$$\text{MAD} = \frac{\sum |A_t - F_t|}{n},$$

where $A_t$ is the actual value in period $t$, $F_t$ the forecast for period
$t$, and $n$ the number of forecasting errors.
:::

The absolute value matters. If the forecast is 10 too high one quarter and 10
too low the next, the average *error* is zero — which would be a lie about a
method that missed by 10 every time. The average *absolute* error is 10.

```{python}
#| label: sma3-mad
#| output: asis
t3 = error_table(CAR_SALES, sma3).dropna()
show_math(r"\text{MAD}_{\text{SMA}(3)} = \frac{%s}{%d} = \frac{%.1f}{%d} = %.1f"
          % (" + ".join(f"{v:.1f}" for v in t3["abs_error"]), len(t3),
             t3["abs_error"].sum(), len(t3), t3["abs_error"].mean()))
```

A three-period moving average would have missed by about `{python} num(mad(CAR_SALES, sma3))`
cars a quarter. Whether that is acceptable is a business question, not a
statistical one. Whether it is *better than the alternatives* is a statistical
one, and it is answered by computing the alternatives.

### What if $p = 4$?

```{python}

sma4 = sma_forecast(CAR_SALES, 4)
error_table(CAR_SALES, sma4).round(1)[["actual", "forecast", "error", "abs_error"]]
```

The four-period forecast for 2019Q1 is `{python} num(sma4.iloc[-1])` with a MAD of
`{python} num(mad(CAR_SALES, sma4))`, against `{python} num(mad(CAR_SALES, sma3))`
for three periods.

::: {.charcoal}
**All else equal, choose the forecast with the lower MAD.** Increasing $p$
always smooths *more* — the forecast moves less from period to period and
responds less to the latest data. Whether that lowers the error depends
entirely on the series. Here it helps a lot, and it is worth asking why: a
four-quarter average contains one of every season, so the seasonal spikes
cancel inside the average. A three-quarter average never does. That is not a
coincidence you should count on for a different series.
:::

The row for $p = 1$ is the naive forecast. Read across: the MAD does not fall
smoothly as $p$ grows. It jumps around, and the even windows win — which is the
seasonal pattern again. (The $p = 6$ MAD averages only six errors; do not read
too much into it.)

The dashed lines are smoother than the actual series: that is the point of

## The Weighted Moving Average

::: {.blue}
A **weighted moving average** (WMA) applies weights to the $p$ most recent
values, conventionally giving the most recent value the largest weight. The
denominator is the sum of the weights:

$$\text{WMA}(p=3, t) = \frac{3A_{t-1} + 2A_{t-2} + 1A_{t-3}}{3 + 2 + 1}.$$
:::

The reasoning is that last month probably tells you more about next month than
the month before it did. For Brandywine Ford with weights 3, 2, 1:

```{python}
#| label: wma-math
#| output: asis
show_math(r"\text{WMA}(2019\text{Q1}) = \frac{(3)(%d) + (2)(%d) + (1)(%d)}{6} = \frac{%d}{6} = %.1f"
          % (a[-1], a[-2], a[-3], 3*a[-1] + 2*a[-2] + a[-3], (3*a[-1] + 2*a[-2] + a[-3]) / 6))
```

```{python}
#| label: wma-table
wma = wma_forecast(CAR_SALES, [1, 2, 3])     # weights run oldest -> newest
error_table(CAR_SALES, wma).round(1)[["actual", "forecast", "error", "abs_error"]]
```

MAD `{python} num(mad(CAR_SALES, wma))`: a little better than the three-period
SMA (`{python} num(mad(CAR_SALES, sma3))`), not as good as the four-period SMA
(`{python} num(mad(CAR_SALES, sma4))`). The SMA is a WMA with equal weights.
The choice of weights is arbitrary and found by trial and error — which is a
polite way of saying that if you try enough weights you will find some that fit
the past well, and there is no guarantee they fit the future.

## Two More Yardsticks

MAD is the textbook's measure and the one you should be able to compute by
hand. Two companions are worth knowing because the challenge is scored on more
than one series, and they answer slightly different questions.

::: {.blue}
The **root mean squared error** squares the errors before averaging, so one
large miss counts for more than several small ones:
$\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\sum (A_t - F_t)^2 / n}$.

The **mean absolute percentage error** divides each error by the actual value,
so it is unit-free: $\text{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum |A_t - F_t| / |A_t|$.
A MAPE of 2% on the CPI and a MAPE of 4% on jobless claims can be compared; a
MAD of 1.2 index points and a MAD of 8,000 claims cannot.
:::

```{python}
#| label: summary-cars
accuracy(CAR_SALES, {"naive": nv, "SMA p=3": sma3, "SMA p=4": sma4,
                     "WMA 3-2-1": wma}).round(2)
```

The last column matters. Each method is judged on a different number of
errors, because each needs a different number of periods to get started. With
twelve observations that is a real limitation; with twelve *years* of monthly
data it is not.

# The Challenge Series: the CPI

Now a series you will actually forecast. The Consumer Price Index measures the
price of a fixed basket of goods relative to 1982–84 = 100. It is monthly,
seasonally adjusted, and revised rarely.

## Look before you forecast

Chapter 1 taught you to check a data set before analysing it. That does not
stop being true because the next step is a forecast.

```{python}
#| label: cpi-check
pd.set_option("display.float_format", lambda v: f"{v:,.3f}")
checks = pd.Series({
    "observations": len(cpi_raw),
    "first": str(cpi_raw.index.min().date()),
    "last": str(cpi_raw.index.max().date()),
    "type": str(cpi_raw.dtype),
    "missing": int(cpi_raw.isna().sum()),
    "missing at": ", ".join(str(d.date()) for d in cpi_raw.index[cpi_raw.isna()]) or "—",
})
checks.to_frame("CPIAUCSL")
```

There is a hole. The Bureau of Labor Statistics did not publish a CPI for
October 2025, because the price survey was not conducted during the federal
shutdown. FRED shows a `.`, and a `.` is not a zero. A moving average needs
consecutive months, so we must decide what to do about it, and the decision is
a modelling choice that belongs in your write-up, not a cleaning step that
happens silently. Here we draw a straight line between September and November
and say so.

```{python}
#| label: cpi-fill
cpi = fill_gaps(cpi_raw, "interpolate")
recent = cpi.loc["2022-01-01":]
```

The second decision is the window. The CPI runs back to 1947 on FRED (1970 in
our file). The 1970s are real data, but they are not the regime you are
forecasting. We use January 2022 onward — `{python} str(len(recent))` months.
How far back to look is a choice; a different window gives a different
forecast, and you should know by how much.

```{python}
#| label: cpi-plot
_ = time_series(recent.index, recent.values,
                title="CPI, all urban consumers, since 2022", ylab="Index, 1982–84 = 100")
```

## Forecast next month

```{python}
#| label: cpi-methods
next_month = (recent.index[-1] + pd.offsets.MonthBegin(1)).strftime("%B %Y")
cpi_methods = {
    "naive":     naive_forecast(recent),
    "SMA p=2":   sma_forecast(recent, 2),
    "SMA p=3":   sma_forecast(recent, 3),
    "SMA p=6":   sma_forecast(recent, 6),
    "WMA 1-2-3": wma_forecast(recent, [1, 2, 3]),
}
cpi_summary = accuracy(recent, cpi_methods)
cpi_summary.round(3)
```

```{python}
#| label: cpi-best
#| include: false
cpi_best = cpi_summary["MAD"].idxmin()
cpi_worst = cpi_summary["MAD"].idxmax()
```

Forecasts for `{python} next_month` in the first column; the methods are then
judged on every month since 2022 that has a forecast. The smallest MAD belongs
to **`{python} cpi_best`**, and the ranking runs almost exactly opposite to the
car-sales ranking: the widest window is the worst.

::: {.charcoal}
Why? The CPI has a trend. It rises in most months, so *every average of past
values sits below the current level*, and the wider the window, the further
below. A six-month average is forecasting where the CPI was three months ago.
Donnelly says it on p. 770: when a series has a trend or a season, smoothing
methods "lag behind the data, resulting in increased forecasting errors." You
have just watched it happen. The naive forecast lags by only one month, which
is why it wins — for now. After Exam 1 we will meet methods that do not lag at
all.
:::

```{python}
#| label: cpi-forecast-plot
_ = forecast_plot(recent, {"naive": cpi_methods["naive"], "SMA p=6": cpi_methods["SMA p=6"]},
                  title="CPI: the six-month average lags a trending series",
                  ylab="Index", last=24)
```

## Backtest: what the challenge will do to you

The table above judges each method on the whole sample at once. The challenge
does something harsher: every period it asks for one forecast, then reveals the
answer. The fair test of a method is therefore to *replay* that — for each of
the last 24 months, fit the method on the data available at the time, forecast
one month ahead, and record the miss.

```{python}
#| label: cpi-backtest
bt = backtest_summary(recent, {
    "naive":     (naive_forecast, {}),
    "SMA p=2":   (sma_forecast, {"p": 2}),
    "SMA p=3":   (sma_forecast, {"p": 3}),
    "SMA p=6":   (sma_forecast, {"p": 6}),
    "WMA 1-2-3": (wma_forecast, {"weights": [1, 2, 3]}),
}, last=24)
bt.round(3)
```

Same verdict, and now it is a verdict you can defend: had you submitted the
`{python} bt["MAD"].idxmin()` forecast every month for two years, you would
have missed by `{python} num(bt["MAD"].min(), 2)` index points on average.

```{python}
#| label: cpi-backtest-detail
backtest(recent, naive_forecast, last=6).round(3)
```
