Objectives

  • Forecast with trend projection — a regression of the series on the period number — and compute its MAD.
  • Test the residuals for autocorrelation with the Durbin–Watson statistic, and say what a positive result does to your regression.
  • Forecast with multiplicative decomposition: seasonal factors, a deseasonalised trend, and their product.
  • Represent seasonality with dummy variables in a multiple regression.
  • Choose among all of Chapter 16’s methods for each challenge series, and defend the choice with a backtest.

You now have Chapters 14 and 15. That changes what you can do with a time series: instead of averaging its past, you can model it — put the period number on the right-hand side and let regression find the trend; put the season on the right-hand side and let it find the calendar. The methods before Exam 1 assumed a level. The methods before Exam 2 added a drifting level. These add a trend and a season, which is everything in Donnelly’s list of components except the cycle.

The smoothing methods were atheoretic: they had nothing to say about why a series moves. Regression is at least a hypothesis — sales grow with time; March is bigger than February — and a hypothesis can be wrong in ways you can check. That is the point of the Durbin–Watson test below.

Trend Projection

Trend projection fits a straight line to the series against the period number and extends it into the future (Formula 16.6):

\[\hat{y}_t = b_0 + b_1 t,\]

with \(b_1\) and \(b_0\) the ordinary least-squares slope and intercept from Chapter 14 (Formulas 16.7 and 16.8), \(t = 1, 2, \ldots, n\).

For Brandywine Ford, \(t\) runs 1 to 12 and the regression is simple enough to do by hand: \(\sum t = 78\), \(\sum y = 1{,}599\), \(\sum ty = 10{,}783\), \(\sum t^2 = 650\).

Checking for Autocorrelation

Regression assumes the residuals are independent of one another. In a time series that assumption fails more often than it holds, because whatever pushed this quarter’s sales above the line is usually still pushing next quarter.

Autocorrelation is the condition in which the residuals of a regression are not independent of one another. The Durbin–Watson statistic tests for it (Formula 16.10):

\[d = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^{n}(e_t - e_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} e_t^2}.\]

\(d\) ranges from 0 to 4. A value near 2 means no autocorrelation; near 0, perfect positive autocorrelation (residuals in long runs of the same sign); near 4, perfect negative autocorrelation (residuals that alternate).

If consecutive residuals are close to each other, the numerator is small and \(d\) falls toward zero. Donnelly’s Table 16.10 makes the point with five residuals all equal to 20: every difference is zero, \(d = 0\), and knowing one residual tells you the next exactly. Negative autocorrelation is rare in business data, so the test is one-tailed:

\[H_0: \text{no positive autocorrelation} \qquad H_1: \text{positive autocorrelation is present}.\]

The test needs at least 15 observations, so the book switches to a 16-year series of union membership as a share of the labour force (Table 16.12):

The critical values come from the Durbin–Watson table (Appendix A, Table 9), indexed by \(n\) and by \(k\), the number of independent variables. For \(n = 16\), \(k = 1\), \(\alpha = 0.05\): \(d_L\) = 1.1 and \(d_U\) = 1.37. The decision rule has three regions:

Result Conclusion
\(d < d_L\) Reject \(H_0\): positive autocorrelation is present
\(d_L \le d \le d_U\) Inconclusive
\(d > d_U\) Fail to reject \(H_0\): no evidence of positive autocorrelation

Here \(d\) = 1.005 \(< d_L\), so we reject H0: positive autocorrelation is present. (The book gets 1.0085 from residuals rounded to three decimals; the difference is rounding, not disagreement.)

What does a positive result cost you? The point estimates \(b_0\) and \(b_1\) are still unbiased; the standard errors are wrong, usually too small, so the t-test on the slope claims more certainty than it has. The trend line may still forecast tolerably. But autocorrelated residuals are also a message: there is information in the residuals a better model could use. If the last three residuals were negative, the next one probably is too — and the trend projection ignores that, while the naive forecast, without knowing it, uses it. The fix Donnelly suggests is to add an explanatory variable. Another, which you will meet if you take a time-series course, is to model the residuals themselves.

Forecasting with Seasonality

Look at the car sales again: quarters 2 and 4 spike, every year. Every method so far has treated that as noise. It is not noise; it is the calendar, and a calendar can be forecast.

Multiplicative Decomposition

The multiplicative decomposition model writes each observation as the product of its components (Formula 16.11):

\[y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t,\]

trend, seasonal and random. (The cycle is left out: three years of data are not enough to see one.) The forecast is \(F_t = T_t \times S_t\) (Formula 16.14) — the random component is, by definition, unforecastable.

Four steps: identify the seasonal factors, deseasonalise the data, fit a trend to the deseasonalised data, and multiply.

Step 1: the centered moving average

Average four consecutive quarters. Because four is even, the average of periods 1–4 belongs to period 2.5, halfway between 2 and 3; so average the period-2.5 and period-3.5 averages to get a value centred on period 3. That is the centered moving average, and it contains one of every season, so the seasonal component has been averaged out of it — and so, roughly, has the random component. What is left is the trend: \(\text{CMA}_t \approx T_t\).

Step 2: the ratio to moving average

Divide each actual by its CMA (Formula 16.12): \(\text{RMA}_t = y_t / \text{CMA}_t = S_t \times R_t\). The ratio is what the season and the noise did to that period.

Seasonality with Dummy Variables

Chapter 15 gave you dummy variables. A season is a qualitative variable with four categories, so it needs three dummies (one category is the base):

\[F_t = b_0 + b_1 t + b_2\,\text{SD}_2 + b_3\,\text{SD}_3 + b_4\,\text{SD}_4,\]

where \(\text{SD}_q = 1\) in quarter \(q\) and 0 otherwise, and the first quarter is the base. Each \(b_q\) is how many more cars quarter \(q\) sells than the first quarter at the same point on the trend.

The book runs best-subsets regression and keeps the period and two of the three dummies — the third-quarter dummy is dropped, because the third quarter is not distinguishable from the first. Dummies are named here by quarter, so the book’s SD\(_1\) and SD\(_3\) are our SD\(_2\) and SD\(_4\).

# Checking for Autocorrelation

Regression assumes the residuals are independent of one another. In a time
series that assumption fails more often than it holds, because whatever
pushed this quarter's sales above the line is usually still pushing next
quarter.

::: {.blue}
**Autocorrelation** is the condition in which the residuals of a regression
are not independent of one another. The **Durbin–Watson statistic** tests for
it (Formula 16.10):

$$d = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^{n}(e_t - e_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} e_t^2}.$$

$d$ ranges from 0 to 4. A value near 2 means no autocorrelation; near 0,
perfect positive autocorrelation (residuals in long runs of the same sign);
near 4, perfect negative autocorrelation (residuals that alternate).
:::

If consecutive residuals are close to each other, the numerator is small and
$d$ falls toward zero. Donnelly's Table 16.10 makes the point with five
residuals all equal to 20: every difference is zero, $d = 0$, and knowing
one residual tells you the next exactly. Negative autocorrelation is rare in
business data, so the test is one-tailed:

$$H_0: \text{no positive autocorrelation} \qquad H_1: \text{positive autocorrelation is present}.$$

The test needs at least 15 observations, so the book switches to a 16-year
series of union membership as a share of the labour force (Table 16.12):

```{python}
#| label: union
pd.set_option("display.float_format", lambda v: f"{v:,.4f}")
union = pd.Series([13.0, 12.6, 12.5, 12.1, 12.2, 12.5, 12.4, 12.5,
                   11.9, 11.8, 11.3, 11.3, 11.1, 11.1, 10.7, 10.7],
                  index=range(2002, 2018), name="pct_union")
u = trend_projection(union)
res = u["table"]["residual"]
pieces = pd.DataFrame({"residual": res, "prior": res.shift(1),
                       "(e_t - e_t-1)^2": (res - res.shift(1)) ** 2, "e_t^2": res ** 2})
pieces.round(4)
```

```{python}
#| label: dw-math
#| output: asis
dwr = dw_test(res)
show_math(r"\hat{y}_t = %.4f %+.4f\,t \qquad d = \frac{%.4f}{%.4f} = %.4f"
          % (u["b0"], u["b1"], pieces["(e_t - e_t-1)^2"].sum(), pieces["e_t^2"].sum(), dwr["d"]))
```

The critical values come from the Durbin–Watson table (Appendix A, Table 9),
indexed by $n$ and by $k$, the number of independent variables. For $n = 16$,
$k = 1$, $\alpha = 0.05$: $d_L$ = `{python} str(dwr["d_L"])` and
$d_U$ = `{python} str(dwr["d_U"])`. The decision rule has three regions:

| Result | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| $d < d_L$ | Reject $H_0$: positive autocorrelation is present |
| $d_L \le d \le d_U$ | Inconclusive |
| $d > d_U$ | Fail to reject $H_0$: no evidence of positive autocorrelation |

Here $d$ = `{python} num(dwr["d"], 3)` $< d_L$, so we **`{python} dwr["verdict"]`**.
(The book gets 1.0085 from residuals rounded to three decimals; the
difference is rounding, not disagreement.)

::: {.charcoal}
What does a positive result cost you? The point estimates $b_0$ and $b_1$ are
still unbiased; the *standard errors* are wrong, usually too small, so the
t-test on the slope claims more certainty than it has. The trend line may
still forecast tolerably. But autocorrelated residuals are also a message:
there is information in the residuals a better model could use. If the last
three residuals were negative, the next one probably is too — and the trend
projection ignores that, while the naive forecast, without knowing it, uses
it. The fix Donnelly suggests is to add an explanatory variable. Another,
which you will meet if you take a time-series course, is to model the
residuals themselves.
:::

# Forecasting with Seasonality

Look at the car sales again: quarters 2 and 4 spike, every year. Every method
so far has treated that as noise. It is not noise; it is the calendar, and a
calendar can be forecast.

## Multiplicative Decomposition

::: {.blue}
The **multiplicative decomposition** model writes each observation as the
product of its components (Formula 16.11):

$$y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t,$$

trend, seasonal and random. (The cycle is left out: three years of data are
not enough to see one.) The forecast is $F_t = T_t \times S_t$ (Formula
16.14) — the random component is, by definition, unforecastable.
:::

Four steps: identify the seasonal factors, deseasonalise the data, fit a trend
to the deseasonalised data, and multiply.

### Step 1: the centered moving average

Average four consecutive quarters. Because four is even, the average of
periods 1–4 belongs to period 2.5, halfway between 2 and 3; so average the
period-2.5 and period-3.5 averages to get a value centred *on* period 3. That
is the **centered moving average**, and it contains one of every season, so
the seasonal component has been averaged out of it — and so, roughly, has the
random component. What is left is the trend: $\text{CMA}_t \approx T_t$.

### Step 2: the ratio to moving average

Divide each actual by its CMA (Formula 16.12): $\text{RMA}_t = y_t /
\text{CMA}_t = S_t \times R_t$. The ratio is what the season and the noise
did to that period.

```{python}
#| label: decomp-steps

dec["steps"].round(4)
```

Read period 3: CMA `{python} num(dec["steps"].loc["2016Q3", "CMA"], 3)`, actual
108, ratio `{python} num(dec["steps"].loc["2016Q3", "RMA"], 4)` — the third
quarter of 2016 ran about 12% below trend. The first two and last two periods
have no CMA: a centred average needs data on both sides.

### Step 3: seasonal factors, then normalise them

Each quarter appears twice in the ratios. Average them, and the random
component — which is what differs between the two years — cancels, leaving
the seasonal factor. Then rescale so the four factors sum to exactly 4,
because a "season" that averages more than 100% of an average quarter is not
a season.

multiplying each by 4 / `{python} num(dec["factors"].attrs["sum_SF"], 4)`
gives the normalised factors. Quarter 2 runs about
`{python} num((dec["factors"].loc[2, "NSF"] - 1) * 100, 0)`% above an
average quarter; quarters 1 and 3 run about 10% below.

### Step 4: deseasonalise, fit the trend, multiply

Divide each observation by its quarter's factor (Formula 16.13) and the
seasonal spikes disappear. Regress what is left on the period number; that is
the trend component. The forecast is the projected trend times the factor for
the quarter being forecast.

## Seasonality with Dummy Variables

Chapter 15 gave you dummy variables. A season is a qualitative variable with
four categories, so it needs three dummies (one category is the base):

::: {.blue}
$$F_t = b_0 + b_1 t + b_2\,\text{SD}_2 + b_3\,\text{SD}_3 + b_4\,\text{SD}_4,$$

where $\text{SD}_q = 1$ in quarter $q$ and 0 otherwise, and the first quarter
is the base. Each $b_q$ is how many more cars quarter $q$ sells than the
first quarter *at the same point on the trend*.
:::

The book runs best-subsets regression and keeps the period and two of the
three dummies — the third-quarter dummy is dropped, because the third quarter
is not distinguishable from the first. Dummies are named here by quarter, so
the book's SD$_1$ and SD$_3$ are our SD$_2$ and SD$_4$.

every season — is next. Complexity is not what wins. Matching the method to
the components that are actually in the series is what wins, and a method
that models a component the series does not have loses ground for it. The
next section shows both halves of that.
:::

`{python} str(len(recent))` quarters from 2015, and the trend line is $T_t$ = `{python} num(th["b0"], 1)` + `{python} num(th["b1"], 2)` $t$

shape. The naive forecast, which knows only where the index *is*, has the
last residual built in; the trend-adjusted smoothing from Part 2 tracks the
slope as it changes. Check both against the line in your backtest before you
