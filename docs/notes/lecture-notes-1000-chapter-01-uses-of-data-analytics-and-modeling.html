Our AI-assisted analytics workflow

This semester we will use Claude as an analytical collaborator. Claude can help us write, run, explain, and debug computer code, and it can execute that code and show us the result. It can often complete calculations much faster than we can complete them manually. However, speed does not guarantee that the data, method, or interpretation is appropriate.

Our work will follow this sequence:

  1. Define the business question. What decision or claim will the analysis support?
  2. Specify the data. What does each observation represent? Which variables, dates, units, and populations are relevant?
  3. Direct Claude. Give Claude a precise task, relevant constraints, and a required output.
  4. Run reproducible code. Preserve the code and record where the data came from.
  5. Verify the work. Inspect the data, calculations, assumptions, and output instead of accepting them automatically.
  6. Interpret and communicate. Translate results into a defensible business conclusion and disclose important limitations.

Claude is a capable junior analyst, not the decision maker. You are responsible for the analytical specification, verification, interpretation, and final recommendation.

The one rule that governs everything else

Every number you report must come from code that was actually executed. Never take a figure from an assistant’s prose. If you cannot point to the cell that produced a number, the number does not go in your submission.

An assistant that writes a paragraph containing plausible-looking values has not performed an analysis. An assistant that runs a script and shows you its output has. The difference is invisible in the writing and decisive in the result.

Organizing an analysis

You will do most of your work this semester in Claude’s code-execution environment: you upload a data file and a starting script, and Claude runs Python on them and shows you the output. There is nothing to install.

Even in that setting, keep the same discipline a full project folder would impose:

Inputs      -- the data file exactly as it was downloaded, unmodified
Program     -- the script that transforms inputs into results
Outputs     -- tables and figures, always regenerated by running the program
Provenance  -- a written record of where the data came from

We do not silently overwrite or hand-edit source data. If a number needs to change, the program changes, and we run it again. This is what makes an analysis reproducible: someone else can start from your inputs and your program and arrive at your outputs.

Some uses of Data Analytics and models

Marketing research

Taste tests have been a staple of marketing research. Kellogg researchers, for example, would provide consumers with Cheez-It crackers and Nabisco’s Cheese Nips. The researchers would ask consumers to rate the product. By repeating this research with thousands of consumers, the researchers would establish trends in preferences across the market. This research would shape product development. If you avoid the researchers armed with clipboards in the mall (and everyone like you with similar tastes), your preferences will not be reflected in the next evolution of snack food!

It is actually much more difficult to elude the market researchers today. Many websites you visit are monitoring clickthrough rates (CTR). We will say much more about marketing research in this class.

Advertising

The television industry for decades has used Nielsen Media Research. These ratings are based on data collected from a panel of US households. The Nielsen researchers sample from these households to measure viewing habits and preferences for the entire country. The design of these samples is critical in ensuring that the results from the survey responses reflect the nation as a whole. Sampling strategies and statistical inferences are a topic for us this semester.

Operations

Quality control is an important consideration for today’s large scale producers. As many production processes are automated, sampling from the large quantities of goods produced in each period is a technique to assess production quality. As no production process is perfect, quality control engineers establish benchmarks for inherent variation in, say, product dimensions. Six Sigma is a methodology intent on reducing this inherent variation to a small or minuscule level. Our quality control engineers identify problems in the production processes when they find production deformities outside the defined benchmarks. Statistical variation is an important topic for our investigations.

Finance

Many economic events cannot be perfectly anticipated. This is particularly troublesome given the experience of some economic shocks can have disruptive change on a business. Moreover, many of these events cannot be avoided. For example, if the global economy enters a recession, pro cyclical businesses would more than likely experience sharp reductions in consumer demand. Decisions regarding launching a new product line or new manufacturing facility could have disastrous implications for a business if these launches coincided with a recession. Finance professionals use probability as a tool to weight potential outcomes across all potential business climate scenarios. As economic conditions change, these probabilities are reassessed and the average or expected returns from a business decision are recalculated. Modern statistical spreadsheets and statistical packages are used to cascade the changing probabilities across all upcoming or reversible decisions of the business.

Sports business

Insights regarding player performance that impacts team outcomes can be exploited by a team if they can recognize undervalued traits in players. Famously, the Oakland Athletics front office did exactly this by recognizing that a statistical analysis identified players who were having large impacts on positive team performance but were not being compensated for these contributions (see Moneyball). This provided an opportunity for the organization to buy and play this undervalued talent.

Data

Data are values assigned to observations or measurements and are the building blocks of statistical analysis.

Information is data that are transformed into useful facts that can be used for a specific purpose, such as making a decision.

A data set is a collection of data points in a single application.

Structured data are data that has been ordered into rows and columns (a data table). The rows represent a distinct element (i.e., a client or a person). The columns represent different characteristics that have been measured for the distinct element (i.e., street address, postal code, vertical jump).

Unstructured data are data that is not ordered into data tables. Examples include the characters in a tweet, the text from a news break, and the transcript of a corporate report.

Secondary data are data that somebody else has collected and made available for others to use.

Primary data are data that you have collected for your own use.

Direct observation is a method of gathering data while the subjects of interest are in their natural environment, often unaware that their behavior is being recorded.

A focus group is a direct observational technique whereby individuals are often paid to discuss their attitudes toward products or services in a group setting controlled by a moderator.

An experiment is a method of gathering primary data by exposing subjects to certain treatments and recording the data of interest.

A survey is a method of collecting primary data by directly asking people a series of questions. They can be administered by e-mail, via the web, through snail mail, face to face, or over the telephone.

Two main types of data

Quantitative data use numerical values to measure a characteristic of interest.

Qualitative data use non-numerical values to assign labels to a characteristic. For example, level-of-education may be the degree earned: Less than High School, High School Diploma, Associates Degree, Bachelors Degree, Masters Degree, Doctorate.

Classifying data by their measurement approach

Nominal data are data that are described as a category or a label. Examples are marital status (married, single, divorced, widowed), or membership status (yes, no).

Ordinal data have all the properties of nominal data, with the added feature that we can rank-order the values from highest to lowest.

Interval data is strictly quantitative and allows us to measure the differences between the categories with actual numbers in a meaningful way. For example, temperatures, or product dimensions.

Ratio data have all the features of interval data with the added benefit of having a true zero point. True zero: If the phrase “twice as much” accurately describes the relationship between two values that differ by a multiple of two, the data can be considered ratio data.

Time series versus cross-sectional data

Time series data are values that correspond to specific measurements taken over a range of time periods.

The Federal Funds Effective Rate is the interest rate at which depository institutions trade federal funds (balances held at Federal Reserve Banks) with each other overnight. The FRED series identifier is DFF. Source: Federal Funds Effective Rate on FRED.

Claude Analytics Laboratory 1: Obtaining data from a data service

An application programming interface (API) is a documented way for one computer program to request data or services from another. Instead of downloading and editing a spreadsheet manually, we can write a reproducible program that requests a defined FRED series. The program can be re-run tomorrow and will pick up tomorrow’s data; a spreadsheet you edited by hand cannot.

FRED exposes its data two ways, and the distinction matters for this course:

  • A public CSV endpoint that requires no credentials. It is an ordinary URL that returns ordinary CSV:

    https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/fredgraph.csv?id=DFF&cosd=2020-01-01

    Paste that into a browser and you will get the data. This is what we will use for lecture work and Laboratory 1, because every student can run it immediately with nothing installed but pandas.

You will not run it. Your exercises read the CSV it produced, which is committed to the repository along with a record of exactly where each series came from. That is deliberate: an analysis that depends on a live network connection fails in ways that have nothing to do with the analysis.

Verification checklist

Do not stop when the program runs without an error. Verify that:

  • the file really is DFF — the Federal Funds Effective Rate — and not some neighbouring series with a similar name;
  • the index is stored as dates and the rate column is numeric;
  • the dates are unique and in order, before you trust any count or average computed from them;
  • FRED’s . missing-value marker became a missing numeric value rather than the text ".";
  • the script reports rather than silently deletes missing observations;
  • the row count and latest date are plausible against the FRED webpage for DFF; and
  • every number in your write-up appears somewhere in the executed output above.

The third item is the one students skip. A duplicated date inflates every count and can offset a genuine gap, so a coverage check that looks reassuring may be measuring a broken series. Verify the thing you are measuring with, not just the thing you are measuring.

A data request that succeeds establishes only that the server returned something. It does not establish that we requested the correct concept, units, frequency, dates, or transformation. Those are analytical responsibilities.

Cross-sectional data

Cross-sectional data are values collected from a number of subjects (firms, individuals, states, regions, etc.) during the same time period (or nearly the same).

State unemployment levels can also be retrieved from FRED. Conveniently, the symbol for the unemployment rate for a state is the state’s two letter abbreviation followed by the letters “UR”. The Nevada unemployment rate is NVUR.

Extending the workflow to cross-sectional data

The definition of a cross section requires more care than merely downloading 50 series. State unemployment observations are released on schedules that can differ, and the latest non-missing date need not be identical for every state. A defensible comparison must establish a common reference date or explicitly document any timing differences.

The Laboratory 1 pack already contains this script and its data — you do not need to download anything else. In the same conversation where you ran 01a_inspect_dff.py, ask Claude:

Prompt

First: does every state’s most recent observation fall on the same date? If the dates differ, report the differences and ask me to choose a common-date rule rather than silently combining unlike periods.

Second: are there any missing values anywhere in this series — not just at the end? If so, tell me how many, which states, and which dates. Do not drop them silently.

Run it, and then read the two answers against each other.

The first check passes. Every state reports through the same month, so this really is a clean cross section and there is no rule for you to choose.

The second check does not pass. Look at what it found.

That gap is the lesson. The date-alignment check examines each state’s latest observation, and the hole is not at the end of the series — it is in the middle. No month is absent from the file, so a row count cannot see it either: the row exists, and the value inside it is empty.

A check that passes is not the same as data that is clean. It means the question you asked came back fine. A different question, asked of the same file, comes back differently.

This is why 01a_inspect_dff.py reports a missing-value count rather than assuming zero, and why the script you just ran prints how many observations it is about to discard instead of discarding them quietly. An analysis that drops data without saying so is not wrong in its arithmetic. It is wrong about what it is describing — and nobody reading the output can tell.

data/README.md documents this gap and says plainly that it is how FRED distributes the series. Missing months in official statistics usually have a reason, and finding out what it was is a better exercise than filling the hole.

A population represents all possible subjects of interest. A sample is a subset of a population.

Inferential statistics allows us to make claims or conclusions about the data and its population.

Inferential statistics at work:

  • Production engineers tell us that a properly functioning production line will produce boxes of cereal with a weight of 18.0 oz.
  • We select a random sample of cereal from boxes coming from this production line (i.e., population).
  • We calculate the sample average to be 18.2 oz.
  • Using inferential statistics, we determine that the probability of observing a sample average of 18.2 oz is less than 1% if the actual population average were 18.0 oz.
  • Conclude that the actual population average is greater than 18.0 oz. That is, we find that there is a problem with this production line that needs to be solved.

Predictive statistics analyzes past data to predict future values and make predictions.

Data that describe a characteristic about a population are known as parameters.

Data that describe a characteristic about a sample are known as statistics.

Business analytics uses statistical analysis and predictive models in the business world to analyze data and make meaningful conclusions about decisions.

Data Analytics uses statistical analysis and predictive models to answer questions in the business world and more broadly to make meaningful conclusions about decisions.

Ethics and statistics — it’s a dangerous world of data out there

A biased sample is a sample that does not represent the intended population and that can lead to distorted findings. Biased sampling can occur either intentionally or unintentionally.

One of the most frequent abuses of statistics that I notice regularly is the focus on low probability events and the use of these outcomes in the headlines. For example, a properly trained data analyst will report a 95% confidence interval for a particular parameter. The analyst may say that there is a 95% chance that the Nevada State net budget will range from \((-\$750\text{ million}, \$2100\text{ million})\). It would be misleading in an article to say that:

Researchers find the State of Nevada is in budgetary peril: A budget shortfall of nearly $1 Billion in the cards

When we see a wide ranging 95% confidence interval, the appropriate takeaway is that there is a lot of uncertainty and a lot is unknown about the processes. I believe journalists and politicians behave like they do because it sells papers and can spur voting interest.

The same failure mode appears in AI-assisted work. An assistant will produce a confident paragraph whether or not the underlying computation supports it. Confidence in the prose is not evidence about the data. Check the output.

## Some uses of Data Analytics and models

### Marketing research

**Taste tests** have been a staple of marketing research. Kellogg researchers,
for example, would provide consumers with **Cheez-It** crackers and
**Nabisco's Cheese Nips**. The researchers would ask consumers to rate the
product. By repeating this research with thousands of consumers, the
researchers would establish trends in preferences across the market. This
research would shape product development. If you avoid the researchers armed
with clipboards in the mall (and everyone like you with similar tastes), your
preferences will not be reflected in the next evolution of snack food!

It is actually much more difficult to elude the market researchers today. Many
websites you visit are monitoring
[**clickthrough rates** (CTR)](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/2615875?hl=en).
We will say much more about **marketing research** in this class.

### Advertising

The television industry for decades has used
[**Nielsen Media Research**](https://markets.nielsen.com/us/en/about-us/panels/ratings-and-families/).
These ratings are based on data collected from a panel of US households. The
Nielsen researchers sample from these households to measure viewing habits and
preferences for the entire country. The design of these samples is critical in
ensuring that the results from the survey responses reflect the nation as a
whole. **Sampling strategies** and **statistical inferences** are a topic for
us this semester.

### Operations

**Quality control** is an important consideration for today's large scale
producers. As many production processes are **automated**, sampling from the
large quantities of goods produced in each period is a technique to assess
production quality. As no production process is perfect, quality control
engineers establish benchmarks for inherent variation in, say, product
dimensions. [**Six Sigma**](https://www.6sigma.us/six-sigma.php) is a
methodology intent on reducing this **inherent variation** to a small or
minuscule level. Our quality control engineers identify problems in the
production processes when they find production deformities outside the defined
benchmarks. Statistical **variation** is an important topic for our
investigations.

### Finance

Many economic events cannot be perfectly anticipated. This is particularly
troublesome given the experience of some economic shocks can have disruptive
change on a business. Moreover, many of these events cannot be avoided. For
example, if the global economy enters a recession, **pro cyclical** businesses
would more than likely experience sharp reductions in consumer demand.
Decisions regarding launching a new product line or new manufacturing facility
could have disastrous implications for a business if these launches coincided
with a recession. Finance professionals use **probability** as a tool to weight
potential outcomes across all potential business climate scenarios. As economic
conditions change, these probabilities are reassessed and the **average** or
**expected returns** from a business decision are recalculated. Modern
statistical spreadsheets and statistical packages are used to **cascade** the
changing probabilities across all upcoming or reversible decisions of the
business.

### Sports business

Insights regarding player performance that impacts team outcomes can be
exploited by a team if they can recognize undervalued traits in players.
Famously, the Oakland Athletics front office did exactly this by recognizing
that a statistical analysis identified players who were having large impacts on
positive team performance but were not being compensated for these
contributions (see [Moneyball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moneyball_(film))).
This provided an opportunity for the organization to buy and **play** this
undervalued talent.

### Two main types of data

::: {.charcoal}
**Quantitative data** use numerical values to measure a characteristic of

:::

::: {.charcoal}
**Qualitative data** use non-numerical values to assign labels to a

### Classifying data by their measurement approach

::: {.charcoal}
**Nominal data** are data that are described as a category or a label. Examples
are marital status (married, single, divorced, widowed), or membership status
(yes, no).
:::

::: {.charcoal}
**Ordinal data** have all the properties of nominal data, with the added
feature that we can rank-order the values from highest to lowest.
:::

::: {.charcoal}
**Interval data** is strictly quantitative and allows us to measure the
differences between the categories with actual numbers in a meaningful way. For
example, temperatures, or product dimensions.
:::

::: {.charcoal}
**Ratio data** have all the features of interval data with the added benefit of
having a true zero point. *True zero*: If the phrase "twice as much" accurately

## Selecting versus calculating: an audit

The previous version of this note built a sequence of month-start dates and
used it to index the daily series directly. In R that looked like
`dff[my_months, ]`; the Python equivalent is a `.loc` lookup on a list of
dates. Both do the same thing, and it is worth being precise about what that
thing is.

```{python}
#| label: month-starts

my_months = pd.date_range("2020-01-01", pd.Timestamp.today().normalize(), freq="MS")
selected = dff.reindex(my_months)
selected.head(12)
```

::: {.scarlet}
This **selects particular daily observations** whose dates happen to be the
first of a month. It does **not calculate** a monthly federal funds rate.
:::

There is a second, quieter hazard in selecting by date. A requested date only
returns a value if the series actually has an observation on that day, and
whether it does depends on the series' publication calendar. Rather than assume,
we check:

```{python}
#| label: month-start-gaps

n_requested = len(my_months)
n_missing = int(selected["federal_funds_rate"].isna().sum())

print(f"Month-start dates requested: {n_requested}")
print(f"Dates with no observation:   {n_missing}")
if n_missing:
    print("Missing:", [d.date() for d in
                       selected.index[selected['federal_funds_rate'].isna()]][:12])
```

`DFF` is published on a 7-day daily calendar, so it has a value on weekends and
holidays and this lookup happens to find one every month. Run the same code
against a business-day series — `DGS10`, say, or `SP500` — and month-starts
that land on a weekend come back empty. Selecting by date is fragile in a way
that is invisible until the calendar changes underneath you.

### Three defensible alternatives

```{python}
#| label: monthly-alternatives

rate = dff["federal_funds_rate"]

monthly = pd.DataFrame({
    "mean":  rate.resample("MS").mean(),
    "first": rate.resample("MS").first(),
    "last":  rate.resample("MS").last(),
})
monthly.head(12).round(4)
```

Each answers a different business question:

+ the **monthly mean** describes average borrowing conditions *during* the
  month;
+ the **first available observation** describes the rate the month *opened*
  with; and
+ the **last available observation** describes the rate the month *closed*
  with.

These calculations produce different numbers. The correct transformation
depends on the question, and choosing one without stating the question is not a
technical shortcut — it is an unstated assumption.

```{python}
#| label: monthly-divergence

spread = (monthly["mean"] - monthly["last"]).abs()
print(f"Largest gap between the monthly mean and the month-end rate: "
      f"{spread.max():.3f} percentage points, in "
      f"{spread.idxmax().strftime('%B %Y')}.")
```

A **population** represents all possible subjects of interest. A **sample** is

:::

Inferential statistics at work:

+ Production engineers tell us that a properly functioning production line will
  produce boxes of cereal with a weight of 18.0 oz.
+ We select a **random sample** of cereal from boxes coming from this
  production line (i.e., population).
+ We calculate the sample average to be 18.2 oz.
+ Using **inferential statistics**, we determine that the probability of
  observing a sample average of 18.2 oz is less than 1% if the actual
  population average were 18.0 oz.
+ Conclude that the actual population average is greater than 18.0 oz. That is,
  we find that there is a problem with this production line that needs to be
  solved.

predictions.
:::

## Ethics and statistics — it's a dangerous world of data out there

::: {.charcoal}
A **biased sample** is a sample that does not represent the intended population
and that can lead to distorted findings. Biased sampling can occur either
intentionally or unintentionally.
:::

One of the most frequent *abuses* of statistics that I notice regularly is the
focus on low probability events and the use of these outcomes in the
**headlines**. For example, a properly trained data analyst will report a 95%
confidence interval for a particular parameter. The analyst may say that there
is a 95% chance that the Nevada State net budget will range from
$(-\$750\text{ million}, \$2100\text{ million})$. It would be *misleading* in
an article to say that:

> Researchers find the State of Nevada is in budgetary peril: A budget
> shortfall of nearly \$1 Billion in the cards

When we see a wide ranging 95% confidence interval, the appropriate takeaway is
that there is a lot of **uncertainty** and a lot is **unknown** about the
processes. I believe journalists and politicians behave like they do because it
sells papers and can spur voting interest.

::: {.charcoal}
The same failure mode appears in AI-assisted work. An assistant will produce a
confident paragraph whether or not the underlying computation supports it.
Confidence in the prose is not evidence about the data. **Check the output.**
:::
