Measures of Central Tendency

Central tendency is a single value used to describe a central focal point in a data set.

The Mean

Given a data series \(X=\{x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\}\), the sample mean is

\[\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i,\]

where \(n\) is the number of members of the sample. For a population we write

\[\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i,\]

where \(N\) is the number of members of the population.

The mean is easy to compute, easy to explain, and always produces exactly one number. That is its entire case for the defence. Here is the case against.

Outliers are values much higher or lower than most of the data. The mean is pulled toward them. The median is not.

The Weighted Mean

For a data set \(\{x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\}\) with corresponding weights \(\{w_1,w_2,\dots,w_n\}\), the weighted mean is

\[\bar{x}_w=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_ix_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i}.\]

This is the formula MBAs get wrong most often in practice, usually by not reaching for it at all. Consider the unemployment rate of the fifty states.

Show the code 
plain = states["UR"].mean()
weighted = (states["UR"] * states["POPN"]).sum() / states["POPN"].sum()

pd.DataFrame({
    "measure": ["Unweighted mean of the 50 state rates",
                "Population-weighted mean"],
    "unemployment rate (%)": [round(plain, 2), round(weighted, 2)],
})
measure unemployment rate (%)
0 Unweighted mean of the 50 state rates 3.97
1 Population-weighted mean 4.32

The two answers differ by +0.35 percentage points, and only one of them is the unemployment rate of the country. The unweighted mean gives WY — population 588,753 — exactly the same say as CA, population 39,355,309.

Whenever the things you are averaging represent groups of different sizes — states, stores, regions, customer segments — the plain average answers the question “what is the typical store’s rate?” and the weighted average answers “what is the rate across all customers?” Those are different questions. Decide which one you were asked before you pick a formula.

The Median

The median is the value for which half the observations are higher and half are lower.

The index point is the position of the median once the data are arranged in ascending order:

\[i=0.5\,n,\]

where \(n\) is the number of observations. If \(i\) is not an integer, round up and take the value in that position. If \(i\) is an integer, take the average of the values in positions \(i\) and \(i+1\).

The Western states again

The median population of a Western state is 3,538,904. The mean is 7,088,733 — higher by 3,549,829.

The list is already sorted above. With \(n=11\), the index point is \(0.5 \times 11 = 5\.5\), which is not an integer, so we round up to position 6 and read off the value there.

The mode is the value that appears most often in a data set. It is the only measure of central tendency that works on categories.

The Shapes of Distributions

A symmetrical distribution is a mirror image about its centre. Its mean and median are approximately equal.

In a left-skewed distribution, the median is larger than the mean.

In a right-skewed distribution, the median is smaller than the mean.

The single most useful diagnostic in this chapter is free: compute the mean and the median and compare them. If they are far apart, one of them is lying to you about the middle.

In this series the mean sits slightly above the median, which makes the right tail marginally the longer one — but a gap that small is not a finding, and it can change sign as more months are added.

What is not in doubt is the mechanism. Unemployment rises hard and fast in a recession and comes back down slowly, so the months far from the centre tend to be the high ones, and the box plot shows those sitting past the upper fence. Compare that to the GPA and income distributions below, where the gap between mean and median is large enough to survive any amount of new data.

The median exceeds the mean, which is the definition of left skew. Any measure with a ceiling tends to skew left: exam scores, on-time delivery rates, satisfaction surveys capped at 5. Knowing which one you are holding tells you what to expect before you compute anything.

Two notes on this data set, both of which you would want stated if you were the reader rather than the writer.

First, these observations are simulated from the counts the IRS publishes by zip code — the IRS does not release household-level returns. The shape is real; no individual row is a real household.

Second, this file is a random sample of 100,000 households drawn from roughly 1.3 million. Sampling is Chapter 7’s subject, and the reason a sample this size is safe here is exactly what that chapter is about.

Which measure should you use?

Advantage Disadvantage
Mean Easy to compute and to explain; uses every observation Pulled by outliers
Median Unaffected by outliers Ignores the size of the values on either side
Mode The only option for categorical data May not exist, and may not be unique

The practical rule: report both the mean and the median. When they agree, you have lost nothing. When they disagree, you have found the most important sentence in your write-up.

Measures of Variability

Two data sets can share a mean and have nothing else in common. Variability is what separates them.

The Range

The range is the largest value minus the smallest value.

The range is built from exactly two observations and ignores the other 48. Add one catastrophic year and the range moves; add fifty ordinary years and it does not move at all. It is a useful headline and a poor summary.

The Variance and the Standard Deviation

For a sample, the variance is

\[\hat{\sigma}^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2.\]

The standard deviation is its square root:

\[\hat{\sigma}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2}.\]

The standard deviation is carried in the same units as the original data — the variance is in units squared, which is why nobody reports “the variance in returns was 0.0246 return-squared.” Square it back and report the standard deviation.

The divisor is \(n-1\), not \(n\). The sample variance computed this way is an unbiased estimator of the population variance: repeat the sampling many times and the average of the sample variances converges on the true value. Divide by \(n\) and you will, on average, understate the spread.

In Excel this is the difference between STDEV.S and STDEV.P. In pandas it is the ddof argument, and pandas defaults to the sample version while numpy defaults to the population version. Two libraries in the same language, two different answers. Know which one you called.

s = returns["sp500"].dropna()
pd.DataFrame({
    "divisor": ["n - 1  (sample, Excel STDEV.S)", "n      (population, STDEV.P)"],

})

Using the Mean and Standard Deviation Together

The Coefficient of Variation

The coefficient of variation measures the standard deviation as a percentage of the mean:

\[CV=\frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\bar{x}}\cdot100.\]

Written out for each:

\[CV_{S\&P}=\frac{0.1568}{0.0993}\cdot100=158.0\]

\[CV_{WMT}=\frac{0.3443}{0.2478}\cdot100=138.9\]

Read those two lines together.

Walmart’s standard deviation is 2.20 times the S&P 500’s, so by standard deviation alone Walmart is the more volatile holding — and that is true. But Walmart’s coefficient of variation is 138.9 against the index’s 158.0, because the mean return it is deviating around is 2.5 times larger. Per unit of return earned, the S&P 500 carried the more variable ride.

An MBA who takes only the standard deviation away from this chapter will rank those two backwards. The standard deviation answers “how much does it move?” The coefficient of variation answers “how much does it move, given what it pays?” — and the second question is the one an investment committee is actually asking.

The coefficient of variation is only interpretable when the mean is positive and comfortably away from zero. As \(\bar{x}\) approaches zero the ratio explodes, and for a mean that can be negative — a spread, a net change, a margin swing — the CV is meaningless and occasionally sign-flipped. Check the mean before you quote the ratio.

The \(z\)-score

The \(z\)-score is the number of standard deviations a particular value sits from the mean of its distribution:

\[z=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{\hat{\sigma}}.\]

Values with \(|z|>3\) are conventionally categorised as outliers.

Expressing a value as a \(z\)-score is what makes two things measured in different units comparable at all. It is also the machinery underneath every inference test in the second half of this course, so it repays attention now.

The Empirical Rule

If a distribution is bell-shaped and symmetric, approximately 68%, 95%, and 99.7% of the values fall within one, two, and three standard deviations of the mean, respectively.

Rearranged, a value can be recovered from its \(z\)-score:

\[x=\mu+\sigma\cdot z.\]

That picture is of a mathematical function, not of any data set. The rule only applies to the extent your data resembles it, and that is a claim to be checked rather than assumed:

Applying a symmetric rule to a skewed distribution produces intervals that reach into territory the variable cannot occupy, and nothing in the arithmetic warns you.

Chebyshev’s Theorem

Chebyshev’s Theorem states that for any \(z>1\), the percentage of values falling within \(z\) standard deviations of the mean is at least

\[\left(1-\frac{1}{z^2}\right)\cdot100.\]

This is the tool for the situation you are actually in most of the time: a distribution whose shape you do not know. It applies to every distribution — skewed, bimodal, lumpy, anything — which is exactly why the guarantee it gives is weak. It is a floor, never an estimate.

Applied to inflation

Take the two standard deviation threshold from the section above. What does Chebyshev guarantee about how often inflation can exceed it?

Show the code 
chebyshev_table(2).to_frame("Chebyshev at z = 2")
Chebyshev at z = 2
z 2.0
at_least_within_percent 75.0
at_most_outside_percent 25.0
at_most_one_tail_percent 12.5

\[\left(1-\frac{1}{z^2}\right)\cdot100=\left(1-\frac{1}{(2)^2}\right)\cdot100=75.00\]

At least 75.00% of months must lie within two standard deviations of the mean, so at most 25.00% lie outside that interval — and, if we are willing to treat the two tails as comparable, at most 12.50% above it.

In this data, 92.79% of months actually fell inside that interval and 7.06% were above it.

The bound held, and it held by a wide margin. That is the normal outcome and it is the thing to understand about Chebyshev: it is almost always loose. It tells you what cannot be true, not what is likely. A consultant who reports the Chebyshev bound as a forecast has reported a number that is correct and useless at the same time.

Two cautions worth carrying out of here. The theorem bounds both tails together; splitting the remainder in half assumes a symmetry the theorem never promised, so state a one-tail figure as an upper bound and never as an estimate. And for \(z \le 1\) the formula returns zero or worse — it tells you nothing at all, which is honest but unquotable.

Applied to graduate GPA

Working with Grouped Data

Sometimes you never see the individual observations. What you are handed is a table of counts by class, which is how most published statistics arrive.

The midpoint of a class is the average of its endpoints.

The sample mean for grouped data is

\[\bar{x}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i\cdot m_i,\]

where \(f_i\) is the frequency of class \(i\), \(m_i\) its midpoint, \(n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i\), and \(k\) the number of classes.

The sample variance for grouped data is

\[\hat{\sigma}^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i\cdot\left(m_i-\bar{x}\right)^2.\]

Measures of Relative Position

Where does one value sit relative to the rest?

A percentile measures the approximate percentage of values in the data set that fall below the value of interest.

The \(\rho\)th percentile is the value that at least \(\rho\) percent of the observations fall below.

The index point for the \(p\)th percentile is

\[i=\frac{p}{100}\cdot n.\]

If \(i\) is not a whole number, round up; that position holds the value. If \(i\) is a whole number, take the midpoint of positions \(i\) and \(i+1\).

Show the code 
percentile(gpa["GPA"], 0.80)
3.925

With \(n=507\) students, the index point for the 80th percentile is \(0.80 \times 507 = 405\.6\). The 80th percentile GPA is 3.9250.

There is more than one rule for this, and different software uses different ones. Here is the same percentile computed three ways:

numpy default (linear) 3.925

They are close, and on a data set this size the disagreement is in the third decimal. On a data set of twelve observations it would not be. This course uses the index-point rule above, matching Excel’s PERCENTILE.EXC. The point is not that one is right — it is that you should be able to say which rule produced your number when someone else’s differs.

Percentile rank

The percentile rank of a value \(x\) is

\[\text{Percentile Rank}=\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}I\{x_i<x\}\right]\cdot100,\]

where \(I\{\cdot\}\) is an indicator function taking the value 1 when the statement inside is true and 0 when it is false.

Show the code 
round(percentile_rank(gpa["GPA"], 3.75), 2)
52.96

A student with a 3.75 GPA sits at the 52.96th percentile: 268 of 507 students are below them.

Percentile and percentile rank are inverses of one another. The first takes a percentage and returns a value; the second takes a value and returns a percentage. Getting them the wrong way round is the most common error in this section.

The first, second, and third quartiles are the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles. The second quartile is the median.

Measures of Association Between Two Variables

The sample covariance measures the direction of the linear relationship between two variables:

\[\hat{\sigma}_{xy}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)\left(y_i-\bar{y}\right).\]

The sample correlation coefficient measures both the strength and the direction:

\[\hat{\rho}_{xy}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{xy}}{\hat{\sigma}_x\hat{\sigma}_y},\]

where \(\hat{\sigma}_x\) and \(\hat{\sigma}_y\) are the sample standard deviations of \(x\) and \(y\). It always lies between \(-1\) and \(+1\).

## The Mean

::: {.blue}
Given a data series $X=\{x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\}$, the sample **mean** is

$$\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i,$$

where $n$ is the number of members of the sample. For a population we write

$$\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_i,$$

where $N$ is the number of members of the population.
:::

The mean is easy to compute, easy to explain, and always produces exactly one
number. That is its entire case for the defence. Here is the case against.

## The Weighted Mean

::: {.blue}
For a data set $\{x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\}$ with corresponding weights
$\{w_1,w_2,\dots,w_n\}$, the **weighted mean** is

$$\bar{x}_w=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_ix_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i}.$$
:::

This is the formula MBAs get wrong most often in practice, usually by not
reaching for it at all. Consider the unemployment rate of the fifty states.

where $n$ is the number of observations. If $i$ is not an integer, round up
and take the value in that position. If $i$ is an integer, take the average of
the values in positions $i$ and $i+1$.

## The Mode

::: {.blue}
The **mode** is the value that appears most often in a data set. It is the
only measure of central tendency that works on categories.
:::

# The Shapes of Distributions

::: {.brown}
A **symmetrical** distribution is a mirror image about its centre. Its mean
and median are approximately equal.

In a **left-skewed** distribution, the median is larger than the mean.

In a **right-skewed** distribution, the median is smaller than the mean.
:::

The single most useful diagnostic in this chapter is free: compute the mean
and the median and compare them. If they are far apart, one of them is lying
to you about the middle.

## Which measure should you use?

|  | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean** | Easy to compute and to explain; uses every observation | Pulled by outliers |
| **Median** | Unaffected by outliers | Ignores the size of the values on either side |
| **Mode** | The only option for categorical data | May not exist, and may not be unique |

::: {.charcoal}
The practical rule: report both the mean and the median. When they agree,
you have lost nothing. When they disagree, you have found the most important
sentence in your write-up.
:::

# Measures of Variability

Two data sets can share a mean and have nothing else in common. Variability is
what separates them.

## The Range

::: {.blue}
The **range** is the largest value minus the smallest value.
:::


Over these `{python} str(n_years)` years the range is
`{python} f"{sp_range:.4f}"` — `{python} f"{100*sp_range:.1f}"` percentage
points separating the best year (`{python} str(int(best['year']))`, at
`{python} f"{100*best['sp500']:+.1f}"`%) from the worst
(`{python} str(int(worst['year']))`, at
`{python} f"{100*worst['sp500']:+.1f}"`%).

::: {.brown}
The range is built from exactly two observations and ignores the other
`{python} str(n_years - 2)`. Add one catastrophic year and the range moves;
add fifty ordinary years and it does not move at all. It is a useful headline
and a poor summary.
:::

## The Variance and the Standard Deviation

::: {.blue}
For a *sample*, the **variance** is

$$\hat{\sigma}^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2.$$

The **standard deviation** is its square root:

$$\hat{\sigma}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)^2}.$$
:::

The standard deviation is carried in the same units as the original data — the
variance is in units *squared*, which is why nobody reports "the variance in
returns was 0.0246 return-squared." Square it back and report the standard
deviation.

::: {.brown}
The divisor is $n-1$, not $n$. The sample variance computed this way is an
**unbiased estimator** of the population variance: repeat the sampling many
times and the average of the sample variances converges on the true value.
Divide by $n$ and you will, on average, understate the spread.

In Excel this is the difference between `STDEV.S` and `STDEV.P`. In pandas it
is the `ddof` argument, and pandas defaults to the sample version while numpy
defaults to the population version. Two libraries in the same language, two
different answers. Know which one you called.
:::

```{python}
#| label: ddof-demo
s = returns["sp500"].dropna()
pd.DataFrame({
    "divisor": ["n - 1  (sample, Excel STDEV.S)", "n      (population, STDEV.P)"],
    "standard deviation": [round(s.std(ddof=1), 6), round(s.std(ddof=0), 6)],
})
```

# Using the Mean and Standard Deviation Together

## The Coefficient of Variation

::: {.brown}
The **coefficient of variation** measures the standard deviation as a
percentage of the mean:

$$CV=\frac{\hat{\sigma}}{\bar{x}}\cdot100.$$
:::

### Example: the S&P 500 against Walmart

```{python}
wm_cv = coefficient_of_variation(wm_v)
sd_ratio = wm_sd / sp_sd
```

Walmart's standard deviation is `{python} f"{sd_ratio:.2f}"` times the S&P
500's, so by standard deviation alone `{python} riskier_by_sd` is the more
volatile holding — and that is true. But Walmart's *coefficient of variation*
is `{python} f"{wm_cv:.1f}"` against the index's
`{python} f"{sp_cv:.1f}"`, because the mean return it is deviating around is
`{python} f"{wm_mu/sp_mu:.1f}"` times larger. Per unit of return earned,
`{python} riskier_by_cv` carried the more variable ride.

An MBA who takes only the standard deviation away from this chapter will rank
those two backwards. The standard deviation answers *"how much does it move?"*
The coefficient of variation answers *"how much does it move, given what it
pays?"* — and the second question is the one an investment committee is
actually asking.
:::

::: {.brown}
The coefficient of variation is only interpretable when the mean is positive
and comfortably away from zero. As $\bar{x}$ approaches zero the ratio
explodes, and for a mean that can be negative — a spread, a net change, a
margin swing — the CV is meaningless and occasionally sign-flipped. Check the
mean before you quote the ratio.
:::

## The $z$-score

::: {.brown}
The **$z$-score** is the number of standard deviations a particular value sits
from the mean of its distribution:

$$z=\frac{x-\bar{x}}{\hat{\sigma}}.$$

Values with $|z|>3$ are conventionally categorised as **outliers**.
:::

Expressing a value as a $z$-score is what makes two things measured in
different units comparable at all. It is also the machinery underneath every
inference test in the second half of this course, so it repays attention now.

## Chebyshev's Theorem

::: {.blue}
**Chebyshev's Theorem** states that for any $z>1$, the percentage of values
falling within $z$ standard deviations of the mean is at least

$$\left(1-\frac{1}{z^2}\right)\cdot100.$$
:::

This is the tool for the situation you are actually in most of the time: a
distribution whose shape you do not know. It applies to *every* distribution —
skewed, bimodal, lumpy, anything — which is exactly why the guarantee it gives
is weak. It is a floor, never an estimate.

### Applied to inflation

Take the two standard deviation threshold from the section above. What does
Chebyshev guarantee about how often inflation can exceed it?

```{python}

```

```{python}

#| echo: false

show_math(r"\left(1-\frac{1}{z^2}\right)\cdot100"
          rf"=\left(1-\frac{{1}}{{(2)^2}}\right)\cdot100={chebyshev(2):.2f}")
```

```{python}
#| label: cheb-cpi-values
#| include: false

cheb_in = chebyshev(2)
cheb_out = 100 - cheb_in
cheb_tail = cheb_out / 2
actual_in = float(((infl["inflation"] >= mu_i - 2*sd_i) &
                   (infl["inflation"] <= mu_i + 2*sd_i)).mean() * 100)
actual_tail = 100 * len(infl[infl["inflation"] > mu_i + 2*sd_i]) / len(infl)
```

At least `{python} f"{cheb_in:.2f}"`% of months must lie within two standard
deviations of the mean, so at most `{python} f"{cheb_out:.2f}"`% lie outside
that interval — and, if we are willing to treat the two tails as comparable,
at most `{python} f"{cheb_tail:.2f}"`% above it.

In this data, `{python} f"{actual_in:.2f}"`% of months actually fell inside
that interval and `{python} f"{actual_tail:.2f}"`% were above it.

::: {.charcoal}
The bound held, and it held by a wide margin. That is the normal outcome and
it is the thing to understand about Chebyshev: it is almost always loose. It
tells you what *cannot* be true, not what is likely. A consultant who reports
the Chebyshev bound as a forecast has reported a number that is correct and
useless at the same time.

Two cautions worth carrying out of here. The theorem bounds both tails
together; splitting the remainder in half assumes a symmetry the theorem never
promised, so state a one-tail figure as an upper bound and never as an
estimate. And for $z \le 1$ the formula returns zero or worse — it tells you
nothing at all, which is honest but unquotable.
:::

# Working with Grouped Data

Sometimes you never see the individual observations. What you are handed is a
table of counts by class, which is how most published statistics arrive.

::: {.blue}
The **midpoint** of a class is the average of its endpoints.

The **sample mean for grouped data** is

$$\bar{x}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i\cdot m_i,$$

where $f_i$ is the frequency of class $i$, $m_i$ its midpoint,
$n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i$, and $k$ the number of classes.

The **sample variance for grouped data** is

$$\hat{\sigma}^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i\cdot\left(m_i-\bar{x}\right)^2.$$
:::

# Measures of Relative Position

Where does one value sit relative to the rest?

::: {.blue}
A **percentile** measures the approximate percentage of values in the data set
that fall below the value of interest.

The **$\rho$th percentile** is the value that at least $\rho$ percent of the
observations fall below.

The **index point** for the $p$th percentile is

$$i=\frac{p}{100}\cdot n.$$

If $i$ is not a whole number, round up; that position holds the value. If $i$
is a whole number, take the midpoint of positions $i$ and $i+1$.
:::

```{python}
#| label: pct-80
percentile(gpa["GPA"], 0.80)
```

```{python}
#| label: pct-80-stats
#| include: false

p80 = percentile(gpa["GPA"], 0.80)
p80_i = 0.80 * len(gpa)
```

With $n=`{python} num(len(gpa))`$ students, the index point for the 80th
percentile is $0.80 \times `{python} num(len(gpa))` = `{python} num(p80_i, 1)`$.
The 80th percentile GPA is `{python} f"{p80:.4f}"`.

::: {.charcoal}
There is more than one rule for this, and different software uses different
ones. Here is the same percentile computed three ways:
:::

```{python}
#| label: pct-methods
percentile_methods(gpa["GPA"], 0.80).to_frame()
```

They are close, and on a data set this size the disagreement is in the third
decimal. On a data set of twelve observations it would not be. This course
uses the index-point rule above, matching Excel's `PERCENTILE.EXC`. The point
is not that one is right — it is that you should be able to say which rule
produced your number when someone else's differs.

## Percentile rank

::: {.blue}
The **percentile rank** of a value $x$ is

$$\text{Percentile Rank}=\frac{1}{n}\left[\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}I\{x_i<x\}\right]\cdot100,$$

where $I\{\cdot\}$ is an **indicator function** taking the value 1 when the
statement inside is true and 0 when it is false.
:::

```{python}
#| label: pct-rank
round(percentile_rank(gpa["GPA"], 3.75), 2)
```

```{python}
#| label: pct-rank-stats
#| include: false

pr = percentile_rank(gpa["GPA"], 3.75)
n_below = int((gpa["GPA"] < 3.75).sum())
```

A student with a 3.75 GPA sits at the `{python} f"{pr:.2f}"`th percentile:
`{python} num(n_below)` of `{python} num(len(gpa))` students are below them.

Percentile and percentile rank are inverses of one another. The first takes a
percentage and returns a value; the second takes a value and returns a
percentage. Getting them the wrong way round is the most common error in this
section.

## Quartiles and the interquartile range

::: {.blue}
The first, second, and third **quartiles** are the 25th, 50th, and 75th
percentiles. The second quartile is the median.

The **interquartile range** is

$$IQR=Q_3-Q_1,$$

the spread of the middle 50% of the data.
:::

```{python}
#| label: quartiles
q1, q2, q3 = quartiles(gpa["GPA"])
lo, hi = outlier_fences(gpa["GPA"])
pd.Series({
    "Q1 (25th percentile)": q1,
    "Q2 (median)": q2,
    "Q3 (75th percentile)": q3,
    "IQR": q3 - q1,
    "lower fence  Q1 - 1.5*IQR": lo,
    "upper fence  Q3 + 1.5*IQR": hi,
}).round(4).to_frame("Graduate GPA")
```

The IQR is a measure of spread with the same virtue the median has: the two
most extreme observations in the data set cannot move it. Where the range is
built entirely from outliers, the IQR is immune to them.

means by an outlier.

# Measures of Association Between Two Variables

::: {.blue}
The **sample covariance** measures the *direction* of the linear relationship
between two variables:

$$\hat{\sigma}_{xy}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_i-\bar{x}\right)\left(y_i-\bar{y}\right).$$
:::

::: {.blue}
The **sample correlation coefficient** measures both the strength and the
direction:

$$\hat{\rho}_{xy}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}_{xy}}{\hat{\sigma}_x\hat{\sigma}_y},$$

where $\hat{\sigma}_x$ and $\hat{\sigma}_y$ are the sample standard deviations
of $x$ and $y$. It always lies between $-1$ and $+1$.
:::

```{python}
#| label: assoc-scatter
paired = returns.dropna(subset=["sp500", "wmt"])
_ = scatter(paired["sp500"], paired["wmt"],
            title="Annual returns: Walmart against the S&P 500",
            xlab="S&P 500 annual return", ylab="Walmart annual return")
```

```{python}
#| label: assoc-returns
association(paired["sp500"], paired["wmt"], "sp500", "wmt") \
    .round(6).to_frame("S&P 500 vs Walmart")
```

```{python}
#| label: assoc-stats
#| include: false

a_ret = association(paired["sp500"], paired["wmt"], "sp500", "wmt")
cov_ret = a_ret["covariance"]
cor_ret = a_ret["correlation"]
direction = "move together" if cov_ret > 0 else "move in opposite directions"
strength = ("weak" if abs(cor_ret) < 0.3 else
            "moderate" if abs(cor_ret) < 0.6 else "strong")
```

The covariance is `{python} f"{cov_ret:.6f}"`. Its sign says the two
`{python} direction`. Its magnitude says nothing you can act on, because it
carries the units of both variables multiplied together — rescale returns from
decimals to percentages and the covariance changes by a factor of 10,000 while
the relationship does not change at all.

Dividing by both standard deviations removes the units and leaves
`{python} f"{cor_ret:.4f}"` — a `{python} strength` positive relationship, on a
scale that runs from $-1$ to $+1$ and means the same thing whatever the
variables were measured in.

```{python}
#| label: assoc-states
state_pairs = states[["HPI", "UR", "POPN", "gPOP"]].dropna()
state_pairs.corr().round(3)
```

::: {.scarlet}
A correlation matrix is the fastest way to mislead yourself in this entire
course. Four things it does not tell you:

1. **Causation.** Neither direction, nor the possibility that a third variable
   drives both.
2. **Non-linear relationships.** A perfect U-shape can produce a correlation
   of zero. Draw the scatter plot before you trust the coefficient.
3. **What outliers did to it.** A single extreme pair can create a correlation
   or destroy one, and the number gives no hint.
4. **Whether the sample is large enough to mean anything.** With
   `{python} num(len(state_pairs))` observations, a coefficient of 0.25 is
   not distinguishable from zero — which is a Chapter 9 question, and one we
   will come back to.
:::
