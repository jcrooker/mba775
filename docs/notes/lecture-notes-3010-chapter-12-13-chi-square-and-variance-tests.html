Objectives

  • Explain the chi-square statistic as a measure of how far observed counts sit from the counts a hypothesis predicts, and find its p-value.
  • Test whether k proportions are equal, and recognize the \(2\times2\) case as last week’s two-proportion test.
  • Test whether counts follow a stated distribution (goodness of fit), including the degrees-of-freedom cost of estimating a parameter.
  • Test whether two categorical variables are independent, find which cells drive a rejection, and measure how strong the association is.
  • Know when expected counts are too small, and how to combine categories.
  • Test a single population variance, and build an interval for \(\sigma\).
  • Compare two population variances with the \(F\) test.
  • Explain why the variance tests need normal data much more than the \(t\)-test does, and what to do instead of pretesting variances.

Weeks 9 and 10 tested claims about means and proportions. This week has two new questions.

Chapter 12: do counts match a hypothesis? Is the share of “up” days the same every weekday? Do absences follow a Poisson model? Does the brand a customer buys depend on age? Every one of these is answered with one statistic,

\[\chi^2=\sum_{\text{cells}}\frac{(\text{observed}-\text{expected})^2}{\text{expected}},\]

and the three tests in the chapter differ only in where the expected counts come from. Learn the statistic once, then learn three ways of filling in “expected.”

Chapter 13: is the spread what was claimed? A refrigerator can average 37°F and still freeze the lettuce. A checkout process can average 4 minutes and still leave some customers waiting 10. Here the parameter of interest is the variance \(\sigma^2\), and the test has an important catch that the \(t\)-test does not.

Part I: Chi-Square Tests (Chapter 12)

The chi-square statistic

For counts in \(k\) cells, the chi-square statistic is

\[\chi^2=\sum\frac{(f_o-f_e)^2}{f_e},\]

where \(f_o\) is the observed count in a cell and \(f_e\) is the expected count if \(H_0\) is true. Expected counts need not be whole numbers; they are averages.

If \(H_0\) is true, \(\chi^2\) follows (approximately) a chi-square distribution, which is right-skewed, never negative, and has mean equal to its degrees of freedom. The approximation needs every expected count to be at least 5; if one is not, combine categories.

Show the code 
cx = np.linspace(0.01, 20, 600)
_ = density_chart(cx, {"df = 1": chi2_pdf(cx, 1), "df = 2": chi2_pdf(cx, 2),
                       "df = 4": chi2_pdf(cx, 4), "df = 8": chi2_pdf(cx, 8)},
                  title="Chi-square distributions: right-skewed, mean = df",
                  xlab="chi-square", ymax=0.5)

Three things follow from the formula.

  • Every test is upper-tailed. Squaring makes a cell count too high and a cell count too low both add to \(\chi^2\). Only a large \(\chi^2\) is evidence against \(H_0\), so the p-value is always the right-tail area, Excel’s =CHISQ.DIST.RT(x, df).
  • Each cell’s contribution is scaled by what was expected. Being 10 off when 20 were expected matters more than being 10 off when 200 were.
  • A quick sanity check: since the mean is the df, a \(\chi^2\) near its df is entirely ordinary. A value of 5 with 4 df is nothing; a value of 30 with 4 df is a lot.

We can check that the chi-square distribution really describes the statistic. Below, a model with six categories is assumed true, 240 observations are drawn from it 20,000 times, and \(\chi^2\) is computed each time.

Show the code 
drive_p = [0.20, 0.10, 0.10, 0.15, 0.25, 0.20]

_ = density_histogram(sim[sim < 20], x=cx, density=chi2_pdf(cx, 5), bins=60,
                      title="20,000 chi-square statistics when H0 is true (k = 6)",
                      xlab="chi-square", curve_label="chi-square, df = 5",
                      bar_label="simulated")

The simulated statistics average 5.04, against the df of 5, and 5.2% of them exceed the 5% critical value. The formula’s distribution is the right one, provided the expected counts are large enough. Here the smallest is 24.

Test 1: are k proportions equal?

To test \(H_0:p_1=p_2=\cdots=p_k\), pool all the groups to estimate the common proportion \(\bar{p}\), and expect \(\bar{p}\times n_j\) successes (and \((1-\bar{p})\times n_j\) failures) in group \(j\). The statistic has \(\text{df}=k-1\).

Test 2: goodness of fit

A goodness-of-fit test asks whether observed counts follow a stated probability distribution. \(H_0\) says they do. The expected count in each category is \(n\times\) the probability the model gives it, and

\[\text{df}=k-m-1,\]

where \(k\) is the number of categories and \(m\) is the number of parameters estimated from the sample to compute those probabilities (\(m=0\) if \(H_0\) states the model completely). Each estimated parameter lets the model bend toward the data, and costs one degree of freedom.

Two cautions.

  1. A large p-value does not show that the model is right. It shows the data cannot tell the model apart from the truth. Using the Poisson to plan an attendance policy may be a sensible business decision, but this test did not prove the data are Poisson. Your textbook goes further than the evidence when it says there is “more evidence supporting the use of the Poisson distribution than there is to not use it.”
  2. Excel’s CHISQ.TEST gets the df wrong here. =CHISQ.TEST(observed, expected) always uses \(k-1\) degrees of freedom. That is right for the drive-through (\(m=0\)) and wrong whenever a parameter was estimated: here it would use 5 df instead of 4. Use =CHISQ.DIST.RT(chi-square, k-m-1).

Test 3: are two categorical variables independent?

A contingency table cross-classifies \(n\) observations by two categorical variables, with \(r\) rows and \(c\) columns. The test of independence has

\[H_0:\ \text{the row and column variables are independent},\]

and, if \(H_0\) is true, the expected count in a cell is

\[f_e=\frac{(\text{row total})\times(\text{column total})}{n}, \qquad \text{df}=(r-1)(c-1).\]

Where the formula comes from. Chapter 4 said events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when \(P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)\,P(B)\). Estimate each probability from the margins, \(P(\text{row})=\text{row total}/n\) and \(P(\text{column})=\text{column total}/n\), and multiply by \(n\): \(n\cdot\frac{\text{row}}{n}\cdot\frac{\text{column}}{n}=\frac{\text{row}\times\text{column}}{n}\). Read it another way: if the column a customer falls in has nothing to do with the row, every row should split across the columns in the same proportions as the whole sample does. The test of independence is Chapter 4’s definition, checked with data.

Where is the dependence? Standardized residuals

A rejection says only that somewhere the table departs from independence. To find where, look at each cell’s standardized residual,

\[\frac{f_o-f_e}{\sqrt{f_e}}.\]

Squared, these are exactly the pieces that add up to \(\chi^2\). Read them like \(z\)-scores: beyond about \(\pm2\) is a cell that clearly departs from independence, and beyond \(\pm1.5\) is worth a look.

How strong is the dependence? Cramér’s V

The \(\chi^2\) statistic grows with \(n\): double every count and \(\chi^2\) doubles, though the pattern is unchanged. So, as in weeks 9 and 10, a small p-value says the association is real, not that it is large. The effect size for a contingency table is Cramér’s V:

\[V=\sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n\,\big(\min(r,c)-1\big)}},\]

which runs from 0 (independence) to 1 (each row predicts the column perfectly). A rough guide for tables with a shorter side of 2 or 3: about 0.1 is small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large.

\[V=\sqrt{\frac{12.145}{150\times(3-1)}}=0.201\]

Part II: Tests for a Population Variance (Chapter 13)

One variance

If the population is normal, the statistic

\[\chi^2=\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2}\]

follows a chi-square distribution with \(n-1\) df when \(H_0:\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2\) is true. Unlike Chapter 12, the test can be lower-tailed, upper-tailed, or two-tailed, depending on \(H_1\). A claim about a standard deviation is tested as a claim about its square: \(\sigma<2\) minutes is \(\sigma^2<4\) minutes².

The intuition: \(s^2/\sigma_0^2\) is the ratio of the variance you saw to the variance claimed, and should be near 1 if the claim is right. Multiplying by \(n-1\) puts it on the chi-square scale, whose mean is \(n-1\). A statistic far below \(n-1\) says the data are less variable than claimed; far above says more.

Two variances: the F test

For independent random samples from two normal populations,

\[F=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}\]

follows an \(F\) distribution with \((n_1-1,\ n_2-1)\) df when \(H_0:\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2\) is true. You met this distribution in week 10’s ANOVA slice. \(F\) near 1 means similar spread.

Which population is “1” is fixed by the question, before looking at the data. For \(H_1:\sigma_1^2>\sigma_2^2\), the p-value is the right tail, =F.DIST.RT(F, n1-1, n2-1). For \(H_1:\sigma_1^2\ne\sigma_2^2\), it is twice the smaller tail.

The catch: these tests need normal data, and more data does not help

Every test in weeks 9 and 10 leaned on the Central Limit Theorem: whatever the population’s shape, \(\bar{x}\) is close to normal once \(n\) is large enough. Nothing like that rescues \(s^2\). The distribution of \((n-1)s^2/\sigma^2\) is chi-square only for a normal population, and for a skewed or heavy-tailed population it is wrong at every sample size: its spread depends on how heavy the population’s tails are.

To see how much this matters, take the course’s Nevada income file, 100,000 simulated tax returns (AGI in tens of thousands of dollars), strongly right-skewed. Treat it as the population. What to do about it.

  1. Use the variance tests only for data you have reason to think are normal: measurements from a stable process (fill weights, temperatures, machined parts), not incomes, sales, or waiting times with a long right tail. Check first: a histogram, and the Chapter 6 tail check.
  2. Do not use the \(F\) test to choose between the pooled and Welch \(t\)-tests. Your textbook suggests it (the rat Your Turn). The \(F\) test is the less reliable of the two procedures, and choosing a test based on a pretest changes the error rates of the test you end up running. Use Welch, as in week 10. It is valid whether or not the variances are equal.
  3. When a variance question matters on non-normal data, more robust methods exist (Levene’s test in most software, or a bootstrap interval). They are beyond our textbook; ask Claude to run one and explain it.

Reading the Textbook: Corrections

  1. “A smaller chi-square test statistic … increases the likelihood that there is support for the null hypothesis” (Section 12.1). A small \(\chi^2\) means the data fail to contradict \(H_0\). It is not support for \(H_0\).
  2. The drive-through example sets \(\alpha=0.10\), then compares the p-value with 0.05 in its last step. The conclusion is the same either way, but use the \(\alpha\) you set.
  3. Southwest’s late flights. “By rejecting the null hypothesis, we have no evidence to conclude that the number of late flights … follows the binomial distribution” is garbled: rejecting \(H_0\) is the evidence that the binomial does not fit. And the book never says why it fails (weather makes a day’s flights dependent; see the exercise above).
  4. “More evidence supporting the use of the Poisson distribution than there is to not use it” (\(p=0.94\)), and similarly for the normal fit to SAT scores. A large p-value is not evidence for the model. The normal fit used 1 df on 40 scores and would miss almost any departure.
  5. CHISQ.TEST is presented as equivalent to CHISQ.DIST.RT. It is only when no parameter was estimated (\(m=0\)).
  6. “Always assign the larger sample variance to Population 1” (Section 13.2). Fine for a two-tailed test; for a one-tailed test, the direction comes from the question (above).
  7. Using the \(F\) test to decide whether to pool (the rat Your Turn). Don’t pretest; use Welch.
  8. The waiting-line Focus on Analytics box gives a variance “of 7.2 minutes” and says times “would vary by less than 7.2 minutes.” A variance is in minutes²; the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{7.2}\approx2.7\) minutes.
  9. Typos. CHI.DIST.RT should be CHISQ.DIST.RT. The checkout example’s hand-computed variance is misprinted; Excel’s VAR.S value, 2.1464, is correct.
  10. The refrigerator: “choosing to find support for the null hypothesis.” This is accepting \(H_0\). The interval for \(\sigma\) above shows how little 30 readings pin down.
  11. The book’s normality warning is right and understated. It calls the normal requirement “fairly rigid.” The AGI simulation shows a nominal 5% test rejecting a true null about 21% of the time at every sample size.

# Part I: Chi-Square Tests (Chapter 12)

## The chi-square statistic

::: {.blue}
For counts in $k$ cells, the **chi-square statistic** is

$$\chi^2=\sum\frac{(f_o-f_e)^2}{f_e},$$

where $f_o$ is the **observed** count in a cell and $f_e$ is the
**expected** count if $H_0$ is true. Expected counts need not be whole
numbers; they are averages.

If $H_0$ is true, $\chi^2$ follows (approximately) a **chi-square
distribution**, which is right-skewed, never negative, and has mean equal to
its degrees of freedom. The approximation needs **every expected count to be
at least 5**; if one is not, combine categories.
:::

```{python}
#| label: chi2-curves
cx = np.linspace(0.01, 20, 600)
_ = density_chart(cx, {"df = 1": chi2_pdf(cx, 1), "df = 2": chi2_pdf(cx, 2),
                       "df = 4": chi2_pdf(cx, 4), "df = 8": chi2_pdf(cx, 8)},
                  title="Chi-square distributions: right-skewed, mean = df",
                  xlab="chi-square", ymax=0.5)
```

::: {.charcoal}
Three things follow from the formula.

+ **Every test is upper-tailed.** Squaring makes a cell count too high and a
  cell count too low both add to $\chi^2$. Only a *large* $\chi^2$ is
  evidence against $H_0$, so the p-value is always the right-tail area,
  Excel's `=CHISQ.DIST.RT(x, df)`.
+ **Each cell's contribution is scaled by what was expected.** Being 10 off
  when 20 were expected matters more than being 10 off when 200 were.
+ **A quick sanity check:** since the mean is the df, a $\chi^2$ near its df is
  entirely ordinary. A value of 5 with 4 df is nothing; a value of 30 with 4
  df is a lot.
:::

statistic. Below, a model with six categories is assumed true, 240

time.

## Test 1: are k proportions equal?

::: {.blue}
To test $H_0:p_1=p_2=\cdots=p_k$, pool all the groups to estimate the common
proportion $\bar{p}$, and expect $\bar{p}\times n_j$ successes (and
$(1-\bar{p})\times n_j$ failures) in group $j$. The statistic has
$\text{df}=k-1$.
:::

## Test 2: goodness of fit

::: {.blue}
A **goodness-of-fit test** asks whether observed counts follow a stated
probability distribution. $H_0$ says they do. The expected count in each
category is $n\times$ the probability the model gives it, and

$$\text{df}=k-m-1,$$

where $k$ is the number of categories and $m$ is the number of parameters
**estimated from the sample** to compute those probabilities ($m=0$ if $H_0$
states the model completely). Each estimated parameter lets the model
bend toward the data, and costs one degree of freedom.
:::

## Test 3: are two categorical variables independent?

::: {.blue}
A **contingency table** cross-classifies $n$ observations by two categorical
variables, with $r$ rows and $c$ columns. The **test of independence** has

$$H_0:\ \text{the row and column variables are independent},$$

and, if $H_0$ is true, the expected count in a cell is

$$f_e=\frac{(\text{row total})\times(\text{column total})}{n},
\qquad \text{df}=(r-1)(c-1).$$
:::

::: {.charcoal}
**Where the formula comes from.** Chapter 4 said events $A$ and $B$ are
independent when $P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)\,P(B)$. Estimate each probability
from the margins, $P(\text{row})=\text{row total}/n$ and
$P(\text{column})=\text{column total}/n$, and multiply by $n$:
$n\cdot\frac{\text{row}}{n}\cdot\frac{\text{column}}{n}=\frac{\text{row}\times\text{column}}{n}$.
Read it another way: if the column a customer falls in has nothing to do with
the row, every row should split across the columns in the same proportions
as the whole sample does. The test of independence is Chapter 4's
definition, checked with data.
:::

### How strong is the dependence? Cramér's V

The $\chi^2$ statistic grows with $n$: double every count and $\chi^2$
doubles, though the pattern is unchanged. So, as in weeks 9 and 10, a small
p-value says the association is *real*, not that it is *large*. The effect
size for a contingency table is **Cramér's V**:

::: {.blue}
$$V=\sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n\,\big(\min(r,c)-1\big)}},$$

which runs from 0 (independence) to 1 (each row predicts the column
perfectly). A rough guide for tables with a shorter side of 2 or 3: about
0.1 is small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large.
:::

| Test | $H_0$ | Expected count $f_e$ | df |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| $k$ proportions | $p_1=\cdots=p_k$ | $\bar{p}\,n_j$ and $(1-\bar{p})\,n_j$ | $k-1$ |
| Goodness of fit | the counts follow a stated model | $n\times$ model probability | $k-m-1$ |
| Independence | rows and columns are independent | row × column / $n$ | $(r-1)(c-1)$ |

and every $f_e$ should be at least 5. The $k$-proportions test is in fact a
test of independence on a $2\times k$ table (success/failure by group), so
the first and third rows always give the same answer.
:::

# Part II: Tests for a Population Variance (Chapter 13)

## One variance

::: {.blue}
If the population is **normal**, the statistic

$$\chi^2=\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2}$$

follows a chi-square distribution with $n-1$ df when $H_0:\sigma^2=\sigma_0^2$
is true. Unlike Chapter 12, the test can be lower-tailed, upper-tailed, or
two-tailed, depending on $H_1$. A claim about a standard deviation is tested
as a claim about its square: $\sigma<2$ minutes is $\sigma^2<4$
minutes².
:::

::: {.charcoal}
The intuition: $s^2/\sigma_0^2$ is the ratio of the variance you saw to the
variance claimed, and should be near 1 if the claim is right. Multiplying by
$n-1$ puts it on the chi-square scale, whose mean is $n-1$. A statistic far
below $n-1$ says the data are less variable than claimed; far above says more.
:::

### Application: refrigerator temperatures (two tails), and an interval for $\sigma$

Amana wants a refrigerator model whose temperature has a standard deviation
of **4.0°F**: more variation freezes or spoils food, less means the
compressor cycles too often. A test unit records 30 temperatures. Test at

$$H_0:\sigma^2=16\qquad H_1:\sigma^2\ne16.$$


::: {.charcoal}

between the two critical values, and the two-sided p-value (twice the
smaller tail) is `{python} f"{am['p-value']:.3f}"`. **The data are not
evidence that the standard deviation differs from 4.0°F.**
:::

How close to 4.0°F is the standard deviation, then? A confidence interval
answers that. Because the chi-square distribution is skewed, the interval
is **not** "estimate ± margin":

::: {.blue}
For a normal population, a $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval for $\sigma$ is

$$\sqrt{\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{\alpha/2,\ \text{upper}}}}\ \le\ \sigma\ \le\
\sqrt{\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\chi^2_{\alpha/2,\ \text{lower}}}},$$

where the two $\chi^2$ values cut off $\alpha/2$ in each tail with $n-1$ df.
In Excel they are `=CHISQ.INV.RT(alpha/2, n-1)` and `=CHISQ.INV(alpha/2, n-1)`.
:::

```{python}

numbers(am_ci)
```

::: {.scarlet}
**Failing to reject is not showing that $\sigma=4$.** Your textbook
concludes that "choosing to find support for the null hypothesis … is the
appropriate business decision" and that the design "is working properly."
The 95% interval says $\sigma$ could be anywhere from
`{python} f"{am_ci['lower limit for sigma']:.2f}"`°F to
`{python} f"{am_ci['upper limit for sigma']:.2f}"`°F. At the top of that range,
the refrigerator's temperature varies about 50% more than the target. Thirty
readings cannot tell a good design from a mediocre one. The business
decision might still be to ship, but the honest summary is "we found no
problem, and our test could not have found a moderate one." If the
decision matters, collect more data, and decide in advance how far from
4.0°F would be unacceptable.
:::

## Two variances: the F test

::: {.blue}
For independent random samples from two **normal** populations,

$$F=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}$$

follows an $F$ distribution with $(n_1-1,\ n_2-1)$ df when
$H_0:\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2$ is true. You met this distribution in week 10's
ANOVA slice. $F$ near 1 means similar spread.

**Which population is "1" is fixed by the question, before looking at the
data.** For $H_1:\sigma_1^2>\sigma_2^2$, the p-value is the right tail,
`=F.DIST.RT(F, n1-1, n2-1)`. For $H_1:\sigma_1^2\ne\sigma_2^2$, it is twice
the smaller tail.
:::

Blue Cross Blue Shield (BCBS) compares the variability of the length of stay

**Your textbook's rule, "always put the larger sample variance in the
numerator and do a right-tail test," is safe only for two-tailed tests.**
For a two-tailed test, larger-on-top with $\alpha/2$ in the right tail gives
exactly the p-value above. For a **one-tailed** test, it lets the data choose
the direction, the tail-picking problem from week 9. In the Taco Bell case
the question and the data happen to agree. Had the outsourced sample come out
*more* variable, the rule would have flipped the populations and tested a
question the owner never asked. Set the direction from the question. If the
data go the other way, $F<1$ and you simply do not reject.
:::

## The catch: these tests need normal data, and more data does not help

Every test in weeks 9 and 10 leaned on the Central Limit Theorem: whatever
the population's shape, $\bar{x}$ is close to normal once $n$ is large
enough. **Nothing like that rescues $s^2$.** The distribution of
$(n-1)s^2/\sigma^2$ is chi-square only for a normal population, and for a
skewed or heavy-tailed population it is wrong at every sample size: its
spread depends on how heavy the population's tails are.

To see how much this matters, take the course's Nevada income file,
`{python} f"{len(agi):,}"` simulated tax returns (AGI in tens of thousands of
dollars), strongly right-skewed. Treat it as the population. Draw 20,000
samples of size $n$, test the **true** variance at $\alpha=0.05$ two-sided,
and count how often the test rejects. Every rejection is a false positive,
so a valid test should reject 5% of the time. For comparison, do the same
with a $t$-test of the true mean, and with a normal population.

```{python}
#| label: robust-sim
sizes = [10, 20, 30, 50, 100, 200, 400]
rows = []
for n_ in sizes:
    a_ = variance_test_error_rate(agi.values, n_, reps=20_000)
    z_ = variance_test_error_rate(None, n_, reps=20_000)
    rows.append({"n": n_,
                 "variance test, AGI": a_["variance test rejects a true H0"],
                 "t-test, AGI": a_["t-test rejects a true H0"],
                 "variance test, normal": z_["variance test rejects a true H0"],
                 "t-test, normal": z_["t-test rejects a true H0"]})
robust = pd.DataFrame(rows).set_index("n")
robust.round(3)
```

```{python}
#| label: robust-chart
_ = line_chart(robust.index, {c: robust[c] for c in robust.columns},
               title="How often a 5% test rejects a TRUE null",
               xlab="sample size n", ylab="false-positive rate",
               hlines=[0.05], ylim=(0, 0.3))
```

::: {.charcoal}
With normal data, both tests reject about 5% of the time, as advertised. With
the skewed income data, the $t$-test starts too high
(`{python} pct(robust.loc[10, 't-test, AGI'], 1)` at $n=10$) and settles to
about 5% as $n$ grows, the Central Limit Theorem at work. **The variance
test does not settle.** It rejects a true null about
`{python} pct(robust.loc[30, 'variance test, AGI'], 0)` of the time at
$n=30$ and still `{python} pct(robust.loc[400, 'variance test, AGI'], 0)`
at $n=400$: four times the advertised rate, and a larger sample buys
nothing. The $F$ test, built from two sample variances, has the same
weakness.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
**What to do about it.**

1. **Use the variance tests only for data you have reason to think are
   normal:** measurements from a stable process (fill weights,
   temperatures, machined parts), not incomes, sales, or waiting times with
   a long right tail. Check first: a histogram, and the Chapter 6 tail
   check.
2. **Do not use the $F$ test to choose between the pooled and Welch
   $t$-tests.** Your textbook suggests it (the rat Your Turn). The $F$ test
   is the *less* reliable of the two procedures, and choosing a test based
   on a pretest changes the error rates of the test you end up running.
   Use Welch, as in week 10. It is valid whether or not the variances are
   equal.
3. When a variance question matters on non-normal data, more robust
   methods exist (Levene's test in most software, or a bootstrap interval).
   They are beyond our textbook; ask Claude to run one and explain it.
:::

# Reading the Textbook: Corrections

::: {.scarlet}
1. **"A smaller chi-square test statistic … increases the likelihood that
   there is support for the null hypothesis"** (Section 12.1). A small
   $\chi^2$ means the data fail to contradict $H_0$. It is not support for
   $H_0$.
2. **The drive-through example sets $\alpha=0.10$**, then compares the

   way, but use the $\alpha$ you set.
