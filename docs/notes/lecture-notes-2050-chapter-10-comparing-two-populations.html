Objectives

  • Choose the right two-sample procedure from the design: independent samples, paired samples, or two proportions.
  • Compare two means from independent samples with Welch’s \(t\)-test (the default) and know when the pooled test applies.
  • Explain why pairing makes a test more powerful, and analyse matched pairs as a one-sample test on the differences.
  • Compare two proportions with a test (pooled) and an interval (unpooled).
  • Use “does the interval for the difference contain zero?” to read a two-sample result.
  • Size an A/B test, and read its result as a manager should.
  • Tell an experiment from an observational comparison, and say what a significant difference does and does not let you conclude.
  • Explain why testing many pairs of groups inflates false positives, and read a one-way ANOVA table.

Week 9 built one engine: an estimate, its standard error, and a reference distribution. This week every question compares two groups: federal vs. private salaries, end-aisle vs. middle-aisle displays, version A of a web page vs. version B. The engine does not change. Every test in the chapter has the same shape:

\[\frac{(\text{difference in the estimates})-(\text{difference claimed in }H_0)}{\text{standard error of the difference}} .\]

What changes is the standard error, and the design of the study decides which one. Were the two groups sampled independently? Was the same store or customer measured twice? Are we comparing averages or percentages? Get the design right and the formula follows. Get it wrong and a real effect can vanish, as the Rockstar example shows.

The note ends with a slice of Chapter 11: what goes wrong when you compare many groups two at a time, and the one test (ANOVA) built to avoid it.

Two Independent Samples

The sampling distribution of a difference

For two independent samples, where what is drawn from one population has no bearing on what is drawn from the other, the difference in sample means \(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2\) has

\[\mu_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}=\mu_1-\mu_2,\qquad \sigma_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} .\]

The second quantity is the standard error of the difference. Variances add, even though the means subtract: two uncertain numbers make their difference more uncertain, never less.

Show the code 
rng = np.random.default_rng(20261022)
x1 = rng.normal(8_025, 225, (100_000, 100)).mean(axis=1)
x2 = rng.normal(8_000, 225, (100_000, 100)).mean(axis=1)
sim_se = (x1 - x2).std()
formula_se = sqrt(225 ** 2 / 100 + 225 ** 2 / 100)
_ = density_histogram(x1 - x2, bins=60,
                      x=np.linspace(-130, 180, 400),
                      density=normal_pdf(np.linspace(-130, 180, 400), 25, formula_se),
                      title="100,000 differences in sample means (n = 100 bulbs per line)",
                      xlab="x-bar 1 - x-bar 2 (hours)", curve_label="normal, SE from the formula")

Two production lines of light bulbs, with true means 8,025 and 8,000 hours and \(\sigma=225\) on each line. The simulated differences have standard deviation 31.88; the formula gives \(\sqrt{225^2/100+225^2/100}=\) 31.82. Notice how often the difference comes out negative even though line 1 really is better. A 25-hour edge is barely one standard error, which is why the old version of this note found a Type II error with exactly these settings.

Define population 1 and population 2 before writing any hypothesis. The choice is arbitrary, but every sign that follows depends on it. With federal workers as population 1, “federal pays more” is \(\mu_1-\mu_2>0\); reverse the labels and it becomes \(\mu_1-\mu_2<0\). Most sign errors in this chapter start here.

Sigmas unknown: Welch’s test is the default

In practice the population standard deviations are unknown, and the sample standard deviations take their place. That brings in the \(t\)-distribution, and a choice.

Welch’s two-sample \(t\)-test (unequal variances) uses

\[t=\frac{(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)-(\mu_1-\mu_2)_{H_0}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}\]

with degrees of freedom from the Welch formula (the textbook’s Formula 10.11, which software computes for you). It makes no assumption that the two populations have the same variance.

The pooled \(t\)-test assumes \(\sigma_1=\sigma_2\), combines the two sample variances into \(s_p^2=\dfrac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}\), and uses \(n_1+n_2-2\) degrees of freedom.

Use Welch unless you have a reason not to. When the variances really are equal, Welch gives nearly the same answer as the pooled test. When they are not, the pooled test can be badly wrong, especially with unequal sample sizes. Your textbook gives the same advice (“when in doubt … proceed with the unequal variances test”), and it is the default in most modern software. Excel offers both, side by side, in Data → Data Analysis: t-Test: Two-Sample Assuming Unequal Variances (Welch) and … Assuming Equal Variances (pooled).

Application: air conditioning or open windows?

A consumer group drives a Nissan Maxima on one tank of highway fuel, 10 drivers with the air conditioning on and windows up, 11 with the AC off and windows down. Is there a difference in fuel economy (mpg)? Population 1 is AC on.

Application: a pooled test, in an experiment

In the textbook’s rat study, rats from one population were assigned to an enriched environment or a barren one, and brain weights were compared after three months: enriched \(\bar{x}=2.6\), \(s=0.6\), \(n=20\); barren \(\bar{x}=2.1\), \(s=0.8\), \(n=25\). Does the interval for the difference contain zero?

A confidence interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) is

\[(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)\pm(\text{critical value})\times(\text{standard error of the difference}).\]

If it excludes 0, a two-tailed test at \(\alpha=1-\text{confidence}\) rejects “no difference”; if it includes 0, the test does not. This is week 9’s interval–test link, applied to a difference.

When both population standard deviations are known (rare, but it is where the textbook starts), the critical value comes from \(z\). A sample of 35 federal jobs averages $66,700 and 32 comparable private-sector jobs average $60,400, with \(\sigma_1=\$12{,}000\) and \(\sigma_2=\$11{,}000\) taken as known.

Paired Samples

Two samples are dependent, or paired, when each observation in one is matched to a specific observation in the other: the same store in two weeks, the same customer before and after, the same golfer with two balls. A matched-pairs test works with the differences \(d_i=x_{1i}-x_{2i}\) and runs a one-sample \(t\)-test on them:

\[t=\frac{\bar{d}-\mu_{d,H_0}}{s_d/\sqrt{n}},\qquad \text{df}=n-1,\]

where \(n\) is the number of pairs. Hypotheses are about \(\mu_d\), the population mean difference, never about \(\bar{d}\).

The same difference, 11.44 units, gives \(p=\) 0.030 analysed as pairs and \(p=\) 0.197 analysed as independent samples. One analysis finds the effect; the other misses it entirely.

The chart shows why. Store 3 sells about 100+ units whatever the placement; store 5 sells about 40. That store-to-store spread is huge, and an independent-samples test counts all of it as noise. But it is the same spread in both weeks: the two columns are strongly correlated (\(r=\) 0.84). Taking each store’s difference cancels it. In numbers,

\[\text{Var}(d)=s_1^2+s_2^2-2rs_1s_2 ,\]

which is 247 here, against 1535 if the pairing is ignored. Pairing removes variation you already know about, so the variation you care about can show through. It is the most powerful idea in this chapter, and it returns at the end of the note as blocking.

The analysis must match the design. Paired data analysed as independent throws away power, as above. Independent data analysed as paired is simply wrong (which observation would you subtract from which?). Ask: was each observation in one group matched to a specific observation in the other? If yes, pair.

A design flaw worth spotting. In the Rockstar study, every store ran the end-aisle week first. If demand was rising (warmer weather, a holiday, a promotion), the first week would look better for reasons that have nothing to do with placement. A better design flips a coin in each store to decide which placement goes first. Statistics can measure a difference; only the design can tell you what caused it.

Comparing Two Proportions

For \(x_1\) successes in \(n_1\) and \(x_2\) in \(n_2\), with \(\hat{p}_1=x_1/n_1\) and \(\hat{p}_2=x_2/n_2\):

Test of \(H_0:p_1=p_2\). \(H_0\) says there is one common proportion, so estimate it by pooling both samples, \(\bar{p}=\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2}\), and use

\[z=\frac{\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)}} .\]

Confidence interval for \(p_1-p_2\). The interval claims nothing, so each sample keeps its own proportion:

\[(\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2)\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}} .\]

This is the two-sample version of week 9’s rule: the test uses what \(H_0\) claims, the interval uses what the data show. In week 9 that meant \(p_0\) for the test and \(\hat{p}\) for the interval; here it means the pooled \(\bar{p}\) for the test and the separate \(\hat{p}\)’s for the interval.

Experiments and Observational Comparisons

In an experiment, the investigator assigns the units to the groups, ideally at random. In an observational comparison, the groups come as they are: the investigator only records which group each unit belongs to.

Example Who chose the group? What a significant difference shows
Rats, enriched vs. barren cages the researchers, at random enrichment causes heavier brains
Rockstar, end vs. middle aisle Josh, but always end-aisle first placement probably matters; order is a rival explanation
Federal vs. private salaries the workers themselves the averages differ; not why (education, location, job mix)
Good students vs. others the students themselves the accident rates differ; not that grades cause safe driving
A/B test of two web pages random assignment of visitors the page version causes the difference in conversions

A two-sample test tells you whether the groups differ, not why. Random assignment is what licenses “causes”: it makes the groups alike, on average, in every respect except the one you changed, including the ones you never measured. Without it, every difference comes with rival explanations, and a tiny p-value does nothing to rule them out. This is the most important judgment call you will make with this chapter’s tools, and it returns in the regression weeks as “correlation is not causation.”

A/B Testing

An A/B test is a two-proportion test run as an experiment: visitors are randomly shown version A or version B of a page, an email, or an offer, and the conversion rates are compared. It is the most common use of this chapter in business today, and the random assignment is what makes its conclusions causal.

Three rules for reading an A/B test.

  1. Fix the sample size in advance, and don’t stop when \(p\) first dips below 0.05. Checking every day and stopping at the first “significant” reading is a way of running many tests and keeping the best one; the false positive rate climbs far above 5%.

  3. One primary metric, decided in advance. Testing ten metrics and reporting the one that “won” is the multiple comparisons problem at the end of this note.

Reading the Textbook: Corrections

  1. “If the populations are not normally distributed, the \(t\)-distribution can still be used … if both sample sizes are 30 or more. This is because the shape of the \(t\)-distribution approaches the normal.” Large samples help because the Central Limit Theorem acts on \(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2\), not because \(t\) becomes \(z\). Strongly skewed data may need more than 30 per group (week 9’s income simulation).
  2. Welch degrees of freedom “rounded up … is a Type I error.” Rounding up would raise the Type I error rate slightly; it is not itself an error. Software uses the exact fraction, Excel rounds to the nearest whole number, the book rounds down. Any of the three is acceptable.
  3. Rockstar’s design. End-aisle always came first, so a trend in demand is a rival explanation (above).
  4. Causal language on observational data. The federal-salary and good-student conclusions show that the groups differ, not why.
  5. “Because α = 0.05 is within this range, we can reject” (Rockstar, Step 8). The table brackets \(p\) between 0.025 and 0.05; the exact value, 0.030, settles it. Use exact p-values from Excel.

The remedies are to decide on the comparison in advance, to use one test built for many groups (ANOVA), or to demand a stricter \(\alpha\) for each comparison (the Bonferroni rule divides \(\alpha\) by the number of tests, the last column above).

Idea 2: one-way ANOVA compares many means with one test

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests

\[H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k\qquad H_1:\text{not all the means are equal}\]

with a single statistic,

\[F=\frac{\text{variation between the group means (per df)}}{\text{variation within the groups (per df)}}=\frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}} .\]

If \(H_0\) is true, the group means differ only by noise and \(F\) is usually near 1. A large \(F\) says the means differ by more than the noise within the groups can explain. The test is always upper-tailed.

# Two Independent Samples

## The sampling distribution of a difference

::: {.blue}
For two **independent samples**, where what is drawn from one population
has no bearing on what is drawn from the other, the difference in sample
means $\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2$ has

$$\mu_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}=\mu_1-\mu_2,\qquad
\sigma_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} .$$

The second quantity is the **standard error of the difference**. Variances
add, even though the means subtract: two uncertain numbers make their
difference *more* uncertain, never less.
:::

## Sigmas unknown: Welch's test is the default

In practice the population standard deviations are unknown, and the sample
standard deviations take their place. That brings in the $t$-distribution,
and a choice.

::: {.blue}
**Welch's two-sample $t$-test** (unequal variances) uses

$$t=\frac{(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)-(\mu_1-\mu_2)_{H_0}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}$$

with degrees of freedom from the Welch formula (the textbook's Formula
10.11, which software computes for you). It makes **no assumption** that the
two populations have the same variance.

The **pooled $t$-test** assumes $\sigma_1=\sigma_2$, combines the two
sample variances into
$s_p^2=\dfrac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}$, and uses
$n_1+n_2-2$ degrees of freedom.
:::

::: {.charcoal}
**Use Welch unless you have a reason not to.** When the variances really
are equal, Welch gives nearly the same answer as the pooled test. When they
are not, the pooled test can be badly wrong, especially with unequal sample
sizes. Your textbook gives the same advice ("when in doubt … proceed with
the unequal variances test"), and it is the default in most modern
software. Excel offers both, side by side, in **Data → Data Analysis**:
*t-Test: Two-Sample Assuming Unequal Variances* (Welch) and *… Assuming
Equal Variances* (pooled).
:::

`{python} f"{maxima['df']:.2f}"` degrees of freedom, and the two-tailed
p-value is `{python} f"{maxima['p-value']:.4f}"`. Above 0.05: **the data are
not sufficient evidence that fuel economy differs** between the two ways of
driving. The 95% interval for the difference,
`{python} f"{maxima_ci['lower']:.2f}"` to
`{python} f"{maxima_ci['upper']:.2f}"` mpg, contains zero, which says the
same thing and shows how wide the uncertainty is.

Notice the two standard deviations: `{python} f"{maxima['s1']:.2f}"` and
`{python} f"{maxima['s2']:.2f}"`. Windows-down mileage is more than twice
as variable, so assuming equal variances would be hard to defend. (The
pooled test gives p = `{python} f"{maxima_pooled['p-value']:.4f}"` here,
the same conclusion.)
:::

::: {.brown}
**About those degrees of freedom.** The Welch formula gives a fraction,
`{python} f"{maxima['df']:.2f}"` here. Software uses the fraction. Excel's
Data Analysis tool rounds it to the nearest whole number (14). Your textbook
rounds *down* by hand (13), to be conservative. The three p-values differ
only in the third decimal place, so any of them is acceptable; on exams,
read the one the output gives you.
:::

### Application: a pooled test, in an experiment

## Does the interval for the difference contain zero?

::: {.blue}
A confidence interval for $\mu_1-\mu_2$ is

$$(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)\pm(\text{critical value})\times(\text{standard error of the difference}).$$

If it **excludes 0**, a two-tailed test at $\alpha=1-\text{confidence}$
rejects "no difference"; if it **includes 0**, the test does not. This is
week 9's interval–test link, applied to a difference.
:::

When both population standard deviations are known (rare, but it is where
the textbook starts), the critical value comes from $z$. A sample of 35
federal jobs averages \$66,700 and 32 comparable private-sector jobs average
\$60,400, with $\sigma_1=\$12{,}000$ and $\sigma_2=\$11{,}000$ taken as known.

```{python}
#| label: federal
fed = two_sample_z(66_700, 12_000, 35, 60_400, 11_000, 32, 0, "two-sided")
fed_ci = diff_interval(66_700, 12_000, 35, 60_400, 11_000, 32, 0.95, sigma_known=True)
pd.concat([numbers(fed), numbers(fed_ci).rename(columns={"value": "interval"})], axis=1).fillna("")
```

::: {.charcoal}
$z=$ `{python} f"{fed['test statistic z']:.2f}"`, $p=$
`{python} f"{fed['p-value']:.4f}"`, and the 95% interval for the difference
is `{python} money(fed_ci['lower'])` to `{python} money(fed_ci['upper'])`.
It excludes zero, so the test rejects. The interval says more than the
test: federal salaries are higher on average, by an amount we can pin down
only to somewhere between about \$800 and \$11,800. That is a wide range for
a policy argument, and it is the interval, not the p-value, that shows it.
:::

`{python} f"{ruby['p-value']:.4f}"` (Welch:
`{python} f"{ruby_w['p-value']:.4f}"`). Reject: **the data are evidence

# Paired Samples

::: {.blue}
Two samples are **dependent**, or **paired**, when each observation in one
is matched to a specific observation in the other: the same store in two
weeks, the same customer before and after, the same golfer with two balls.
A **matched-pairs test** works with the differences $d_i=x_{1i}-x_{2i}$ and
runs a **one-sample $t$-test** on them:

$$t=\frac{\bar{d}-\mu_{d,H_0}}{s_d/\sqrt{n}},\qquad \text{df}=n-1,$$

where $n$ is the number of **pairs**. Hypotheses are about $\mu_d$, the
population mean difference, never about $\bar{d}$.
:::

The same difference, `{python} f"{rock['mean difference d-bar']:.2f}"`

analysed as pairs and $p=$

The chart shows why. Store 3 sells about 100+ units whatever the placement;
store 5 sells about 40. That store-to-store spread is huge, and an
independent-samples test counts all of it as noise. But it is the *same*
spread in both weeks: the two columns are strongly correlated
($r=$ `{python} f"{pv['r']:.2f}"`). Taking each store's difference cancels

$$\text{Var}(d)=s_1^2+s_2^2-2rs_1s_2 ,$$

which is `{python} f"{pv['Var(d) = sum']:.0f}"` here, against
`{python} f"{pv['Var(d) if the pairing is ignored (r = 0)']:.0f}"` if the

the variation you care about can show through.** It is the most powerful
idea in this chapter, and it returns at the end of the note as *blocking*.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
**The analysis must match the design.** Paired data analysed as
independent throws away power, as above. Independent data analysed as
paired is simply wrong (which observation would you subtract from which?).
Ask: was each observation in one group matched to a *specific* observation
in the other? If yes, pair.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
**A design flaw worth spotting.** In the Rockstar study, every store ran the
end-aisle week *first*. If demand was rising (warmer weather, a holiday, a
promotion), the first week would look better for reasons that have nothing
to do with placement. A better design flips a coin in each store to decide
which placement goes first. Statistics can measure a difference; only the
design can tell you what caused it.
:::

# Comparing Two Proportions

::: {.blue}
For $x_1$ successes in $n_1$ and $x_2$ in $n_2$, with
$\hat{p}_1=x_1/n_1$ and $\hat{p}_2=x_2/n_2$:

**Test of $H_0:p_1=p_2$.** $H_0$ says there is one common proportion, so
estimate it by pooling both samples, $\bar{p}=\dfrac{x_1+x_2}{n_1+n_2}$, and use

$$z=\frac{\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\bar{p}(1-\bar{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)}} .$$

**Confidence interval for $p_1-p_2$.** The interval claims nothing, so each
sample keeps its own proportion:

$$(\hat{p}_1-\hat{p}_2)\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1-\hat{p}_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat{p}_2(1-\hat{p}_2)}{n_2}} .$$
:::

::: {.charcoal}
This is the two-sample version of week 9's rule: **the test uses what $H_0$
claims, the interval uses what the data show.** In week 9 that meant $p_0$
for the test and $\hat{p}$ for the interval; here it means the pooled
$\bar{p}$ for the test and the separate $\hat{p}$'s for the interval.
:::

# Experiments and Observational Comparisons

::: {.blue}
In an **experiment**, the investigator assigns the units to the groups,
ideally at random. In an **observational comparison**, the groups come as
they are: the investigator only records which group each unit belongs to.
:::

| Example | Who chose the group? | What a significant difference shows |
|---|---|---|
| Rats, enriched vs. barren cages | the researchers, at random | enrichment **causes** heavier brains |
| Rockstar, end vs. middle aisle | Josh, but always end-aisle first | placement *probably* matters; order is a rival explanation |
| Federal vs. private salaries | the workers themselves | the averages differ; not *why* (education, location, job mix) |
| Good students vs. others | the students themselves | the accident rates differ; not that grades cause safe driving |
| A/B test of two web pages | random assignment of visitors | the page version **causes** the difference in conversions |

::: {.scarlet}
**A two-sample test tells you whether the groups differ, not why.** Random
assignment is what licenses "causes": it makes the groups alike, on average,
in every respect except the one you changed, including the ones you never
measured. Without it, every difference comes with rival explanations, and a
tiny p-value does nothing to rule them out. This is the most important
judgment call you will make with this chapter's tools, and it returns in
the regression weeks as "correlation is not causation."
:::

# A/B Testing

An **A/B test** is a two-proportion test run as an experiment: visitors are
randomly shown version A or version B of a page, an email, or an offer, and
the conversion rates are compared. It is the most common use of this
chapter in business today, and the random assignment is what makes its
conclusions causal.

   positive rate climbs far above 5%.
2. **Report the interval for the lift, not just the p-value.** "B converts
   0.3 to 2.1 points better" supports a decision; "$p=0.03$" does not.
3. **One primary metric, decided in advance.** Testing ten metrics and
   reporting the one that "won" is the multiple comparisons problem at the
   end of this note.
:::

# A Test With No Formula: Shuffling the Labels

If the group labels made no difference, the 21 Maxima mileages could have
been split into "AC on" and "windows down" in any way at all. So shuffle the
labels thousands of times, recompute the difference in means each time, and
see how often chance alone produces a gap as big as the real one.

```{python}
#| label: perm
perm = permutation_test(MAXIMA_AC, MAXIMA_WINDOWS, reps=20_000, alternative="two-sided")
shuffled = perm.attrs["shuffled differences"]
_ = density_histogram(shuffled, bins=50,
                      title="20,000 shuffles of the labels (if H0 is true)",
                      xlab="difference in means after shuffling (mpg)",
                      bar_label="shuffled differences",
                      mark=[perm["observed difference"], -perm["observed difference"]])
tidy(perm).to_frame("Maxima, two-sided")
```

::: {.charcoal}
`{python} pct(perm['permutation p-value'], 1)` of the shuffles produce a
difference at least as large as the observed
`{python} f"{perm['observed difference']:.2f}"` mpg in either direction.
That share *is* a p-value, and it agrees closely with Welch's
`{python} f"{maxima['p-value']:.4f}"`. No formula, no normality
assumption, no degrees of freedom: just the definition of a p-value, done by
brute force. When a formula and a permutation test agree, you can trust
both; when they disagree (small, skewed samples), trust the permutation test.
:::

# Reading the Textbook: Corrections

::: {.scarlet}
1. **"If the populations are not normally distributed, the $t$-distribution
   can still be used … if both sample sizes are 30 or more. This is because
   the shape of the $t$-distribution approaches the normal."** Large samples
   help because the Central Limit Theorem acts on $\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2$, not
   because $t$ becomes $z$. Strongly skewed data may need more than 30 per
   group (week 9's income simulation).
2. **Welch degrees of freedom "rounded up … is a Type I error."** Rounding
   up would raise the Type I error *rate* slightly; it is not itself an
   error. Software uses the exact fraction, Excel rounds to the nearest
   whole number, the book rounds down. Any of the three is acceptable.
3. **Rockstar's design.** End-aisle always came first, so a trend in demand
   is a rival explanation (above).
4. **Causal language on observational data.** The federal-salary and
   good-student conclusions show that the groups differ, not why.
5. **"Because α = 0.05 is within this range, we can reject"** (Rockstar,
   Step 8). The table brackets $p$ between 0.025 and 0.05; the exact value,
   0.030, settles it. Use exact p-values from Excel.
:::

# Many Groups: A Slice of Chapter 11

Chapter 11 extends this week's question to three or more groups: three
store layouts, four suppliers, five ad campaigns. We will not compute its
sums of squares by hand. Three ideas from it are worth your time.

## Idea 1: comparing many pairs inflates false positives

The obvious approach to four groups is to run a $t$-test on every pair.
Four groups make six pairs, each tested at $\alpha=0.05$. If all four
population means are in fact equal, what is the chance that at least one of
the six tests cries wolf?

```{python}
#| label: familywise
fw = familywise_error()
fw.round(4)
```

```{python}
#| label: familywise-sim
fw_sim = simulate_pairwise_false_positives(k=4, n=20, reps=5_000)
fw_sim.to_frame("4 identical groups of 20, simulated 5,000 times")
```

::: {.charcoal}
Your textbook's arithmetic: with six independent tests, the chance of at
least one false positive is $1-0.95^6=$
`{python} f"{fw.loc[4, 'P(at least one false positive)']:.3f}"`. With ten
groups (45 pairs) it is
`{python} pct(fw.loc[10, 'P(at least one false positive)'], 0)`. The
simulation draws four groups from the *same* population, so every
"significant" difference is false, and still finds at least one significant
pair `{python} pct(fw_sim["share with >= 1 'significant' pair"], 1)` of the
time. (The six tests share data, so they are not independent and the rate
is a little below the formula's.) One ANOVA test on the same data rejects
`{python} pct(fw_sim['share where ANOVA rejects'], 1)` of the time, as it
should.

This is week 9's false-positive problem scaled up, and it is everywhere in
business: dashboards that flag "significant" changes in dozens of metrics,
A/B tests read segment by segment, "we sliced the data twelve ways and found
something." **The more comparisons you make, the more false positives you
should expect.** The remedies are to decide on the comparison in advance,
to use one test built for many groups (ANOVA), or to demand a stricter
$\alpha$ for each comparison (the Bonferroni rule divides $\alpha$ by the
number of tests, the last column above).
:::

## Idea 2: one-way ANOVA compares many means with one test

::: {.blue}
**One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)** tests

$$H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_k\qquad H_1:\text{not all the means are equal}$$

with a single statistic,

$$F=\frac{\text{variation between the group means (per df)}}{\text{variation within the groups (per df)}}=\frac{MS_{\text{between}}}{MS_{\text{within}}} .$$

If $H_0$ is true, the group means differ only by noise and $F$ is usually
near 1. A large $F$ says the means differ by more than the noise within the
groups can explain. The test is always upper-tailed.
:::

The textbook's airfare data record 30 fares between Chicago and London, 10
on each of three airlines (half of each airline's fares in each direction). Do average fares differ by airline?

```{python}
#| label: anova-strip
_ = strip_chart(airfare, "fare", "airline", title="Thirty fares by airline (black bar = mean)",
                ylab="Fare (dollars)")
```

```{python}
#| label: anova-one
summ, aov = one_way_anova({a: g["fare"].to_numpy()
                           for a, g in airfare.groupby("airline", sort=False)})
summ.round(1)
```

```{python}
#| label: anova-table
aov.round(4).fillna("")
```

::: {.charcoal}
This is the layout of Excel's **Data → Data Analysis → Anova: Single
Factor**. Read it in three steps. **Between groups** is how far the three
airline means sit from the overall mean; **within groups** is how much fares
vary inside each airline; $F$ is their ratio per degree of freedom, here
`{python} f"{aov.loc['Between Groups', 'F']:.2f}"`. The p-value is
`{python} f"{aov.loc['Between Groups', 'P-value']:.4f}"`, just above 0.05
(equivalently, $F$ is just below the critical value
`{python} f"{aov.loc['Between Groups', 'F crit']:.2f}"`). **The data are
not quite sufficient evidence that average fares differ by airline.**

Two cautions. A rejection says only that *some* means differ, not which
ones; a follow-up procedure (the textbook's Tukey–Kramer test) answers
that while holding the overall false positive rate at $\alpha$. And ANOVA
assumes roughly normal data with similar spreads in each group, the same
conditions as the pooled $t$-test.
:::

```{python}
#| label: anova-t2
#| include: false
route_groups = {r: g["fare"].to_numpy() for r, g in airfare.groupby("route")}
_, aov_route = one_way_anova(route_groups)
t_route = two_sample_t_from_data(*route_groups.values(), pooled=True)
```

::: {.brown}
**ANOVA with two groups is the pooled $t$-test.** Run a one-way ANOVA on the
same 30 fares grouped by *route* (Chicago–London vs. London–Chicago):
$F=$ `{python} f"{aov_route.loc['Between Groups', 'F']:.4f}"`. The pooled
two-sample $t$ on the same data is
`{python} f"{t_route['test statistic t']:.4f}"`, and $t^2=$
`{python} f"{t_route['test statistic t'] ** 2:.4f}"`. Same test, same
p-value (`{python} f"{aov_route.loc['Between Groups', 'P-value']:.4f}"`).
ANOVA is this week's test, generalized to more than two groups.
:::

## Idea 3: blocking is pairing for many groups

Look at the strip chart again, now coloured by the direction of travel.

```{python}
#| label: route-gap
#| include: false
_cell = airfare.groupby(["airline", "route"])["fare"].mean().unstack()
route_gap = _cell["Chicago-London"] - _cell["London-Chicago"]
```

```{python}
#| label: block-strip
_ = strip_chart(airfare, "fare", "airline", color_by="route",
                title="The same fares, coloured by route", ylab="Fare (dollars)")
```

::: {.charcoal}
Inside every airline, the Chicago–London fares average well above the
London–Chicago fares, by `{python} money(route_gap.min())` to
`{python} money(route_gap.max())`. One-way ANOVA counted that route-to-route spread as
noise, exactly as the independent-samples test counted Rockstar's
store-to-store spread as noise. Treat route as a **block**, a known source
of variation to be removed, and test airline again:
:::

```{python}
#| label: block-table
blk = block_anova(airfare, "fare", "airline", "route")
tidy(blk.astype(float).round(4), 4).fillna("")
```

::: {.charcoal}
With route removed from the error term, the airline effect has $F=$
`{python} f"{blk.loc['airline (factor)', 'F']:.2f}"` and $p=$
`{python} f"{blk.loc['airline (factor)', 'P-value']:.4f}"`: now **the data
are evidence that average fares differ by airline.** Nothing about the
airlines changed between the two analyses; the noise got smaller. This is
the Rockstar lesson again. Pairing compared each store with itself;
blocking compares each airline within the same route. Your textbook calls
this design a *randomized block ANOVA*.
:::

::: {.brown}
**What we leave for later.** Chapter 11's two-way ANOVA also tests whether
the *airline* effect depends on the *route* (an **interaction**; here it
does not, $p\approx0.84$). Interactions are easier to understand as terms
in a regression model, and that is where we will meet them. The ANOVA
table itself also returns: every regression output you read in the coming
weeks begins with one, and its $F$-test asks the same question as here.
:::
