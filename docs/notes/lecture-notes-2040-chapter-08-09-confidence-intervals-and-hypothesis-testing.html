Objectives

  • Distinguish a point estimate from an interval estimate, and say what the “95%” in a 95% confidence interval does and does not mean.
  • Build and interpret a confidence interval for a mean (with \(t\), and with \(z\) when \(\sigma\) is known) and for a proportion.
  • Explain how the confidence level, the variability of the data, and the sample size each move the margin of error, and find the sample size a target margin requires.
  • State null and alternative hypotheses from a claim, before seeing the data, and choose between a one-tailed and a two-tailed test.
  • Define Type I and Type II errors as false positives and false negatives, with their probabilities \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).
  • Calculate and correctly interpret a p-value for a \(t\)-test of a mean and a \(z\)-test of a proportion.
  • Use the link between a confidence interval and a two-tailed test.
  • Calculate and interpret the power of a test, and say how \(\alpha\), the true effect, and \(n\) change it.
  • Tell statistical significance from practical importance.

Chapter 7 asked: if we knew the population, how would a sample statistic behave? These two chapters turn that question around. A manager holds one sample and wants to say something about the population she cannot see. She wants either a range for the unknown value (“average order size is between $118 and $141”) or a verdict on a claim (“the new kiosks really are faster than five minutes”).

Both answers come from one piece of machinery, built from the standard error of Chapter 7:

\[\text{estimate}\ \pm\ (\text{critical value})\times(\text{standard error}).\]

Read as a range, it is a confidence interval (Chapter 8). Solved for \(n\), it tells you how large a sample to take (Chapter 8). Rearranged as \((\text{estimate}-\text{claimed value})/\text{standard error}\), it is the test statistic of a hypothesis test, and its tail area is the p-value (Chapter 9). One engine, three uses. That is why we can cover two chapters in one week, and it is the thread to hold on to.

As before, you will not compute these by hand on an exam. You will be handed output, from Excel or from code, and asked what it means.

Part I: Confidence Intervals (Chapter 8)

Point estimates and interval estimates

A point estimate is a single number computed from a sample to estimate a population parameter: the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) for \(\mu\), the sample proportion \(\hat{p}\) for \(p\).

An interval estimate, or confidence interval, is a range of values, computed from the sample, constructed by a method that captures the parameter in a stated share of samples, the confidence level, such as 95%.

A point estimate is the best single guess, and it is almost certainly wrong: Chapter 7 showed that \(\bar{x}\) lands above or below \(\mu\) by a sampling error that changes from sample to sample. A point estimate says nothing about how wrong it is likely to be. The interval does, and its half-width has a name.

The margin of error is the distance from the point estimate to either end of a confidence interval:

\[\text{margin of error}=(\text{critical value})\times(\text{standard error}),\qquad \text{interval}=\text{estimate}\pm\text{margin of error}.\]

When \(\sigma\) is unknown: the Student’s \(t\)-distribution

When the sample standard deviation \(s\) replaces \(\sigma\), the standardized mean

\[t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{s/\sqrt{n}}\]

follows the Student’s \(t\)-distribution with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom (df), provided the population is normal. The degrees of freedom count the values free to vary once \(\bar{x}\) is fixed: knowing the mean and \(n-1\) of the values pins down the last one.

Properties of the \(t\)-distribution

  • Bell-shaped and symmetric around 0, with total area 1.
  • Flatter in the middle and heavier in the tails than the normal, because \(s\) itself varies from sample to sample and adds uncertainty.
  • A family of curves, one for each df. As df grows, \(s\) becomes a reliable stand-in for \(\sigma\) and \(t\) approaches the standard normal.
  • So the critical \(t\) is always larger than the critical \(z\) for the same confidence, and \(t\)-intervals are wider.

William Sealy Gosset worked out this distribution in 1908 while testing barley and hops for the Guinness brewery in Dublin, where samples were small and expensive. Guinness did not let its staff publish under their own names, so he signed the paper “Student.” The most-used small-sample method in statistics came out of quality control in a business.

What “95% confident” means

A confidence interval is not a probability statement about \(\mu\). Once the sample is drawn, the interval is fixed and so is \(\mu\): the interval either contains \(\mu\) or it does not. There is nothing left to be random. It is wrong to say “there is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is between $92,930 and $117,250.”

The confidence level describes the method, not any one interval: if we drew sample after sample and built an interval from each in the same way, 95% of those intervals would capture \(\mu\). The share that miss is \(\alpha\). “We are 95% confident” is shorthand for “this interval came from a method that works 95% of the time.”

We can watch the method work, because for the faculty we know \(\mu\). Each horizontal line below is the 95% \(t\)-interval from a fresh sample of 30 (drawn with replacement, so the population behaves as if it were infinite).

Repeat the experiment 100,000 times and the share that capture the truth settles down. From a normal population it is 95.1%, as promised. From the faculty salaries it is 94.6%, a little short of 95%, because salaries are right-skewed and 30 observations do not fully cure that. Keep that number in mind for the section after next.

The margin of error and its three levers

The margin of error is \((\text{critical value})\times s/\sqrt{n}\). Three things move it, and a manager controls two of them.

  1. The confidence level. Higher confidence means a larger critical value and a wider interval (the QVC table above).
  2. The variability of the data, \(s\). Noisier data, wider interval. You rarely control this, though a better measurement process or a stratified design (Chapter 7) can reduce it.
  3. The sample size, \(n\). Larger samples, narrower intervals, but through \(\sqrt{n}\): to halve the margin you need about four times the data.

When is the \(t\)-interval trustworthy?

The \(t\)-interval assumes the population is normal. The assumption matters most when \(n\) is small. As \(n\) grows, the Central Limit Theorem makes \(\bar{x}\) close to normal anyway, and the \(t\)-procedure becomes reliable for most populations, but how large is “large” depends on how skewed the population is.

The textbook’s rule of thumb is \(n\ge30\). The honest version is a simulation. A confidence interval for a proportion

For a population proportion, with \(x\) successes in a sample of \(n\) and \(\hat{p}=x/n\),

\[\hat{p}\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}} .\]

The normal approximation needs enough successes and failures: \(n\hat{p}\ge5\) and \(n(1-\hat{p})\ge5\) (many texts ask for 10; the stricter rule is safer).

How large a sample do you need?

This is the most practical result in Chapter 8. Set the margin of error you can live with, \(E\), and solve for \(n\).

For a mean: \(n=\left(\dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma}{E}\right)^2\).

For a proportion: \(n=\dfrac{z_{\alpha/2}^2\,p(1-p)}{E^2}\).

Always round up. Rounding down gives a margin slightly larger than you asked for. \(\sigma\) and \(p\) are planning values from a pilot study or past data. With no information about \(p\), use \(p=0.5\), which makes \(p(1-p)\) as large as it can be, so the sample is never too small.

Part II: Hypothesis Testing (Chapter 9)

The logic of a test

A hypothesis is a claim about a population parameter, such as a mean or a proportion.

The null hypothesis, \(H_0\), is the status quo or the claim being tested. It always contains the equality: \(=\), \(\le\), or \(\ge\) a specific value.

The alternative hypothesis, \(H_1\), is what we would conclude if the data are strong enough evidence against \(H_0\). It never contains the equality: \(\ne\), \(<\), or \(>\).

A hypothesis test is a trial. \(H_0\) is the defendant, presumed innocent. The data are the evidence. The verdict is reject \(H_0\) (guilty: the evidence is strong enough) or do not reject \(H_0\) (not guilty: it is not). A jury never declares a defendant innocent, only not guilty, because a lack of evidence is not proof of innocence.

We never “accept” \(H_0\). Failing to reject it means the data are consistent with \(H_0\), not that they show it is true. The same data are usually consistent with many other values too. Your textbook occasionally says a sample “supports the original assumption” after a failure to reject. Read that as “fails to contradict.”

Stating the hypotheses

Three claims, three setups.

Time on a web page. A study reported in the Wall Street Journal (Does the Internet Make You Smarter? and Does the Internet Make You Dumber?) says the average American looks at a web page for 56 seconds. A student wants to know whether UNLV students are different, in either direction:

\[H_0:\mu=56\qquad H_1:\mu\ne56\qquad\text{(two-tailed)}\]

Light bulb life. A lab wants evidence that a new bulb lasts longer than the industry’s 8,000 hours. The claim needing evidence goes in \(H_1\):

\[H_0:\mu\le8{,}000\qquad H_1:\mu>8{,}000\qquad\text{(one-tailed, upper)}\]

An 18-game NFL season. The commissioner says a majority of fans favor it. The players’ union, which opposes it, wants evidence that the commissioner is wrong:

\[H_0:p\ge0.5\qquad H_1:p<0.5\qquad\text{(one-tailed, lower)}\]

A two-tailed test has \(H_1:\ne\) and looks for a difference in either direction. A one-tailed test has \(H_1:<\) or \(H_1:>\) and looks in one direction only.

Choose the hypotheses and the tail before you look at the data. \(H_1\) is the claim that requires evidence, not the answer you are hoping for. A one-tailed p-value is half the two-tailed one, so choosing the tail after seeing which way the data point turns a two-tailed p of 0.08 into a one-tailed 0.04 and an “insignificant” result into a “significant” one. That is not analysis; it is a thumb on the scale. When in doubt, test two-sided.

Two ways to be wrong: false positives and false negatives

Because the verdict rests on a sample, it can be wrong in two ways, and the two mistakes have very different costs.

In the language of testing, a positive result is a test that rejects \(H_0\): it reports that it found something, an effect, a difference, a disease, a fraud, a benefit. A negative result is a test that does not reject \(H_0\): it reports that it found nothing.

A Type I error is a false positive: rejecting \(H_0\) when \(H_0\) is actually true. The test reports an effect that is not there. Its probability is \(\alpha\), the significance level, which we choose:

\[\alpha=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ is true}).\]

A Type II error is a false negative: failing to reject \(H_0\) when \(H_0\) is actually false. The test misses an effect that is there. Its probability is \(\beta\):

\[\beta=P(\text{do not reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ is false}).\]

The power of a test is the probability of a true positive, correctly rejecting a false \(H_0\): \(\text{power}=1-\beta\).

A memory aid: a Type I error is crying wolf (an alarm with no wolf); a Type II error is missing the wolf. “Positive” never means “good”; it means the test said yes, something is here. A positive cancer screen is bad news and a positive drug trial is good news; a false positive is a wrong “yes” either way.

Your textbook gets these labels backwards in one place. The Focus on Analytics box on the FDA’s drug approvals, at the end of Section 9.5, says “a Type I error (false negative)” and “a Type II error (false positive).” It is the other way around. With \(H_0\): the drug has no benefit,

  • approving a drug that does not work is rejecting a true \(H_0\): a Type I error, a false positive;
  • refusing a drug that does work is failing to reject a false \(H_0\): a Type II error, a false negative.

The box’s own definitions and its decision figure are correct; only the two parenthetical labels are swapped. Use the definitions above on exams.

The FDA example is still worth reading for its point about costs. A false positive (a harmful or useless drug approved) produces identifiable victims and headlines, so regulators guard hard against it with a small \(\alpha\). A false negative (a beneficial drug refused) produces victims nobody can name, people who would have been helped, so it gets less attention. A small \(\alpha\) buys protection against the visible error at the price of a larger \(\beta\), more of the invisible one. Which error is worse is a business or policy judgment, not a statistical one, and it should drive the choice of \(\alpha\).

\(\alpha\) is \(P(\text{reject}\mid H_0\text{ true})\), the false positive rate among situations where nothing is there. It is not the probability that a rejection is a false positive, \(P(H_0\text{ true}\mid\text{reject})\). That second number also depends on how often \(H_0\) is true to begin with: the base-rate problem of the screening-test example in Chapter 4. If a company tests 100 marketing ideas and only a few really work, a good share of its “significant” results will be false positives even at \(\alpha=0.05\).

In quality control, a Type I error (rejecting a good batch) is sometimes called the producer’s risk and a Type II error (accepting a bad batch) the consumer’s risk. The structure is the same.

\(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) pull against each other. For a fixed sample size, making it harder to cry wolf makes it easier to miss the wolf. The only way to reduce both at once is a larger sample. We return to this with numbers at the end of the chapter.

The p-value

The p-value is the probability, computed assuming \(H_0\) is true, of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, in the direction(s) of \(H_1\).

Decision rule: reject \(H_0\) if \(p\le\alpha\); otherwise do not reject.

A small p-value says: if \(H_0\) were true, data like ours would be rare. Either we saw something rare, or \(H_0\) is false. Below \(\alpha\), we bet on the second. This is the same reasoning we used to judge the commute claim in Chapter 7. The p-value is simply that tail probability, given a name.

What the p-value is not.

  • It is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true. It is computed by assuming \(H_0\) is true, so it cannot also measure whether it is.
  • It is not “the confidence you have that your null hypothesis is correct,” as your textbook says in Section 9.2. A p-value of 0.30 does not mean we are 30% sure of \(H_0\).
  • It is not the size or importance of an effect. A tiny, worthless effect can have a tiny p-value if the sample is big enough (see the end of this note).

Your textbook adds that a p-value “can be computed directly only for \(z\).” That was a limit of printed tables, not of the method. Excel’s T.DIST, T.DIST.RT, and T.DIST.2T give exact \(t\) p-values, and the book itself uses them in Section 9.3.

The six steps

  1. State \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) (before seeing the data).
  2. Choose \(\alpha\), by weighing the cost of a false positive against a false negative.
  3. Identify the test statistic and its distribution under \(H_0\) (and, if you use them, the critical values).
  4. Compute the test statistic from the sample.
  5. Compute the p-value and compare it with \(\alpha\) (equivalently, compare the statistic with the critical value).
  6. State the conclusion in the words of the problem, including the direction, and never “accept \(H_0\).”

For a mean with \(\sigma\) unknown, the test statistic is

\[t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}},\qquad\text{df}=n-1,\]

where \(\mu_0\) is the value claimed in \(H_0\). The numerator is not \(\bar{x}-\mu\): we do not know \(\mu\), and the whole calculation asks how far \(\bar{x}\) is from what \(H_0\) says.

Tests and confidence intervals are the same calculation

A two-tailed test at significance level \(\alpha\) rejects \(H_0:\mu=\mu_0\) exactly when \(\mu_0\) lies outside the \((1-\alpha)\) confidence interval. The confidence interval is the set of all claimed values the test would not reject.

Testing a proportion

To test \(H_0:p=p_0\) (or \(\le\), \(\ge\)), with \(\hat{p}=x/n\),

\[z=\frac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}} ,\]

valid when \(np_0\ge5\) and \(n(1-p_0)\ge5\). The p-value comes from the standard normal.

The standard error uses \(p_0\), the value claimed in \(H_0\), not \(\hat{p}\). The test asks how surprising \(\hat{p}\) would be if \(H_0\) were true, so it uses the spread that \(H_0\) implies. The confidence interval, which claims nothing, uses \(\hat{p}\).

Type II errors and power

\(\alpha\) is chosen. \(\beta\) is not: it depends on how wrong \(H_0\) actually is, on \(\alpha\), and on \(n\). To compute it we must pick a specific “true” value, because “\(H_0\) is false” covers infinitely many possibilities.

Application: the Nissan Leaf battery

Nissan’s Leaf averages 151 miles per charge. A new battery is claimed to do better. Nissan will test 50 cars, with \(\sigma=12\) miles known and \(\alpha=0.05\):

\[H_0:\mu\le151\qquad H_1:\mu>151 .\]

The critical sample mean \(\bar{x}_\alpha\) is the value of \(\bar{x}\) at the edge of the rejection region. For an upper-tailed test,

\[\bar{x}_\alpha=\mu_0+z_\alpha\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} ,\]

(with \(-\) for a lower-tailed test). If the true mean is \(\mu_1\),

\[\beta=P\!\left(\bar{x}<\bar{x}_\alpha\ \middle|\ \mu=\mu_1\right), \qquad\text{power}=1-\beta .\]

Statistically significant is not the same as important

A result is statistically significant when \(p\le\alpha\): the data are unlikely under \(H_0\). It is practically significant when the effect is large enough to matter for the decision. The first is a statistical question; the second is a business one.

p-value, z, two-tailed =2*(1 - NORM.S.DIST(ABS(z), TRUE)) both tails
z-test from raw data =Z.TEST(range, mu0, sigma) UPPER-tail p-value only; lower tail is 1 - Z.TEST

Reading the Textbook: Corrections

Donnelly is a clear guide to the mechanics, and the reading for these chapters is assigned. A few statements in it will cost you points if you copy them:

  1. False positive / false negative (Section 9.5, Focus on Analytics). The labels are reversed. A Type I error is a false positive; a Type II error is a false negative.
  2. “The p-value can also be described as the confidence you have that your null hypothesis is correct” (Section 9.2). No. It is computed assuming \(H_0\) is true and says nothing directly about the probability that \(H_0\) is true.
  3. “The sample supports the original assumption.” After a failure to reject, the data are consistent with or fail to contradict \(H_0\). They do not support it.
  4. “p-values can be computed directly only for \(z\).” Only from the printed table. Excel’s T.DIST family gives exact \(t\) p-values.
  5. Larger \(n\) helps “where the \(t\)-distribution can be approximated by the normal.” Larger \(n\) helps because the Central Limit Theorem makes \(\bar{x}\) more normal. For skewed populations \(n=30\) may not be enough.
  6. Rounding. NORM.S.INV(0.95) returns 1.645, not 1.654 as printed. The book’s rounded \(z\)-values (2.33, 1.65) move some sample sizes by one and some \(\beta\) values in the second decimal place. Match Excel, and say so.

# Part I: Confidence Intervals (Chapter 8)

## Point estimates and interval estimates

::: {.blue}
A **point estimate** is a single number computed from a sample to estimate a
population parameter: the sample mean $\bar{x}$ for $\mu$, the sample
proportion $\hat{p}$ for $p$.

An **interval estimate**, or **confidence interval**, is a range of values,
computed from the sample, constructed by a method that captures the
parameter in a stated share of samples, the **confidence level**, such as
95%.
:::

A point estimate is the best single guess, and it is almost certainly
wrong: Chapter 7 showed that $\bar{x}$ lands above or below $\mu$ by a
sampling error that changes from sample to sample. A point estimate says
nothing about how wrong it is likely to be. The interval does, and its
half-width has a name.

::: {.blue}
The **margin of error** is the distance from the point estimate to either end
of a confidence interval:

$$\text{margin of error}=(\text{critical value})\times(\text{standard error}),\qquad
\text{interval}=\text{estimate}\pm\text{margin of error}.$$
:::

## The three intervals of this week

| Parameter | Estimate | Standard error | Critical value | Excel margin of error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| mean, $\sigma$ known | $\bar{x}$ | $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ | $z_{\alpha/2}$ | `=CONFIDENCE.NORM(alpha, sigma, n)` |
| mean, $\sigma$ unknown (the usual case) | $\bar{x}$ | $s/\sqrt{n}$ | $t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}$ | `=CONFIDENCE.T(alpha, s, n)` |
| proportion | $\hat{p}=x/n$ | $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ | $z_{\alpha/2}$ | `=NORM.S.INV(1-alpha/2)*SE` |

Here $\alpha=1-\text{confidence level}$, so a 95% interval has $\alpha=0.05$,
split as 0.025 in each tail. If the sample is more than 5% of a finite
population, multiply the standard error by the finite population correction
$\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}$ exactly as in Chapter 7 (Section 8.6 of the text works
an example).

## When $\sigma$ is unknown: the Student's $t$-distribution

::: {.blue}
When the sample standard deviation $s$ replaces $\sigma$, the standardized
mean

$$t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{s/\sqrt{n}}$$

follows the **Student's $t$-distribution** with $n-1$ **degrees of freedom**
(df), provided the population is normal. The degrees of freedom count the
values free to vary once $\bar{x}$ is fixed: knowing the mean and $n-1$ of the
values pins down the last one.
:::

**Properties of the $t$-distribution**

+ Bell-shaped and symmetric around 0, with total area 1.
+ Flatter in the middle and **heavier in the tails** than the normal,
  because $s$ itself varies from sample to sample and adds uncertainty.
+ A family of curves, one for each df. As df grows, $s$ becomes a reliable
  stand-in for $\sigma$ and $t$ approaches the standard normal.
+ So the critical $t$ is always **larger** than the critical $z$ for the
  same confidence, and $t$-intervals are wider.

## What "95% confident" means

::: {.scarlet}
**A confidence interval is not a probability statement about $\mu$.** Once
the sample is drawn, the interval is fixed and so is $\mu$: the interval
either contains $\mu$ or it does not. There is nothing left to be random. It
is wrong to say "there is a 95% probability that $\mu$ is between
`{python} money(fac_ci["lower"])` and `{python} money(fac_ci["upper"])`."
:::

::: {.blue}
The **confidence level** describes the **method**, not any one interval: if
we drew sample after sample and built an interval from each in the same way,
95% of those intervals would capture $\mu$. The share that miss is $\alpha$.
"We are 95% confident" is shorthand for "this interval came from a method
that works 95% of the time."
:::

## The margin of error and its three levers

The margin of error is $(\text{critical value})\times s/\sqrt{n}$. Three
things move it, and a manager controls two of them.

1. **The confidence level.** Higher confidence means a larger critical value
   and a wider interval (the QVC table above).
2. **The variability of the data, $s$.** Noisier data, wider interval. You
   rarely control this, though a better measurement process or a stratified
   design (Chapter 7) can reduce it.
3. **The sample size, $n$.** Larger samples, narrower intervals, but through
   $\sqrt{n}$: to halve the margin you need about **four times** the data.

# Part II: Hypothesis Testing (Chapter 9)

## The logic of a test

::: {.blue}
A **hypothesis** is a claim about a population parameter, such as a mean or
a proportion.

The **null hypothesis**, $H_0$, is the status quo or the claim being
tested. It always contains the equality: $=$, $\le$, or $\ge$ a specific
value.

The **alternative hypothesis**, $H_1$, is what we would conclude if the data
are strong enough evidence against $H_0$. It never contains the equality:
$\ne$, $<$, or $>$.
:::

A hypothesis test is a trial. $H_0$ is the defendant, presumed innocent. The
data are the evidence. The verdict is **reject $H_0$** (guilty: the evidence
is strong enough) or **do not reject $H_0$** (not guilty: it is not). A jury
never declares a defendant *innocent*, only *not guilty*, because a lack of
evidence is not proof of innocence.

::: {.scarlet}
**We never "accept" $H_0$.** Failing to reject it means the data are
**consistent with** $H_0$, not that they show it is true. The same data are
usually consistent with many other values too. Your textbook occasionally
says a sample "supports the original assumption" after a failure to reject.
Read that as "fails to contradict."
:::

## Stating the hypotheses

Three claims, three setups.

::: {.charcoal}
**Time on a web page.** A study reported in the *Wall Street Journal*
([Does the Internet Make You Smarter?](https://crooker.faculty.unlv.edu/mba775/articles/Does_the_Internet_Make_You_Smarter_-WSJ-2010-06-03.pdf)
and
[Does the Internet Make You Dumber?](https://crooker.faculty.unlv.edu/mba775/articles/Does_the_Internet_Make_You_Dumber_-WSJ-2010-06-04.pdf))
says the average American looks at a web page for 56 seconds. A student
wants to know whether UNLV students are *different*, in either direction:

$$H_0:\mu=56\qquad H_1:\mu\ne56\qquad\text{(two-tailed)}$$

**Light bulb life.** A lab wants evidence that a new bulb *lasts longer*
than the industry's 8,000 hours. The claim needing evidence goes in $H_1$:

$$H_0:\mu\le8{,}000\qquad H_1:\mu>8{,}000\qquad\text{(one-tailed, upper)}$$

**An 18-game NFL season.** The commissioner says a majority of fans favor
it. The players' union, which opposes it, wants evidence that the
commissioner is wrong:

$$H_0:p\ge0.5\qquad H_1:p<0.5\qquad\text{(one-tailed, lower)}$$
:::

::: {.blue}
A **two-tailed test** has $H_1:\ne$ and looks for a difference in either
direction. A **one-tailed test** has $H_1:<$ or $H_1:>$ and looks in one
direction only.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
**Choose the hypotheses and the tail before you look at the data.** $H_1$ is
the claim that *requires evidence*, not the answer you are hoping for. A
one-tailed p-value is half the two-tailed one, so choosing the tail after
seeing which way the data point turns a two-tailed p of 0.08 into a
one-tailed 0.04 and an "insignificant" result into a "significant" one. That
is not analysis; it is a thumb on the scale. When in doubt, test two-sided.
:::

## Two ways to be wrong: false positives and false negatives

Because the verdict rests on a sample, it can be wrong in two ways, and the
two mistakes have very different costs.

In the language of testing, a **positive** result is a test that **rejects
$H_0$**: it reports that it found something, an effect, a difference, a
disease, a fraud, a benefit. A **negative** result is a test that does
**not** reject $H_0$: it reports that it found nothing.

::: {.blue}
A **Type I error** is a **false positive**: rejecting $H_0$ when $H_0$ is
actually true. The test reports an effect that is not there. Its probability
is $\alpha$, the **significance level**, which we choose:

$$\alpha=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ is true}).$$

A **Type II error** is a **false negative**: failing to reject $H_0$ when
$H_0$ is actually false. The test misses an effect that is there. Its
probability is $\beta$:

$$\beta=P(\text{do not reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ is false}).$$

The **power** of a test is the probability of a **true positive**,
correctly rejecting a false $H_0$: $\text{power}=1-\beta$.
:::

```{python}
#| label: error-table
error_table()
```

| | $H_0$ actually true (nothing there) | $H_0$ actually false (something there) |
|---|---|---|
| **Courtroom** ($H_0$: innocent) | convict an innocent person: **false positive, Type I** | acquit a guilty person: **false negative, Type II** |
| **Smoke alarm** ($H_0$: no fire) | alarm sounds, no fire: **false positive, Type I** | fire, no alarm: **false negative, Type II** |
| **Spam filter** ($H_0$: real mail) | real mail sent to spam: **false positive, Type I** | spam reaches the inbox: **false negative, Type II** |
| **Audit** ($H_0$: no fraud) | honest account flagged: **false positive, Type I** | fraud goes undetected: **false negative, Type II** |

::: {.charcoal}
A memory aid: a Type I error is **crying wolf** (an alarm with no wolf); a
Type II error is **missing the wolf**. "Positive" never means "good"; it
means the test said *yes, something is here*. A positive cancer screen is
bad news and a positive drug trial is good news; a *false* positive is a
wrong "yes" either way.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
**Your textbook gets these labels backwards in one place.** The Focus on
Analytics box on the FDA's drug approvals, at the end of Section 9.5, says
"a Type I error (false negative)" and "a Type II error (false positive)." It
is the other way around. With $H_0$: *the drug has no benefit*,

+ approving a drug that does not work is rejecting a true $H_0$: a **Type I
  error, a false positive**;
+ refusing a drug that does work is failing to reject a false $H_0$: a
  **Type II error, a false negative**.

The box's own definitions and its decision figure are correct; only the two
parenthetical labels are swapped. Use the definitions above on exams.
:::

::: {.charcoal}
The FDA example is still worth reading for its point about costs. A false
positive (a harmful or useless drug approved) produces identifiable victims
and headlines, so regulators guard hard against it with a small $\alpha$. A
false negative (a beneficial drug refused) produces victims nobody can
name, people who would have been helped, so it gets less attention. A
small $\alpha$ buys protection against the visible error at the price of a
larger $\beta$, more of the invisible one. Which error is worse is a
business or policy judgment, not a statistical one, and it should drive the
choice of $\alpha$.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
$\alpha$ is $P(\text{reject}\mid H_0\text{ true})$, the false positive rate
*among situations where nothing is there*. It is **not** the probability that
a rejection is a false positive, $P(H_0\text{ true}\mid\text{reject})$. That
second number also depends on how often $H_0$ is true to begin with: the
base-rate problem of the screening-test example in Chapter 4. If a company
tests 100 marketing ideas and only a few really work, a good share of its
"significant" results will be false positives even at $\alpha=0.05$.
:::

In quality control, a Type I error (rejecting a good batch) is sometimes
called the **producer's risk** and a Type II error (accepting a bad batch)
the **consumer's risk**. The structure is the same.

$\alpha$ and $\beta$ pull against each other. For a fixed sample size,
making it harder to cry wolf makes it easier to miss the wolf. The only way
to reduce both at once is a larger sample. We return to this with numbers
at the end of the chapter.

## The p-value

::: {.blue}
The **p-value** is the probability, **computed assuming $H_0$ is true**, of
getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, in the
direction(s) of $H_1$.

**Decision rule:** reject $H_0$ if $p\le\alpha$; otherwise do not reject.
:::

A small p-value says: *if* $H_0$ were true, data like ours would be rare.
Either we saw something rare, or $H_0$ is false. Below $\alpha$, we bet on
the second. This is the same reasoning we used to judge the commute claim in
Chapter 7. The p-value is simply that tail probability, given a name.

::: {.scarlet}
**What the p-value is not.**

+ It is **not** the probability that $H_0$ is true. It is computed by
  *assuming* $H_0$ is true, so it cannot also measure whether it is.
+ It is **not** "the confidence you have that your null hypothesis is
  correct," as your textbook says in Section 9.2. A p-value of 0.30 does not
  mean we are 30% sure of $H_0$.
+ It is **not** the size or importance of an effect. A tiny, worthless effect
  can have a tiny p-value if the sample is big enough (see the end of this
  note).
:::

Your textbook adds that a p-value "can be computed directly only for $z$."
That was a limit of printed tables, not of the method. Excel's `T.DIST`,
`T.DIST.RT`, and `T.DIST.2T` give exact $t$ p-values, and the book itself uses
them in Section 9.3.

## The six steps

1. State $H_0$ and $H_1$ (before seeing the data).
2. Choose $\alpha$, by weighing the cost of a false positive against a false
   negative.
3. Identify the test statistic and its distribution under $H_0$ (and, if you
   use them, the critical values).
4. Compute the test statistic from the sample.
5. Compute the p-value and compare it with $\alpha$ (equivalently, compare
   the statistic with the critical value).
6. State the conclusion **in the words of the problem**, including the
   direction, and never "accept $H_0$."

For a mean with $\sigma$ unknown, the test statistic is

$$t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}},\qquad\text{df}=n-1,$$

where $\mu_0$ is the value **claimed in $H_0$**. The numerator is not
$\bar{x}-\mu$: we do not know $\mu$, and the whole calculation asks how far
$\bar{x}$ is from what $H_0$ says.

## Tests and confidence intervals are the same calculation

::: {.blue}
A two-tailed test at significance level $\alpha$ rejects $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$
**exactly when** $\mu_0$ lies outside the $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval.
The confidence interval is the set of all claimed values the test would not
reject.
:::

```{python}
#| label: duality
nielsen_ci = mean_interval(39.6, 16.4, 10, 0.98)
print(f"98% interval: {nielsen_ci['lower']:.2f} to {nielsen_ci['upper']:.2f}\n")
interval_test_agreement(39.6, 16.4, 10, [20, 25, 34.5, 50, 55], alpha=0.02).round(4)
```

::: {.charcoal}
The 98% interval runs from `{python} f"{nielsen_ci['lower']:.1f}"` to
`{python} f"{nielsen_ci['upper']:.1f}"` hours. Any claim in that range
survives a two-tailed test at $\alpha=0.02$; any claim outside it is
rejected. Notice how wide that is: the same ten people "fail to reject"
25 hours and 50 hours. That is why failing to reject 34.5 says so little.

This is the most useful fact in these two chapters. **The interval tells you
everything the two-tailed test does, and more**: not only whether the claim
survives, but which other values would. When you report a test, report the
interval too. (Your textbook does this with an iPhone price claim of \$811:
the 95% interval $797.8\pm1.96(5.41)$ excludes 811, so the claim is
rejected.)
:::

::: {.scarlet}
The match is exact only for a **two-tailed** test with
$\text{confidence}=1-\alpha$. A one-tailed test at $\alpha=0.05$ matches a
one-sided 95% bound, or equivalently a two-sided **90%** interval.
:::

## Testing a proportion

::: {.blue}
To test $H_0:p=p_0$ (or $\le$, $\ge$), with $\hat{p}=x/n$,

$$z=\frac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}} ,$$

valid when $np_0\ge5$ and $n(1-p_0)\ge5$. The p-value comes from the
standard normal.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
**The standard error uses $p_0$, the value claimed in $H_0$, not $\hat{p}$.**
The test asks how surprising $\hat{p}$ would be *if $H_0$ were true*, so it
uses the spread that $H_0$ implies. The confidence interval, which claims
nothing, uses $\hat{p}$.
:::


**(a)** $H_0:p\ge0.5$ (the commissioner's claim), $H_1:p<0.5$ (what the
union wants evidence for). **(b)** $\hat{p}=$
`{python} f"{nfl['p-hat']:.3f}"`, $z=$
`{python} f"{nfl['test statistic z']:.2f}"`, p-value
`{python} f"{nfl['p-value']:.4f}"`.
`{python} ("Reject H0: the poll is evidence that fewer than half of fans favor the change." if nfl["reject H0?"] else "Do not reject H0: although fewer than half of this sample favor the change, the poll is not strong enough evidence that fewer than half of ALL fans do.")`
**(c)** `{python} f"{nfl_ci['lower']:.3f}"` to
`{python} f"{nfl_ci['upper']:.3f}"`. The test used $p_0=0.5$ in its
standard error; the interval used $\hat{p}=$ `{python} f"{nfl['p-hat']:.3f}"`.
:::

## When $\sigma$ is known: the $z$-test

When the population standard deviation is known, the test statistic is
$z=(\bar{x}-\mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$ and the p-value comes from the standard
normal. Everything else is identical. Your textbook works several examples
this way in Section 9.2; read them for the steps, and use the $t$-test in
practice.

```{python}
#| label: cfl
cfl = z_test(8_120, 500, 36, 8_000, "greater", 0.05)
numbers(cfl)
```

::: {.charcoal}
The textbook's Edalight example: 36 new compact fluorescent bulbs average
8,120 hours, and $\sigma=500$ hours is taken as known. Testing
$H_0:\mu\le8{,}000$ against $H_1:\mu>8{,}000$ gives $z=$
`{python} f"{cfl['test statistic z']:.2f}"` and p-value
`{python} f"{cfl['p-value']:.4f}"`, which is above $\alpha=0.05$: not
enough evidence that the new bulb lasts longer than 8,000 hours.
:::

::: {.scarlet}
Excel's `Z.TEST(range, mu0, sigma)` returns the **upper-tail** p-value only.
For a lower-tailed test use `1 - Z.TEST(...)`; for a two-tailed test,
`2 * MIN(Z.TEST(...), 1 - Z.TEST(...))`. (The textbook's description of its
arguments calls $\sigma$ "the population mean"; it is the population
standard deviation.)
:::

## Type II errors and power

$\alpha$ is chosen. $\beta$ is not: it depends on how wrong $H_0$ actually
is, on $\alpha$, and on $n$. To compute it we must pick a specific "true"
value, because "$H_0$ is false" covers infinitely many possibilities.

### Application: the Nissan Leaf battery

Nissan's Leaf averages 151 miles per charge. A new battery is claimed to do
better. Nissan will test 50 cars, with $\sigma=12$ miles known and
$\alpha=0.05$:

$$H_0:\mu\le151\qquad H_1:\mu>151 .$$

::: {.blue}
The **critical sample mean** $\bar{x}_\alpha$ is the value of $\bar{x}$ at the
edge of the rejection region. For an upper-tailed test,

$$\bar{x}_\alpha=\mu_0+z_\alpha\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} ,$$

(with $-$ for a lower-tailed test). If the true mean is $\mu_1$,

$$\beta=P\!\left(\bar{x}<\bar{x}_\alpha\ \middle|\ \mu=\mu_1\right),
\qquad\text{power}=1-\beta .$$
:::

```{python}
#| label: leaf
leaf = power_mean(151, 12, 50, 156, 0.05, "greater")
numbers(leaf)
```

```{python}
#| label: leaf-chart
leaf_cut = critical_mean(151, 12, 50, 0.05, "greater")
_ = power_chart(151, 156, 12 / sqrt(50), leaf_cut, "greater",
                title="Nissan Leaf: alpha, beta, and power on one picture",
                xlab="Average range of 50 cars (miles), x-bar")
```

::: {.charcoal}
Nissan rejects $H_0$ if the 50 cars average more than
`{python} f"{leaf_cut:.2f}"` miles. If $H_0$ is true (top panel), that
happens 5% of the time: the false positive rate. Now suppose the new
battery really averages 156 miles (bottom panel). The test misses it
whenever the sample mean falls short of `{python} f"{leaf_cut:.2f}"`, which
happens with probability $\beta=$
`{python} f"{leaf['beta = P(Type II error)']:.4f}"`. The power, the chance
of correctly detecting the better battery, is
`{python} f"{leaf['power = 1 - beta']:.4f}"`.

(The textbook gets 0.0968 and 0.9032 by rounding $z$ to $-1.30$. Its
version of the calculation is correct.)
:::

We can check $\beta$ the same way we checked the confidence level: test
many fresh samples from a world where the mean really is 156, and count.

```{python}
#| label: leaf-sim
leaf_sim = simulate_rejections(lambda rng, shape: rng.normal(156, 12, shape),
                               50, 151, "greater", 0.05, sigma=12)
numbers(leaf_sim)
```

::: {.charcoal}
The simulated studies fail to reject `{python} pct(leaf_sim["failed to reject (rate)"], 2)`
of the time, against the formula's
`{python} pct(leaf["beta = P(Type II error)"], 2)`. The formula is not
magic; it is a shortcut to what would happen if we ran the study over and
over.
:::

### Power depends on how big the real effect is

```{python}
#| label: leaf-curve
leaf_pt = power_table(151, 12, 50, np.arange(151, 160.5, 0.5))
_ = line_chart(leaf_pt.index, {"power": leaf_pt["power"]},
               title="Power curve: the chance of detecting the better battery",
               xlab="True average range (miles)", ylab="Power = 1 - beta",
               hlines=[0.05, 0.80], ylim=(0, 1.02))
leaf_pt.loc[[151, 152, 153, 154, 155, 156, 157, 158]].round(4)
```

::: {.charcoal}
If the true mean is barely above 151, the test almost never notices: power
starts at $\alpha=0.05$ (at exactly 151, rejecting *is* a Type I error) and
is only `{python} pct(leaf_pt.loc[152, "power"], 0)` at 152 miles. Power
passes 80% somewhere between 155 and 156. **A test can see large effects
easily and small ones hardly at all.** Before trusting a "no significant
difference," ask how large a difference the study could have detected.

(The textbook's Table 9.10 shows 0.0495 at 151, which should be exactly
0.05; it comes from rounding $z_\alpha$ to 1.65.)
:::

### Power depends on $\alpha$

```{python}
#| label: leaf-alpha
ab = alpha_beta_table(151, 12, 50, 157, alphas=(0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.025,
                                                0.05, 0.10, 0.20))
ab_curve = alpha_beta_table(151, 12, 50, 157, alphas=np.linspace(0.001, 0.20, 60))
_ = line_chart(ab_curve.index, {"beta (false negative)": ab_curve["beta"]},
               title="Lowering alpha raises beta (n = 50, true mean 157)",
               xlab="alpha (false positive rate)", ylab="beta",
               vlines=[0.01, 0.05, 0.10], markers=False)
ab.round(4)
```

::: {.charcoal}
At a true mean of 157 miles, cutting $\alpha$ from 0.05 to 0.01 raises
$\beta$ from `{python} f"{ab.loc[0.05, 'beta']:.4f}"` to
`{python} f"{ab.loc[0.01, 'beta']:.4f}"`. Fewer false positives, more
false negatives: there is no free lunch.

The textbook reports $\beta=0.1190$ for this case. It rounds $z_{0.01}$ to
2.33 and then rounds the critical mean to 155 miles before computing
$\beta$; unrounded, the critical mean is
`{python} f"{critical_mean(151, 12, 50, 0.01):.2f}"` and
$\beta=$ `{python} f"{ab.loc[0.01, 'beta']:.4f}"`. The lesson is the same,
but if you work in Excel you will match this note, not the book.
:::

### Power depends on the sample size, which you control

The textbook says the only way to reduce both errors at once is a larger
sample. Here is how much larger. Suppose the improvement worth detecting is
3 miles (true mean 154):

```{python}
#| label: leaf-n
leaf_n = power_by_n(151, 12, [10, 25, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200], 154)
need = n_for_power(151, 12, 154, power=0.80)
leaf_n.round(4)
```

::: {.charcoal}
With 50 cars, Nissan would detect a 3-mile improvement only
`{python} pct(leaf_n.loc[50, "power"], 0)` of the time, close to a coin
flip. To reach the conventional 80% power it needs
`{python} f"{need['n (round UP)']:.0f}"` cars, from

$$n=\left(\frac{(z_\alpha+z_\beta)\,\sigma}{\mu_1-\mu_0}\right)^2
=\left(\frac{(1.645+0.842)\times12}{3}\right)^2 .$$

This is the calculation to do **before** a study, alongside the
margin-of-error calculation of Chapter 8. A study too small to detect the
effect that matters is a study designed to say "no evidence," and it will
usually oblige.
:::

## Statistically significant is not the same as important

::: {.blue}
A result is **statistically significant** when $p\le\alpha$: the data are
unlikely under $H_0$. It is **practically significant** when the effect is
large enough to matter for the decision. The first is a statistical
question; the second is a business one.
:::

```{python}
#| label: practical
big_n = t_test(4.95, 1.42, 20_000, 5, "less", 0.05)
big_ci = mean_interval(4.95, 1.42, 20_000, 0.95)
pd.Series({"x-bar (minutes)": f"{4.95:.2f}", "n": f"{20_000:,}",
           "p-value": f"{big_n['p-value']:.7f}",
           "95% interval: lower": f"{big_ci['lower']:.3f}",
           "95% interval: upper": f"{big_ci['upper']:.3f}"}).to_frame("Target, all stores")
```

::: {.charcoal}
Suppose Target rolls the kiosks out and logs 20,000 checkouts averaging 4.95
minutes. The p-value is about
`{python} f"{big_n['p-value']:.7f}"`, overwhelming evidence that kiosks are
faster than 5 minutes. By about **three seconds**. That is statistically
significant and commercially irrelevant. The confidence interval shows it
at once, which is another reason to report one.

The opposite also happens: a small study of an important effect, like the
41-owner Buick survey, can easily come back "not significant." **Large
samples find trivial effects; small samples miss important ones.** Always
ask *how big* as well as *whether*.
:::

# Reading the Textbook: Corrections

Donnelly is a clear guide to the mechanics, and the reading for these
chapters is assigned. A few statements in it will cost you points if you
copy them:

::: {.scarlet}
1. **False positive / false negative (Section 9.5, Focus on Analytics).**
   The labels are reversed. A Type I error is a **false positive**; a Type
   II error is a **false negative**.
2. **"The p-value can also be described as the confidence you have that
   your null hypothesis is correct" (Section 9.2).** No. It is computed
   assuming $H_0$ is true and says nothing directly about the probability
   that $H_0$ is true.
3. **"The sample supports the original assumption."** After a failure to
   reject, the data are *consistent with* or *fail to contradict* $H_0$.
   They do not support it.
4. **"p-values can be computed directly only for $z$."** Only from the
   printed table. Excel's `T.DIST` family gives exact $t$ p-values.
5. **Larger $n$ helps "where the $t$-distribution can be approximated by the
   normal."** Larger $n$ helps because the Central Limit Theorem makes
   $\bar{x}$ more normal. For skewed populations $n=30$ may not be enough.
6. **Rounding.** `NORM.S.INV(0.95)` returns 1.645, not 1.654 as printed.
   The book's rounded $z$-values (2.33, 1.65) move some sample sizes by one
   and some $\beta$ values in the second decimal place. Match Excel, and say
   so.
:::
